# Part V: The Fringe


## Chapter 28: The Newport Tower Date – Pre-Columbian Contact: Vikings, Templars, and the Evidence
In the autumn of 1948, a team from the **Peabody Museum** at Harvard began digging beneath a peculiar stone tower in **Touro Park**, in the center of **Newport, Rhode Island**. The tower had stood there for as long as anyone in the town could remember, which was the problem. It had stood there for longer than anyone could remember, and this had given it a second life as evidence for virtually every theory of pre-Columbian contact that the human imagination could generate. The **Vikings** built it. The **Knights Templar** built it. The **Portuguese** built it. The Chinese built it. A lost colony of Irish monks built it. The structure itself, a rough cylinder of mortared stone resting on eight columns that formed an open arcade at ground level, looked plausible enough as a medieval relic. It had the proportions and the Romanesque arches of a twelfth-century baptistery. The tower was twenty-eight feet in diameter and twenty-six feet tall, built of local stone with a double-fireplace interior that suggested something domestic rather than ecclesiastical. It was precisely the sort of thing a person raised on Walter Scott novels would want to find on American soil, and a steady stream of such persons had been finding it there since the early nineteenth century.

The Peabody team, led by archaeologist **William Godfrey**, dug a trench alongside the tower's foundations and found exactly what a less romantic mind might have predicted: colonial pottery, a gunflint, a seventeenth-century clay pipe stem, and a square-heeled boot print pressed into the clay of the foundation trench. The mortar contained shell fragments consistent with the local lime-burning practices of colonial New England. A radiocarbon date on the mortar placed it at approximately **1680 CE**. This is **the Newport Tower date**: not medieval, not Norse, not pre-Columbian, but colonial New England. The colonial governor **Benedict Arnold** (the first governor of Rhode Island, not the later traitor of the same name) had referred to "my stone built Wind Mill" in his **1677 will**, and the probate inventory listed the structure among his property. A seventeenth-century map of Newport, drawn while Arnold was still alive, showed the tower in its present location and labeled it as a mill. The tower, in short, was a windmill. It had been built by English colonists in the second half of the seventeenth century, and the accumulated evidence from excavation, documentary record, materials analysis, and cartography all pointed to the same conclusion.

This should have been the end of the matter. It was not.

Seventy-five years after Godfrey's excavation, the **Newport Tower** remains one of the most frequently cited artifacts in the pre-Columbian contact literature. The finding of colonial materials in the foundation trench has been reinterpreted, ignored, or explained away by successive generations of enthusiasts. A 2008 re-analysis of the mortar confirmed the 1680 date. A 2009 archaeological survey found additional colonial artifacts in the surrounding stratigraphy. The Danish archaeologist **Johannes Brondsted**, who examined the tower in the 1950s at the invitation of Newport's Norse-enthusiast community, concluded after careful study that it was a colonial windmill of a type common in seventeenth-century England, noting that its eight-column arcade was architecturally identical to the **Chesterton Windmill** in Warwickshire, built in 1632. The idea that the tower predates European colonization persists not because the evidence supports it but because the idea is more interesting than the evidence. This is a recurring pattern in the pre-Columbian contact debate, and it reveals something important about how fringe claims sustain themselves: the evidence against them becomes, paradoxically, evidence for a cover-up, which makes the original claim more compelling, not less.

The actual history of pre-Columbian contact between the Old World and the Americas is, in its way, more surprising than the myths. It contains exactly two confirmed cases. Both are supported by hard, convergent evidence from multiple independent lines of inquiry. And both were resisted by the archaeological establishment for decades before the evidence became undeniable.


### The Norsemen at the Tip

In 1960, a Norwegian adventurer named **Helge Ingstad** and his wife, the archaeologist **Anne Stine Ingstad**, arrived at a remote fishing village on the northern tip of **Newfoundland** called **L'Anse aux Meadows**. Helge had spent years poring over the **Vinland Sagas**, two medieval Icelandic narratives, the ***Saga of the Greenlanders*** and the ***Saga of Erik the Red***, that described Norse voyages westward from Greenland to a land of wild grapes and self-sown wheat around **1000 CE**. He had worked his way down the eastern coast of North America, interviewing locals at every coastal settlement, asking a simple question: had anyone seen old ruins? At L'Anse aux Meadows, a fisherman named **George Decker** pointed him toward a cluster of overgrown ridges near the shore, on a marine terrace overlooking **Epaves Bay**. Decker had always assumed they were remnants of an old Native settlement. The Ingstads thought otherwise.

What they found over the next eight years of excavation was unambiguous. Eight structures built in the Norse **turf-wall** style, including three large halls, a forge with iron slag and a stone anvil, a carpentry workshop with wood shavings, a bronze cloak pin of unmistakably Norse design, and butternuts that did not grow in Newfoundland but did grow further south, indicating the inhabitants had explored well beyond their base camp. The site was laid out on a pattern consistent with Norse farmsteads in Iceland and Greenland: a large longhouse for the chieftain, smaller buildings for retainers, and workshops set apart from living quarters. The iron slag was critical. The indigenous peoples of Newfoundland at this period, the **Beothuk** and earlier **Dorset** cultures, did not smelt iron. Whoever built this settlement had brought iron-working technology from the Old World.

Radiocarbon dating placed the occupation at approximately 1000 CE, consistent with the saga accounts. But the precision of the dating took a dramatic leap forward in 2021, when a study published in *Nature* achieved something remarkable. By identifying a distinctive radiocarbon signal produced by a known **solar storm** in **993 CE**, a spike in cosmic-ray production that had been independently identified in tree rings from Switzerland, Germany, Japan, and New Zealand, researchers dated the cutting of the wood used in the Norse structures to exactly **1021 CE**. Three pieces of wood, from three different trees, each of a different species (fir, juniper, and an unidentified third), all felled in the same year, all bearing the telltale carbon-14 signature of the 993 event precisely 28 growth rings from their outer edge. The trees had been cut with metal blades, consistent with iron tools rather than stone, and the wood showed marks of carpentry rather than natural breakage. Not "around 1000." Not "between 950 and 1050." The year 1021, with a margin of error of exactly one year.

L'Anse aux Meadows is now a **UNESCO World Heritage Site**. The Norse presence in North America, once dismissed as saga-fantasy by the same archaeological establishment that would later resist every other pre-Columbian contact claim, is settled fact. The settlement was small, short-lived (perhaps three to ten years of seasonal occupation), and appears to have served as a base for expeditions further south. It changed nothing about the indigenous populations of the Americas. It left no lasting cultural imprint. The Norse came, stayed briefly, and left. And it was real.

The second confirmed contact sits at the opposite end of the Pacific.


### The Sweet Potato Problem

In 2020, a study published in *Nature* confirmed what botanists and geneticists had suspected for decades: the **sweet potato**, a plant native to South America, was present in **Polynesia** before any European contact. The genetic evidence was unambiguous. The Polynesian sweet potato, known as **kumara** in Maori, **umala** in Samoan, **uala** in Hawaiian, and cognate words across dozens of Pacific Island languages, shared a common ancestor with South American varieties, and the divergence predated European arrival by centuries. The word itself was the first clue: **kumar** is the **Quechua** word for sweet potato in the Andes. The linguistic parallel had been noted as early as the 1930s, but without genetic data to support it, it had remained a curiosity.

A separate genetic study of human remains from Easter Island identified both Polynesian and South American DNA signatures in pre-contact populations, placing the admixture event at approximately **1200 CE**. The finding was published by a team led by **Alexander Ioannidis** at Stanford, who used genome-wide data from modern Polynesian populations to trace the South American genetic signal back through the genealogical record. The contact was real, it was pre-Columbian, and it involved actual human beings crossing the Pacific Ocean and having children together.

The implications are straightforward. Sometime around the twelfth or thirteenth century, Polynesian voyagers and South American coastal populations made contact. Whether Polynesian sailors reached South America, South Americans reached Polynesia, or both, the sweet potato crossed the Pacific in human hands. This was not a drift voyage. The prevailing currents and winds between South America and Polynesia run in the wrong direction for accidental transport of a living tuber. Sweet potatoes do not survive prolonged immersion in seawater. Someone carried them deliberately, which means someone made the crossing deliberately, which means someone had the navigational knowledge to plan, execute, and survive a trans-Pacific voyage roughly eight centuries ago.

The Polynesian capacity for open-ocean navigation is not in dispute. The settlement of Hawaii, **New Zealand**, Rapa Nui (Easter Island), and dozens of smaller islands required sustained, intentional voyaging across thousands of miles of open water, guided by star navigation, wave patterns, swell direction, cloud formations, and the behavior of land-finding birds, a body of traditional knowledge that European sailors did not match until the invention of the chronometer in the eighteenth century. The **Polynesian Voyaging Society**'s vessel **Hokule'a** has repeatedly demonstrated that traditional navigation techniques are sufficient for trans-Pacific voyaging. The settlement of Australia, now dated to roughly 65,000 years ago, required ocean crossings even earlier, at a time when no other human population is known to have engaged in maritime travel.

These two contacts, Norse in Newfoundland and Polynesian-South American in the Pacific, share a feature that separates them from every other pre-Columbian contact claim: they are supported by convergent evidence from archaeology, genetics, and (in the Norse case) documentary records. The evidence is replicable, independently verifiable, and published in peer-reviewed journals. Everything else in the pre-Columbian contact literature lacks at least one of these qualities, and most of it lacks all three.


### The Inscription Problem

The gap between the two confirmed contacts and the remaining claims is not a matter of degree. It is a difference in kind.

The alternative pre-Columbian contact literature rests overwhelmingly on inscriptions: carved or scratched marks on stones, found in the Americas, that are said to represent writing systems from the Old World. The most frequently cited are the **Paraiba Stone** inscription from Brazil, alleged to be Phoenician; the **Bat Creek** inscription from Tennessee, alleged to be Hebrew; the **Los Lunas Decalogue Stone** from New Mexico, alleged to bear the Ten Commandments in paleo-Hebrew; the **Kensington Runestone** from Minnesota, alleged to be a fourteenth-century Norse record; and the **Westford Knight** carving from Massachusetts, alleged to depict a medieval Scottish knight with a broken sword, the heraldic symbol of a fallen Templar.

Every one of these inscriptions has been independently evaluated by specialists in the relevant writing systems, and every one has been found to be either a modern forgery or a misidentification.

The Paraiba Stone was the subject of a definitive analysis by **Frank Moore Cross**, one of the twentieth century's foremost experts in **Semitic epigraphy** and the scholar who authenticated the Dead Sea Scrolls. Cross, who could hardly be accused of insufficient expertise, concluded unequivocally that the inscription was a fabrication, noting linguistic errors, grammatical constructions, and letter forms inconsistent with authentic Phoenician text of any period. The stone itself has never been located; only a transcription exists, sent anonymously to the **Brazilian Historical and Geographical Institute** in 1872. No original artifact. No provenance. No independent verification. Just a letter from someone who claimed to have found something.

The Bat Creek inscription, found during a **Smithsonian Institution** excavation in 1889, was initially identified as Cherokee by the excavation's director. In the 1960s, **Cyrus Gordon**, a respected Semitist at Brandeis University, reidentified it as paleo-Hebrew reading "for the land of Judah," which generated considerable excitement and a flurry of publications arguing for ancient Hebrew presence in Tennessee. Subsequent analysis revealed that the "Hebrew" characters closely matched a specific illustration in **Charles Macoy**'s 1868 ***General History, Cyclopedia, and Dictionary of Freemasonry***, a widely available Masonic reference book that would have been accessible to anyone in the region in the late nineteenth century. The lead excavator, **John Emmert**, was later documented to have fabricated artifacts at other sites in the same survey. The current consensus among epigraphers is that the inscription is a nineteenth-century hoax, likely produced by Emmert himself or someone in his excavation crew.

The Kensington Runestone, found in 1898 by a Swedish farmer named **Olof Ohman** in **Douglas County, Minnesota**, purports to be a record left by a party of eight Goths and twenty-two Norwegians on an exploration in 1362. The inscription describes finding ten men "red with blood and dead" upon returning to their ships. It is a vivid and dramatic text, and it uses runic forms and language features unknown in the fourteenth century but common in nineteenth-century Scandinavian-American communities, which is to say, it reads like what a Swedish-American farmer in 1898 would imagine a medieval Norse inscription should look like. **Scott Wolter**, a forensic geologist and host of the television series ***America Unearthed***, argued that microscopic weathering patterns on the carved runes indicated genuine antiquity. Mainstream **runologists**, including specialists at the universities of **Uppsala** and **Copenhagen** and the **Swedish National Heritage Board**, have consistently classified the inscription as a modern production, noting vocabulary, grammar, and rune forms that postdate the claimed period by centuries.

The pattern across all of these inscriptions is identical. A stone is found. It appears to bear Old World writing. A non-specialist, or a specialist in a different field, identifies the script. Specialists in that specific script examine it and find it inconsistent with authentic examples. The non-specialist's identification persists in the popular literature regardless. The appeal of the inscription is not in its content but in its implication: that the accepted history of who reached the Americas and when is wrong.


### The Templar Detour

The Knights Templar occupy a special position in the pre-Columbian contact mythology, and their role illustrates how a historical reality can be inflated into an unfalsifiable legend.

The historical Templars were real. The **Order of the Temple** was founded around 1119 in Jerusalem, ostensibly to protect Christian pilgrims traveling to the Holy Land. Over two centuries the order accumulated enormous wealth, its own banking system, a fleet of ships, extensive land holdings across Europe, and political influence that rivaled that of monarchs. The order was dissolved by **Pope Clement V** in 1312 under pressure from **King Philip IV of France**, who owed the Templars substantial debts and coveted their assets. The order's last Grand Master, **Jacques de Molay**, was burned at the stake in Paris in 1314 after retracting a confession extracted under torture. The dissolution was violent, politically motivated, and thoroughly documented in papal and royal archives. The Templar fleet, reportedly based at **La Rochelle** on the Atlantic coast of France, disappeared from the historical record after the dissolution. Several Templars are known to have fled to Scotland, where King **Robert the Bruce** had been excommunicated and was thus outside papal jurisdiction. Others went to **Portugal**, where the order was reconstituted under a new name, the **Order of Christ**, which later sponsored the voyages of **Henry the Navigator** and **Vasco da Gama**.

These facts are not in dispute. What happens next in the alternative narrative is.

**Timothy Hogan**, who identifies himself as Grandmaster of the **Order of the Temple of Secret Initiates** and traces his lineage through **General Joseph Warren** of the American Revolution (Warren died at the Battle of **Bunker Hill** in 1775 and was, according to Hogan, a Templar Grand Master), presented at the Cosmic Summit conference a sweeping account in which the Templars inherited pre-diluvian knowledge through **Gnostic** families of southern France, the **Albigensian Cathars**, who referred to themselves as "the poor of Christ." In Hogan's telling, the Templars adopted the Cathars' red equal-armed cross, sailed to the Americas before Columbus, and embedded esoteric symbolism in the architecture of the United States. The **Statue of Liberty** is really the goddess Isis, holding a tablet in the shape of a Templar keystone, wearing a seven-pointed crown representing the seven chakras. The Parthenon replica in Nashville, Tennessee, houses the largest indoor statue in the world, a forty-two-foot rendering of **Athena**, who was, according to Plato, the goddess of Atlantis. The Newport Tower is a Templar church. The symbols are everywhere if you know how to read them, and the tradition that connects them stretches back to Atlantis itself.

Hancock ([Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)) explored a related but distinct thread in ***The Sign and the Seal***, traveling to Ethiopia to investigate the claim that the Ark of the Covenant had been transported from Jerusalem to a church in **Axum** by the son of King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. In ***The Master Game***, co-authored with Robert Bauval ([Chapter 27](27-the-inverted-map.md)), Hancock and Bauval extended the argument to Gnostic and Cathar traditions in southern France, tracing what they called an underground stream of esoteric knowledge from ancient Egypt through the Templars to the founding of the American republic.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-28)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-28)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-28)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-28)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-28)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-28)</sup>

The fundamental problem with the Templar-in-America hypothesis is not that it is impossible. Medieval European sailors demonstrably could cross the Atlantic; the Norse did it. The problem is that there is no evidence they did. No Templar artifact has been found in the Americas in a controlled archaeological context. No Templar-era European goods, no metallurgy, no biological traces (seeds, pollen, animal remains), no datable structural remains. The Newport Tower dates to the colonial period by every available measure: documentary record, mortar analysis, excavation, and comparison with known colonial architecture. The Westford Knight is, on close inspection, natural weathering marks on a glacial boulder, supplemented by modern chalking that enhances the alleged sword outline. The Kensington Runestone, even if accepted as genuine (and it is not), would prove fourteenth-century Scandinavian presence, not Templar presence; the inscription makes no mention of Templars, and the date of 1362 postdates the dissolution of the order by fifty years.

The Templar hypothesis survives because it is constructed to be unfalsifiable. If evidence is absent, it was hidden by the order's legendary secrecy. If artifacts are dated to the wrong period, the dating is flawed. If specialists reject an inscription, the specialists are part of the establishment that has always persecuted the Templars. The structure of the argument immunizes itself against refutation, which is precisely what distinguishes it from the Norse and Polynesian cases, where the evidence was tested, challenged, replicated, and ultimately accepted because it could not be explained away.


### The Phoenician Phantom

The claim that **Phoenician** sailors reached the Americas is older than the Templar hypothesis and equally unsupported. The Phoenicians were, by any measure, the greatest maritime civilization of the ancient Mediterranean. Based in the cities of **Tyre**, **Sidon**, and **Byblos** (in present-day Lebanon), they developed the alphabet that became the ancestor of Greek, Latin, and every European writing system in use today. They sailed beyond the Pillars of Hercules into the Atlantic, established colonies along the African coast and at Cadiz in Spain, and, according to Herodotus, circumnavigated Africa around **600 BCE** on commission from the Egyptian pharaoh **Necho II**, a voyage of roughly two years. They built **Carthage**, which nearly conquered Rome. Their navigational capabilities are not in question.

The question is whether they crossed the Atlantic, and here the evidence is entirely negative. No Phoenician artifact has been found in the Americas in a controlled excavation. No Phoenician pottery, metalwork, glass, purple dye residue, or biological material has been identified by professional archaeologists anywhere in the Western Hemisphere. The Phoenicians left dense material traces wherever they settled in the Mediterranean and along the African coast: harbors, warehouses, temples, coinage, pottery, metalwork, and inscriptions. The total absence of any such material in the Americas, despite centuries of intensive archaeological work across two continents, is not a gap in the record. It is the record.

The inscriptions cited as evidence have each been debunked by the relevant specialist communities. The Society for American Archaeology has formally stated that the absence of Phoenician trade goods, established settlements, or durable material traces typical of Phoenician maritime activity makes the hypothesis untenable. The argument persists for reasons that have more to do with the sociology of alternative history than with the Phoenicians themselves. If the Phoenicians reached the Americas, then the entire framework of pre-Columbian isolation collapses, and with it the establishment narrative about the independent development of New World civilizations. The Phoenicians serve as a wedge argument: accept them, and you must accept the possibility of other contacts. The argument has emotional and ideological appeal. It simply lacks evidence.


### The Counterintuitive Reversal

The strangest feature of the pre-Columbian contact debate is not that fringe claims are made without evidence. That is ordinary. The strange thing is that the two confirmed cases of pre-Columbian contact were themselves treated as fringe claims for decades before the evidence overwhelmed institutional resistance.

The Norse saga accounts of Vinland were dismissed by mainstream Americanist archaeologists for over a century as mythology. **Yale University** acquired a map in 1965, purportedly dating to the fifteenth century and showing a western landmass labeled "Vinlanda Insula," which was trumpeted as proof of Norse exploration and then exposed as a twentieth-century forgery based on the chemical composition of its ink. The controversy over the **Vinland Map** poisoned the academic well: anything associated with pre-Columbian Norse claims was tainted by the forger's ink. When the Ingstads began excavating at L'Anse aux Meadows, they faced skepticism from the same establishment that would later use the Norse discovery as the benchmark against which all other contact claims were measured. It took eight seasons of excavation, multiple independent radiocarbon dates, and the recovery of artifacts with no plausible non-Norse explanation before the archaeological community accepted what the sagas had been saying since the thirteenth century.

The Polynesian-South American contact was resisted even longer. Thor Heyerdahl's 1947 *Kon-Tiki* expedition, in which he sailed a balsa-wood raft from Peru to Polynesia, was designed to demonstrate that South Americans could have colonized the Pacific. Professional anthropologists dismissed the expedition as a stunt. Heyerdahl's broader theories about South American origins for Polynesian civilization were rejected on genetic and linguistic grounds. They were right to reject the broader theory. The Polynesians are of Southeast Asian origin, not South American. But Heyerdahl was right that contact had occurred; he simply had the direction wrong. The sweet potato went from South America to Polynesia, not the other way around, and the genetic admixture in Easter Island populations confirmed that actual human beings, not just tubers, had made the crossing. It took seventy-three years for the mainstream to catch up with the core of Heyerdahl's claim, even as it correctly discarded his specific mechanism.

Richards has noted an important structural feature of the debate: the mainstream tends to collapse all alternative claims into a single category of nonsense, which means that genuine observations get dismissed alongside fabricated ones. Archaeoastronomy, a discipline that studies ancient peoples' relationship to celestial phenomena, produces genuine insights but these get buried under the controversy generated by their association with alternative theorists like Hancock, who push the same data toward conclusions the evidence does not support. The baby gets thrown out with the bathwater, as Richards put it, because the establishment cannot separate the observation from the theorist who made it. Richards himself changed his position on several claims after investigating them: the **Giza ([Chapter 6](06-the-wadi-al-jarf-papyri.md))-Orion correlation** became more plausible to him (at fourth-dynasty dates, not Hancock's 10,500 BCE), while the carbon dating of the pyramids made him certain they were fourth-dynasty constructions. He followed the data, which moved him in different directions on different questions. This is what distinguishes middle-ground investigators from committed believers: a willingness to be moved in the direction the evidence points, even when that direction is inconvenient.

The lesson is not that the mainstream is always wrong. The mainstream was right about the Kensington Runestone, the Paraiba Stone, the Bat Creek inscription, the Newport Tower, the Phoenicians, and the Templars. The lesson is that institutional resistance applies equally to true claims and false ones. The Norse were real, and the establishment resisted. The Templars were imaginary (in America, at least), and the establishment resisted. The resistance looked the same from the outside, which is why proponents of false claims can always point to the Norse precedent and say: they laughed at that, too.

The difference, and it is the only difference that matters, is what happened when the evidence was tested.


### The Two That Were Real

The honest inventory of pre-Columbian transoceanic contact is brief.

Norse settlers occupied a site at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, in 1021 CE. The settlement was small, seasonal, and short-lived. It left no lasting cultural imprint on indigenous populations. The dating, confirmed by a solar-storm calibrated tree-ring analysis published in *Nature* in 2021, is the most precisely dated pre-Columbian contact event in the archaeological record.

Polynesian and South American populations made contact around 1200 CE. The genetic evidence, published in *Nature* in 2020, confirms both the sweet potato transfer and limited human admixture on Easter Island. The direction and mechanism of contact remain debated, but the contact itself is established.

Every other pre-Columbian contact claim, including Phoenician, Templar, Chinese, African, Egyptian, Welsh, and Irish hypotheses, rests on artifacts that have been independently identified as forgeries, misidentifications, or colonial-era constructions. No controlled archaeological excavation has produced material evidence supporting any of these claims. The inscriptions cited as evidence have been rejected by specialists in the relevant writing systems. The Newport Tower is a seventeenth-century colonial windmill. The Kensington Runestone is a nineteenth-century forgery. The Paraiba Stone is an 1872 fabrication that exists only as a transcription. The Bat Creek inscription matches a Masonic reference book illustration.

Two contacts were real. Both were resisted by the establishment before the evidence became undeniable. Both were confirmed by the same scientific methods, radiocarbon dating, genetics, materials analysis, that debunked the false claims. The tools do not have an agenda. The tools simply measure what is there.

Benedict Arnold's will, filed in 1677, lists a stone windmill among his property. The will is available in the Rhode Island state archives. Nobody has disputed its authenticity. The tower is still standing in Touro Park, where tourists photograph it and plaques describe its contested history. The contest, by every measure available to science, ended in 1948 when the Peabody team dug up a boot print and a clay pipe.

The boot print is still in the archive. It has never made anyone's heart beat faster.
