## Chapter 20: The Dry Falls – Megafloods, the Scablands, and Glacial Lake Outbursts
In the autumn of 1922, a geologist named **J Harlen Bretz** drove across eastern **Washington State** and saw a landscape that did not make sense.

Bretz (whose first name was J, a complete name and not an initial, a fact he spent a lifetime correcting proofreaders about) was forty years old, a professor at the University of Chicago, and had been studying the geology of the **Columbia Plateau** for several years. The plateau is a vast expanse of ancient basalt, extruded by volcanic eruptions between seventeen million and six million years ago and in some places more than two thousand meters thick. On top of the basalt, over millions of years, wind had deposited a thick blanket of fine dust called **loess**, and on top of the loess, wheat grew. Eastern Washington is farm country. The plateau should have been, from a geological standpoint, unremarkable: flat, fertile, and monotonous.

It was none of these things. Across more than sixteen thousand square kilometers, the loess was gone. In its place were enormous channels carved directly into the basalt bedrock, channels that ran across drainage divides as though the topography that directed normal river flow had been simply irrelevant to whatever force had cut them. The channels were floored with scoured black rock, carved into mazes of buttes and canyons that local farmers called **scabland**, a name Bretz adopted because it was descriptive and because it carried the connotation of a wound. On the channel floors, arranged in neat curving rows, lay gravel bars the size of buildings, their shapes identical to the ripple marks left by flowing water in a streambed, except that these ripples were tens of meters high and hundreds of meters from crest to crest. There were enormous plunge pools, dry potholes ground into the basalt by the swirling action of debris-laden water at velocities no modern river approaches. There were erratic boulders, some the size of houses, deposited hundreds of feet above any modern watercourse in places where no glacier had existed to carry them. And at the southern end of a sixty-mile gash in the earth called **Grand Coulee**, there was a horseshoe-shaped cliff three and a half miles wide and four hundred feet high, with a dry plunge pool at its base and scalloped headcuts in the basalt where enormous volumes of water had plucked blocks from the bedrock like a hand pulling apart a loaf of bread.

This was **Dry Falls**. It was the largest waterfall that had ever existed on planet Earth. And it was bone dry.

Bretz spent ten weeks in the field that season, driving from channel to channel, climbing ridgelines, measuring gravel bars, photographing the scoured basalt. He later described the experience in a passage that has become one of the most quoted in the history of American geology: "No one with an eye for landforms can cross eastern Washington in daylight without encountering and being impressed by the 'scabland.' Like great scars marring the otherwise fair face of the plateau are these elongated tracts of bare, or nearly bare, black rock carved into mazes of buttes and canyons. Everybody on the plateau knows scabland. It interrupts the wheat lands, parceling them out into hill tracts less than 40 acres to more than 40 square miles in extent."

His 1923 paper ran systematically through the evidence: the scoured channels, the ripple marks, the plunge pools, the erratics, the enormous fans of shattered basalt at the mouths of the channels. "Fully 3,000 square miles of the Columbia plateau were swept by the glacial flood, and the loess and silt cover removed," he wrote. "More than 2,000 square miles of this area were left as bare, eroded, rock-cut channel floors, now the Scablands, and nearly 1,000 square miles carry gravel deposits derived from the eroded basalt."

And then he arrived at the conclusion that would cost him the next forty years of his professional life.

"It was a debacle which swept the Columbia Plateau."

A single catastrophic flood. One event. Unimaginably large. A heresy.


### The Room That Went Silent

The reaction of the geological establishment was, in the words of Bretz's biographer, stunned and embarrassed silence, followed by coordinated opposition.

Since the early nineteenth century, geology had been governed by the principle of **uniformitarianism**, the doctrine that the processes observable in the present (erosion, sedimentation, slow uplift, gradual cooling) are the only processes that have operated in the past, and that they operate at roughly the same rates. Uniformitarianism was geology's founding creed, the intellectual weapon that had liberated the science from biblical literalism and from the catastrophism of **Georges Cuvier**, who had proposed that the Earth's surface was shaped by a series of divine cataclysms culminating in Noah's Flood. The triumph of uniformitarianism over catastrophism in the mid-nineteenth century was, for geologists, what the Copernican revolution was for astronomy: a before-and-after moment that defined the discipline's identity. To propose, in the 1920s, that a single catastrophic flood had reshaped thousands of square miles of North America was to commit the one sin that geology could not forgive. You were trafficking in catastrophes. You were, in effect, bringing back the Flood.

In January 1927, Bretz presented his findings at a meeting of the **Geological Society of Washington**. **David Alt**, who later became Professor Emeritus of Geology at the **University of Montana**, described the reaction through the eyes of one of his own old professors, who had been a student in the audience that day: "The geologists were aghast in the same way that a roomful of physicists would be upon hearing a colleague explain how he had made a perpetual motion machine out of old popsicle sticks. Physicists had all learned very early of the futility of perpetual motion machines, and no properly educated geologist was supposed to traffic in catastrophes of any sort."

**W.C. Alden**, Chief of Pleistocene Geology with the profoundly conservative United States Geological Survey, objected to "the idea that all the channels must have been developed simultaneously in a very short time" and took great offense at "the tremendous amount of water" Bretz postulated. "It seems to me impossible," Alden protested, "that such part of the great ice fields as would have drained across the Columbia Plateau could, under any conditions, have yielded so much water as is called for in so short a time." He admitted that he had never visited the Scablands himself. He felt certain that a uniformitarian explanation was what was required: "The problem would be easier if longer time and repeated floods could be allotted to do the work."

**James Gilluly**, a well-known apostle of geological gradualism, dismissed the notion of a single cataclysmic flood with words like "preposterous," "incompetent," and "wholly inadequate." He found nothing in Bretz's evidence to exclude his own preferred explanation: multiple smaller floods "of the order of magnitude of the present Columbia's, or at most a few times as large."

**G.R. Mansfield** doubted "that so much work could be done on basalt in so short a time" and preferred "persistent ponding and overflow of marginal glacial waters." **O.E. Meinzer** confessed that "the erosion features of the region are large and bizarre" but preferred to believe that "the existing features can be explained by assuming normal stream work of the ancient Columbia River."

Not a single voice was raised in Bretz's support. The massed geologists homed in on what they clearly believed was the fatal flaw: Bretz had failed to identify a convincing source for his floodwaters. The evidence showed a catastrophic flood. He could not say where the water came from.

Bretz replied that the absence of a documented source did not prove the absence of a flood. "I believe that my interpretation of Channeled Scabland should stand or fall on the scabland phenomena themselves," he said. The evidence was in the field. It was carved into bedrock. It would not become less carved because a room full of geologists found it embarrassing.

"Ideas without precedent," he wrote later, "are generally looked on with disfavor and men are shocked if their conceptions of an orderly world are challenged."


### The Missing Lake

The source Bretz could not identify was sitting in western **Montana**, and a man named **Joseph Thomas Pardee** knew about it.

Glacial Lake Missoula had formed during the last ice age when the **Cordilleran ice sheet**, advancing southward from Canada, threw a lobe of glacial ice across the **Clark Fork River** near present-day **Lake Pend Oreille** in Idaho. Behind this natural dam of ice, meltwater accumulated in the valleys of western Montana, filling them to a depth of over two thousand feet and creating an inland sea that extended into the valleys of the **Flathead**, **Bitterroot**, and **Clark Fork** rivers. At maximum extent, the lake held roughly 2,500 cubic kilometers of ice-cold meltwater, approximately half the volume of **Lake Michigan**, contained behind a dam of ice perhaps two thousand feet tall. The mathematics of the situation were remorseless. The basal pressure of that much water on the heel of the ice dam exceeded 950 pounds per square inch. Ice floats when the water it rests on exerts enough upward force to overcome its weight. A column of water two thousand feet deep pushing against the downstream toe of the ice dam would, at a certain critical point, begin to lift the dam off its bed. Once the seal broke, even by inches, water would rush through the gap, widening it exponentially by thermal and mechanical erosion. The dam would not fail in the way that an earthen dam fails, with a slow overtopping followed by a gradual breach. It would fail catastrophically, the ice lifting and shattering as the entire lake behind it sought the Pacific Ocean through a gap that went from inches to hundreds of feet in a matter of hours.

Pardee had first described the lake in a 1910 paper and had been refining his understanding of its drainage characteristics ever since. He wrote to Bretz in 1925, quietly suggesting that a catastrophic draining of this lake might account for the Spokane Flood. At the 1927 Washington meeting, both Alden and Pardee suggested that Bretz consider "the sudden draining of a glacial lake," but Bretz was skeptical. His calculations indicated that the volume of Lake Missoula was insufficient: "'Twould run the flood for only 2 weeks," reads a handwritten note in his outline for the 1927 presentation. Two weeks seemed too short to carve thirty miles of catastrophically retreating waterfall, to excavate twenty cubic miles of basalt from Grand Coulee, and to leave the wreckage that he had spent ten years documenting.

Pardee waited. For thirteen years, he waited. It was not timidity that held him silent. It was the structure of the geological establishment itself. Pardee was a federal employee of the USGS, an agency headed by W.C. Alden, the same man who had led the opposition to Bretz at the 1927 meeting. Alden had objected to Bretz's "tremendous amount of water" without ever visiting the Scablands. He was now Pardee's direct superior. The connection between Glacial Lake Missoula and Bretz's flood was, for Pardee, an insight that could cost him his career if it was presented as support for a hypothesis that the most powerful man in American geology had publicly condemned. So Pardee wrote private letters, gathered evidence in the Montana lake basin, mapped the ancient shorelines, and said nothing in public.

In 1940, the **American Association for the Advancement of Science** held a meeting in Seattle. Bretz was invited but declined; he said his views were already in print. Pardee attended and presented, for the first time publicly, his long-held conclusion that the entire lake had drained catastrophically through a sudden failure of the ice dam. "Most likely quite dramatically," was his understated phrasing. Curiously, he did not connect his findings explicitly to Bretz's well-known flood hypothesis. Bretz, reflecting on this later, wrote with characteristic generosity: "He never said, at least in print, anything about the final deposition of this vigorous discharge. I do believe, however, that he was generously leaving that to me."

In the years that followed, Bretz gradually embraced Lake Missoula as the source. But he also made a strategic concession. "There were several floods," he wrote in 1959. "The theory is elastic enough to take care of that." The concession was necessary for institutional survival. Multiple floods, spread over several thousand years, were more palatable to uniformitarian sensibilities than a single debacle that arrived without warning and was over in weeks.

Bretz accepted up to eight floods. This was the price of rehabilitation. His original insight, that the Scablands were carved by catastrophic flooding, was correct. His original mechanism, a single overwhelming deluge, was correct in kind if not in number. But the history of how his theory was received illustrates something that extends well beyond geology: it is possible for an entire discipline to reject correct evidence for decades, not because the evidence is weak, but because it violates the theoretical framework through which the evidence is evaluated. Bretz had data. His opponents had a worldview. For forty years, the worldview won.


### The Telegram

In 1965, the **International Union for Quaternary Research** organized a field trip to the Columbia Plateau for many of Bretz's former critics. The group traversed the full length of Grand Coulee, part of the Quincy Basin, and much of the Palouse-Snake scabland divide. They drove across the Ephrata Erratics Fan, where millions of jagged basalt boulders lie scattered across the prairie in every direction as far as the eye can see, the rubble of a former world flushed from the coulee by the waters. They stood at the rim of Dry Falls and looked down at the scalloped headcuts, the dry plunge pool, the sheer scale of the thing.

At the end of the trip, the participants, humbled by what they had seen and satisfied as to the source of the floodwaters in Glacial Lake Missoula, sent Bretz a telegram. It closed with five words: "We are all now catastrophists."

"Be assured," Bretz wrote back, "that after 30 years, and 30 papers in self-defense, and more than 30 people who vigorously denied my theory, it did my heart good like medicine."

The final accolade came in 1979, when Bretz, aged ninety-six, received the **Penrose Medal**, the Geological Society of America's highest honor. "Perhaps," he wrote in his acceptance note, "I can be credited with reviving and demystifying legendary catastrophism and challenging a too rigorous uniformitarianism."

After the ceremony, he told his son: "All my enemies are dead, so I have no one to gloat over."

Bretz died on February 3, 1981, at the age of ninety-eight. He had outlived not only his critics but the uniformitarian consensus that had powered their objections. The word "catastrophism," which in 1923 was a professional insult, had by 1981 become a recognized and respectable branch of earth science. Bretz had not merely proposed a theory. He had, almost single-handedly, forced an entire discipline to revise its founding assumptions about what the Earth was capable of doing.


### The Vampire of Gradualism

The vindication appeared complete. The evidence of a land scoured by cataclysmic deluge could not be denied. The source had been identified. The geologists had sent their telegram. The medal had been awarded. But as Hancock ([see Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)) observed in *Magicians of the Gods*, once Bretz had invited the vampire of gradualism through the door, it would not be satisfied with the compromise he had tried to strike. It kept sucking the blood out of any notion that what had happened in the Channeled Scablands had been anything truly extraordinary.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[16](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[17](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[18](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[19](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[20](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[21](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[22](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[23](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[24](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[25](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[26](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

The eight floods Bretz had allowed steadily multiplied. A dozen became twenty. Twenty became thirty-five. Thirty-five became "about forty." Recent papers have proposed as many as ninety or more. As Victor Baker of the University of Arizona's Department of Hydrology summarized: "The most current opinion is that there were about eighty floods that all occurred within a period of 2,500 years." Eighty floods in 2,500 years works out to one flood approximately every thirty-one years, a rather regular, essentially gradualist series of events that does away with the need for any single exceptional cataclysm and accounts for the devastation of the Scablands by accumulated effects, like a bathtub overflowing repeatedly rather than a dam breaking once.

Better still, from the uniformitarian perspective, outburst floods from ice-dammed glacial lakes still occur today. They happen in Iceland, where they are called **jokulhlaups**, the term adopted worldwide. They happen in Alaska and British Columbia, where glacially dammed lakes drain regularly during summer snowmelt. As geology professor David Alt pointed out, the ice dam that held Glacial Lake Missoula "probably floated and broke during the summer for the same reason." The doctrine that "the present is the key to the past" quietly reasserted itself. Bretz's catastrophe was domesticated into a summer routine.

The scholars contrived to have their cake and eat it: on one hand, giving Bretz a medal and proclaiming that they are "all catastrophists now"; on the other, quietly transmuting his catastrophe into the sort of thing one sees every few decades in Alaska.


### The Scale of the Wound

Carlson ([Chapter 4](04-the-cubit-coincidence.md)) does not accept this transmutation.<sup>[27](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

Carlson is in every meaningful sense the inheritor of Bretz's original position. He does not dispute that Glacial Lake Missoula existed, or that outburst floods from it occurred. What he disputes is the claim that the lake, even at its maximum volume, was anywhere near large enough to account for the full extent of the scabland features.

The numbers are the core of his argument.

Grand Coulee, the sixty-mile channel that terminates at Dry Falls, required the excavation and removal of at least twenty cubic miles of basalt. The Ephrata Erratics Fan at its southern end extends for miles. "Everything was reduced to rubble," Carlson explained during a field tour with Hancock, gesturing at the shattered landscape. "And that's what you're seeing. This rubble was part of the former world. The antediluvian world. And what's lying here on the surface is just a fraction of what the flood flushed out of Grand Coulee. The rubble goes down deep."<sup>[28](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

At Dry Falls itself, the mechanism of catastrophic erosion is preserved in the rock. The horseshoe-shaped cliff face shows scalloped headcuts where blocks of basalt were plucked from the bedrock by the turbulence of falling water. "Like giant fingers plucking out blocks of the basalt bedrock, ripping them out, dragging them into the torrent and sweeping them downstream," Carlson described it. The falls migrated northward as the water ate upstream, just as **Niagara Falls** has retreated seven miles in twelve thousand years. The difference: Dry Falls retreated approximately thirty miles, the entire length of Lower Grand Coulee, in what Carlson estimates was less than a month. "Thousands of times faster than Niagara," he calculated, "because of the incredible amount and force of the water here. Dry Falls was the greatest waterfall that's ever existed on planet earth."<sup>[29](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

On the **Waterville Plateau**, north of Grand Coulee, Carlson showed Hancock clusters of enormous boulders deposited hundreds of feet above modern watercourses. These are not ordinary glacial erratics dropped by retreating ice. They are **ice-rafted erratics**: boulders that were embedded in icebergs, carried by floodwaters deep enough to fill the valleys from rim to rim, and dropped when the icebergs melted or grounded on high terrain. Near **Wenatchee**, at the confluence of the Wenatchee and Columbia Rivers, an erratic weighing an estimated eighteen thousand tons sits on a ridgeline hundreds of feet above the modern town. "Obviously," Carlson explained from the top of the boulder, looking down at the rivers glistening far below, "the flood waters must have filled the whole valley from bottom to top, so when the iceberg floated in it stranded right here, then melted away and left this sitting on the ridge."<sup>[30](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

The peak discharge estimates for the largest Missoula flood are staggering by any standard. Hydrological modeling by **Jim O'Connor** and Victor Baker calculated a peak flow rate of roughly seventeen million cubic meters per second at the point where the floodwaters emerged from the ice dam breach. That figure is approximately ten times the combined discharge of every river on Earth flowing simultaneously. At the narrows where the flood poured through **Rathdrum Prairie** and the **Spokane Valley**, the water was moving at velocities estimated between thirty and sixty miles per hour, carrying icebergs the size of buildings and grinding the basalt bedrock into rubble.<sup>[31](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

At **Wallula Gap**, where all the scabland channels converge before the combined floodwaters pour into the Columbia Gorge, Carlson's GPS read 1,150 feet above sea level. The flood evidence reaches to roughly 1,200 feet. The water, had they been standing there during the event, would have been fifty feet over their heads.


### The Source Problem

The objection to Carlson's position is the same one that haunted Bretz in 1927: where did the water come from?

If Glacial Lake Missoula, with its 2,500 cubic kilometers, is insufficient to explain the largest scabland features, then an additional source is required. Carlson proposes that the Missoula floods were real but represent only one component of a much larger hydrological event: the catastrophic collapse of the North American ice sheet itself, possibly triggered by a cosmic impact at the onset of the Younger Dryas, roughly 12,800 years ago. The **Cordilleran** and **Laurentide** ice sheets, at their maximum extent, contained water equivalent to approximately seventy meters of global sea-level rise. If these ice sheets were destabilized not by gradual warming but by an extraterrestrial event that delivered concentrated energy to specific locations on the ice, the resulting meltwater would have been released not as a seasonal trickle but as a catastrophic pulse.

Carlson points to geomorphological evidence that the deglaciation did not proceed uniformly from the margins inward. There were epicenters of extreme melting where water penetrated through the glaciers, reached the substrate, and carved the subglacial environment into massive radial swarms of **drumlins**, elongated hills formed by high-pressure subglacial flow. These drumlin swarms radiate from epicenters, implying concentrated energy inputs. Lake Nipigon in Ontario and **Lake St. John** in Quebec are candidates for such epicenters. If a cosmic impact delivered energy to specific points on the ice sheet, the resulting melt would have been local in origin but continental in consequence.

The mainstream position, represented by the USGS review of Missoula and Bonneville floods, holds that no additional mechanism is needed. The scablands are the product of "dozens to over a hundred" outburst events from Glacial Lake Missoula during the last glacial period. The floods occurred at different times over thousands of years, each one scouring the landscape further. No single debacle is required.

The debate continues. It has continued, in one form or another, for a century.


### The Black Rain

There is a detail in the field evidence that does not fit neatly into either the gradualist or the catastrophist framework, and it is worth pausing on.

Hancock described standing with Carlson on a high bluff overlooking Wallula Gap, where the multiple scabland channels converge before the combined waters enter the Columbia Gorge. The sky was gray and thunderous. Rain fell steadily. Below them, the Columbia River, more than a mile wide at that point, reflected the stormlight. Across the river, the terrain rose again, still covered by the thick powdery loess, still marked by scabland topography: cliffs dropping into valleys, outcrops carved by the ancient floods, and most prominently the two isolated basalt pillars known as the **Twin Sisters** standing as remnants of a landscape that had been mostly stripped away.<sup>[32](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[33](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

Carlson pointed to the loess that lay everywhere under their feet on the bluff. "This is post-flood," he explained. The loess, the fine dust that is now the top layer of the plateau everywhere the scablands did not strip it away, accumulated after the flooding ended. Beneath the loess, the basalt bears the scars. Above it, the world rebuilt itself, grain by wind-blown grain, over thousands of years.

Between these two layers, at some sites, there is a thin stratum of dark material. It contains elevated levels of soot, charcoal, and in some locations, nanodiamonds and platinum-group elements consistent with the **Younger Dryas boundary layer** found at more than fifty sites across three continents. Whether this layer is present at all scabland sites, and whether it predates, postdates, or is contemporaneous with the last major flooding episode, is a question that has not been definitively resolved.<sup>[34](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

If the dark layer precedes the final flood, it suggests that whatever caused the Younger Dryas boundary event (the proposed cosmic impact) also triggered the final and largest pulse of flooding. If it postdates the flood, the connection between the impact hypothesis and the scablands weakens. The stratigraphy at individual sites is often ambiguous, compressed, and disturbed by the very floods it is supposed to date.<sup>[35](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

This is the kind of evidence that is too complex for either side's preferred narrative. The catastrophists want it to confirm that a cosmic impact triggered the megafloods. The gradualists want it to be irrelevant, a coincidental dusting on top of a landscape already carved by ordinary ice-dam processes. The evidence itself is simply there, thin and dark and stubbornly uncommitted.


### The Altai Mirror

The Channeled Scablands are not the only megaflood landscape on Earth, a fact that both strengthens and complicates the catastrophist position.

In the **Altai Mountains** of southern Siberia, along the **Chuya** and **Katun** rivers, geologists have documented a strikingly similar set of features: enormous gravel bars, giant current ripple marks, and scoured bedrock consistent with catastrophic flooding from the draining of **Glacial Lake Kuray-Chuya**. The estimated peak discharge exceeds that of even the largest proposed Missoula flood, making it the largest freshwater flood documented in the geological record of Asia.

**Lake Bonneville**, in what is now **Utah**, experienced a catastrophic outburst around 14,500 years ago when the lake overtopped its natural dam at **Red Rock Pass** in Idaho. The resulting flood, with an estimated peak discharge of one million cubic meters per second, carved channels through the Snake River Plain and deposited boulders, gravel bars, and scoured bedrock across southern Idaho. The Bonneville flood is uncontroversial, its mechanism well-understood, and its features similar in kind (if not in scale) to those of the Scablands.

The **Zanclean flood**, approximately 5.3 million years ago, refilled the **Mediterranean Sea** after it had largely evaporated during the **Messinian salinity crisis**, sending Atlantic water through the **Strait of Gibraltar** at estimated flow rates that would have raised the Mediterranean sea level by ten meters per day. This event predates modern humans by millions of years, but it demonstrates that Earth's hydrological systems are capable of violence on a scale that the gradualist imagination finds difficult to accommodate.

These parallels establish that catastrophic flooding is not a fringe concept. It is a recognized geological process. The question is not whether megafloods occur; it is whether the specific megaflood that carved the Channeled Scablands was as exceptional as Bretz originally believed, or whether it was merely the cumulative result of many smaller events, each one within the range of ordinary glacial outburst processes.

Carlson's position is that the largest features require a flood of singular magnitude. The mainstream position is that accumulation over dozens of events is sufficient. The landscape, stubbornly carved and monumentally silent, does not resolve the disagreement. It merely outlasts it.<sup>[36](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>


### The Wound That Does Not Heal

The honest inventory of the scablands question is shorter than anyone on either side would prefer.<sup>[37](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

J Harlen Bretz was right that the Channeled Scablands were carved by catastrophic flooding. He was right that the evidence required an explanation that uniformitarian geology, for half a century, was unwilling to provide. He was right that the scale of the features demanded water volumes and flow velocities far beyond anything in the modern hydrological record. He spent forty years fighting an establishment that preferred any explanation, however implausible, to a catastrophe, and he lived long enough to see that establishment award him its highest honor.<sup>[38](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

The identification of Glacial Lake Missoula as the primary source of floodwaters is supported by extensive evidence: shoreline deposits, varves in the lake basin, the geometry of the ice dam, the distribution of sediment downstream. The lake existed, it was enormous, and it drained catastrophically.<sup>[39](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

Whether it drained once, eight times, or ninety times is a question that different researchers answer differently, with the number of proposed floods generally increasing in proportion to the researcher's commitment to gradualist principles. This correlation between theoretical preference and empirical conclusion is not conclusive evidence of bias, but it is not nothing either.

The question Carlson raises, whether the Missoula floods alone can account for the full extent of the scabland features or whether a larger, possibly cosmically triggered event is required, remains formally unresolved. Both positions are defended by researchers who have spent time in the field. Neither has been definitively falsified.<sup>[40](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

Dry Falls stands in the middle of Grand Coulee, three and a half miles wide, four hundred feet high, the largest waterfall the planet has ever produced, now silent, dry, surrounded by sagebrush and basalt under an enormous sky. It is a monument to an event that most people have never heard of, carved by forces that the geological establishment spent half a century denying and the other half domesticating. Bretz saw the scars and had the honesty to name what made them. His critics had the institutional authority to deny what they had not seen. The landscape was right all along.<sup>[41](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

"All my enemies are dead," Bretz said in 1979, at ninety-six years old, with two years left to live, "so I have no one to gloat over."<sup>[42](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>

The scars have not healed. They are not the kind that do.<sup>[43](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup><sup>[44](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-20)</sup>
