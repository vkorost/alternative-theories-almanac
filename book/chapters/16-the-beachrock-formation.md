# Part III: The Drowned World


## Chapter 16: The Beachrock Formation – Underwater Ruins and Submerged Civilizations
On September 2, 1968, a diver named **J. Manson Valentine** surfaced off the coast of **North Bimini Island** in the Bahamas and told his colleagues that he had found a road on the sea floor. The structure lay in about five meters of water: a roughly J-shaped alignment of large, flat, roughly rectangular limestone blocks stretching some seven hundred meters along the sandy bottom. The blocks were fitted close together with their longer axes running perpendicular to the formation's overall trend, like a pavement laid by masons who had a clear notion of where the road should go but no particular interest in uniformity of block size. Some were three meters across. Others were half that. The joints between them were straight enough to suggest intention. The overall effect, seen from above in clear Bahamian water, was of something designed.

Valentine was not a geologist. He was a zoologist by training, a diver by avocation, and a museum curator at the **Museum of Science in Miami** by profession. He was also, and this is the detail that has shadowed every subsequent discussion of the site, a devotee of Edgar Cayce, the American clairvoyant who had delivered over fourteen thousand psychic "readings" between 1901 and his death in 1945. In one of those readings, delivered in 1938, Cayce had predicted that a portion of Atlantis would be discovered near Bimini around 1968 or 1969. The prediction was specific in both location and timing, a rarity in the prophetic business. It was specific enough to embarrass anyone who remembered it if nothing turned up, and specific enough to excite everyone when Valentine announced, within the window Cayce had named, that he had found what appeared to be a submerged architectural feature in exactly the place Cayce had specified.

The coincidence was irresistible. The press called the formation the **Bimini Road** before anyone had taken a core sample or measured a joint angle. The name stuck. Within a year, the Bimini Road had become one of the foundational artifacts of the modern alternative-archaeology movement, a movement that was just finding its legs in the late 1960s and needed exactly this kind of visual, visceral, photogenic evidence. Here was a structure on the sea floor. It looked like something built. It was underwater, which meant it must have been on dry land at some earlier time, when sea levels were lower. And the most famous psychic in American history had predicted its discovery, in the right place, at the right time.

The Bimini Road has appeared in every edition of the lost-civilization argument since Valentine's announcement: in television documentaries, in Hancock's ***Underworld*** (2002), in the Netflix series *Ancient Apocalypse* (2022), and in the presentations of researchers who have never dived on the site and never intend to. For over half a century it has served as a visual proof of concept, a formation that looks too regular to be natural and too submerged to be modern. It is the wallpaper of the underwater-ruins genre.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-16)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-16)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-16)</sup>

The difficulty is that geologists looked at the Bimini Road almost immediately after Valentine's announcement, and what they found was not a road.

### The Limestone Fracture

**Eugene Shinn**, a carbonate geologist at the **United States Geological Survey** who had spent most of his career studying the limestone formations of southern Florida and the Bahamas, visited the site in the early 1970s. Shinn was not a debunker by temperament. He was a sedimentologist who knew exactly what limestone does in tropical marine environments, because he had watched it do those things, under the microscope and in the field, for decades. He extracted core samples from the blocks, thin-sectioned them, and examined the internal structure under a petrographic microscope. What he found was decisive in one respect and quietly devastating in another.

The decisive finding: the blocks were composed of **beachrock**, a type of limestone that forms naturally in the intertidal zone when calcium carbonate precipitates from seawater and cements loose sediment, shell fragments, and sand into a hard slab. Beachrock formation is rapid by geological standards, sometimes consolidating loose sand into solid rock in decades rather than centuries. The process requires nothing more than warm seawater, a gently sloping shoreline, and time. It is not rare. It is not exotic. It happens on virtually every tropical and subtropical coastline in the world, and it produces exactly the flat, tabular, uniformly thick slabs that Valentine had found. The blocks at Bimini were not quarried, shaped, or transported. They had formed in place, as a continuous sheet of cemented beach sediment, and then fractured.

The fracture pattern was the second finding, and it was the one that should have ended the debate. Beachrock fractures along **orthogonal joint sets**, planes of weakness that develop as the rock dries during low-tide exposure, contracts slightly as it cures, and responds to the cyclical mechanical stress of tidal flexing. These joints are typically perpendicular to each other, creating a pattern of roughly rectangular blocks separated by straight-edged gaps that look, to a non-geologist viewing them through five meters of water, like the joints in a masonry pavement. Shinn documented that the Bimini blocks' jointing pattern, thickness, composition, and internal sedimentary structure were consistent in every measurable respect with natural beachrock formation and fracture. He also noted that the blocks showed no tool marks, no mortar, no dressed surfaces, no quarry marks, and no foundation preparation beneath them. There was no rubble fill, no leveling layer, no construction debris. Every feature that would be expected in any deliberate construction, regardless of the culture that built it, from the most rudimentary Neolithic wall to a Roman road, was absent.

The comparison that ended the debate for most geologists was geographic rather than chemical. **Identical beachrock pavements** were identified at **Loggerhead Key** in the **Dry Tortugas**, roughly 350 kilometers from Bimini, and at **Pulley Ridge** off Florida's southwest coast. Both formations displayed the same J-curved alignment (a consequence of the geometry of the original shoreline), the same orthogonal jointing, the same block dimensions, and the same absence of associated artifacts. Nobody proposed that the Dry Tortugas formation was a lost highway. Nobody suggested that Pulley Ridge was an Atlantean loading dock. The process was the same; the product was the same. **The Beachrock Formation**, as a natural geological phenomenon, was not exotic. It was ordinary. It was so ordinary that any tropical-coast geologist could have identified it in the field in an afternoon, which is approximately how long it took Shinn to do so.

Richards, who examined both sides of the Bimini debate with his characteristic lack of reverence for either, put the matter in terms that are difficult to improve upon. The Bimini Road, he observed, was the weakest piece of evidence in the lost-civilization portfolio, the one where the geological explanation was not a matter of contested interpretation or missing data but of straightforward, reproducible, utterly uncontroversial sedimentology. Heavy biological erosion on the block surfaces (coral encrustation, boring by marine organisms, dissolution by slightly acidic seawater) had obliterated any surface features that would distinguish worked stone from natural rock. No pottery, tools, inscriptions, or human remains had ever been found in association with the formation, despite more than fifty years of diving at the site by enthusiasts, researchers, and documentary film crews. The absence of artifacts was not a gap in the record. It was the record.

Richards was equally blunt about what the Bimini Road's persistence in the alternative literature revealed about the field itself: when you keep leading with your worst evidence, he observed, it becomes very hard to get people to take your best evidence seriously. The Bimini Road was exhibit A in a courtroom where the prosecution had far better material, and the decision to keep it on the stand was a strategic error that undermined claims that actually deserved investigation.

### The Prophecy Problem

The Bimini Road raises a question that extends beyond geology, one that the alternative community has never adequately addressed. Valentine found the formation because he was looking for it, because Edgar Cayce told him where and when to look. This is not, in itself, a reason to dismiss the finding; scientists have made genuine discoveries while pursuing wrong theories, and the motivation for looking under a particular rock has no bearing on what is actually under it. But the Cayce connection created a confirmation bias so powerful that it shaped the entire subsequent investigation.

The divers who followed Valentine to the site were not, for the most part, trained geologists. They were believers. They arrived with cameras and an expectation, and they left with photographs that confirmed what they had expected to find. The photographs showed rectangular blocks with straight edges. They did not show the petrographic sections that would reveal the blocks' internal sedimentary structure, or the core samples that would establish whether the blocks were cemented beach sand or quarried stone, or the foundation profiles that would show whether anything had been prepared beneath the formation. These are the investigations that distinguish geology from photography. Shinn performed them. The alternative researchers, by and large, did not.

This pattern, photographic evidence without geological analysis, visual persuasion without material testing, recurs at every underwater site in the alternative literature. It is not unique to the alternative community; plenty of mainstream researchers have been misled by visual appearances in the field. But the consistency of the pattern suggests something more than individual error. It suggests a methodology that privileges the eye over the instrument, and that finds the eye sufficient because the conclusion has already been determined.

Richards noted a further irony. The Bimini Road's prominence in the alternative narrative made it harder, not easier, to secure funding for the kind of marine archaeology that might actually find something. Grant committees and institutional review boards had seen the Bimini Road invoked so many times in support of pseudoarchaeological claims that "underwater ruins" had become, in academic circles, a phrase that triggered reflexive skepticism. The professional marine archaeologists who might have surveyed the continental shelves for genuine pre-Holocene settlements were, in part because of Bimini, unable to get the funding to do so. The worst evidence in the portfolio was poisoning the well for the best.

### The Sunken Cities

The Bimini Road is the most famous underwater site in the alternative canon. It is not the most interesting. Far more complex questions surround three other locations: the **Yonaguni Monument** off the coast of Japan, the submerged ruins off the coast of **Dwarka** in India, and the sonar anomalies in the **Gulf of Khambhat**.

The Yonaguni Monument was discovered in 1987 by **Kihachiro Aratake**, a local diver who encountered an enormous terraced formation in the waters off **Yonaguni Island**, the westernmost point of the **Ryukyu archipelago** at the extreme southwestern tip of Japan. The structure lies at depths of five to twenty-five meters and consists of flat platforms, right-angled steps, and what appear to be carved channels. Its largest single feature is a roughly rectangular platform approximately five meters high and over a hundred meters long. Underwater photographs show clean right angles, flat surfaces, and what look like staircases descending from one terrace level to the next. The visual impression is overwhelming. This looks built.

Hancock ([see Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)) devoted extended attention to Yonaguni in *Underworld*, diving it repeatedly and subjecting it to the analysis of Schoch ([Chapter 7](07-the-rain-that-wasnt.md)), whose conclusion was not what Hancock had hoped for. The sandstone at Yonaguni contains well-defined parallel **bedding planes** and crisscrossing vertical **joint sets** that cause it to fracture naturally into rectangular and stepped shapes. The terraces were bedding planes exposed by differential erosion. The "steps" were fracture surfaces along joint sets. The "channels" were dissolution features along weaker strata. Schoch found the same process producing similar formations at multiple other locations worldwide, wherever layered sandstone meets wave action and chemical weathering.

Schoch did acknowledge that some features of the site were difficult to explain purely by natural fracture. A triangular depression resembling a carved pool, a feature that might be a crude face, and a few markings on the rock that could be anthropogenic caught his attention. But his overall assessment was that the monument was a natural geological formation, possibly modified by human activity at some point but not constructed. Neither the **Japanese Agency for Cultural Affairs** nor the **Okinawa** prefectural government recognizes the site as a cultural artifact. No pottery, tools, or human remains have been found in direct association with the structure, despite decades of diving.

The site's most vocal champion, **Masaaki Kimura**, a marine geologist at the **University of the Ryukyus**, published papers arguing that certain features could not be natural. Kimura identified what he described as a carved face, a drainage channel, and a road leading away from the main formation. His interpretations have not been accepted by the broader geological community, but neither have they been formally refuted by a study of equivalent thoroughness. The Japanese government has shown no interest in resolving the question, which would require a full archaeological survey (not merely geological observations by visiting divers) at a depth and in currents that make such a survey logistically difficult and expensive. The site remains unresolved, which is the most honest thing that can be said about it.

### The Dredge and the Sonar

The Gulf of Khambhat case is different in kind from either Bimini or Yonaguni, and it deserves more careful attention than it typically receives, because it illustrates a failure of method that recurs throughout the underwater-archaeology wing of the alternative literature.

In 2001, the **National Institute of Ocean Technology** (**NIOT**) in India announced that **side-scan sonar** surveys in the Gulf of Khambhat (formerly known as the Gulf of Cambay), off the coast of **Gujarat**, had detected geometric patterns on the seabed at depths of twenty to forty meters. The patterns, NIOT claimed, were consistent with the foundations of a large settlement. A subsequent dredging operation recovered artifacts including pottery fragments, stone tools, beads, and a piece of wood. The wood was radiocarbon-dated to approximately 9,500 years before present.

The announcement was sensational. A 9,500-year-old city would predate the Indus Valley Civilization by over four thousand years and would push the origin of urban settlement in South Asia back into the early Holocene, a period when the mainstream chronology places only scattered hunter-gatherer communities in the subcontinent. The Indian government treated the finding as a matter of national prestige. News coverage was extensive. **S.R. Rao**, a pioneer of Indian marine archaeology who had spent decades documenting the submerged remains of ancient Dwarka further south on the Gujarat coast, was cited as supporting authority, though Rao's own work at Dwarka dated to a far more recent period (roughly the second millennium BCE) and involved a completely different methodology.

The mainstream archaeological community did not accept the findings, and the reasons were methodological rather than ideological. The primary objection was devastating in its simplicity: the artifacts had been recovered by dredging, not by controlled excavation. A dredge drags along the seabed, scooping up whatever lies in its path. It cannot establish the spatial relationship between an artifact and a structure. It cannot confirm that the pottery fragment recovered at a depth of thirty meters was resting on a floor, embedded in a wall, or simply lying in the sediment, having been washed there by the Gulf's powerful tidal currents from somewhere else entirely. A dredge is to archaeology what a vacuum cleaner is to a crime scene: it collects material, but it destroys context, and in archaeology, context is everything.

The single radiocarbon date of 9,500 BP applied to a piece of dredged wood, not to a structure. A piece of ancient wood on the seabed of a tectonically active gulf with tidal ranges of up to ten meters (among the highest in the world) and currents strong enough to rearrange the bottom sediments on every tidal cycle, recovered by a dredge that could not establish the wood's original position, is not evidence of a 9,500-year-old city. It is evidence that a piece of wood 9,500 years old was on the seabed. These are not the same statement, and the distance between them is the distance between a finding and a claim.

Geologists further noted that the "geometric patterns" detected by sonar were consistent with natural sediment formations in a highly active tidal environment. The Gulf of Khambhat's extreme tidal regime creates regular patterns in bottom sediment, ridges and troughs and quasi-rectangular outlines, that can resemble structural foundations on a sonar screen. Sonar sees shapes. It does not see walls.

The Gulf of Khambhat case illustrates a problem that recurs throughout the underwater-archaeology chapter of the alternative-history literature, a problem that can be stated in a single sentence: sonar is not archaeology. The sea floor is vast, poorly surveyed, and inherently difficult to investigate with the precision that archaeological claims require. Side-scan sonar can detect geometric anomalies. It cannot distinguish a natural formation from a structural one without ground-truthing: sending divers or remotely operated vehicles to the precise location of the anomaly, documenting the context in situ, photographing the features from multiple angles, and recovering artifacts (if any) with their spatial relationships intact. Ground-truthing is expensive, logistically demanding, and has been performed at essentially none of the sites that appear in the popular literature as "underwater cities." What the popular literature presents as a map of submerged civilizations is, in most cases, a map of unverified sonar anomalies, which is a very different thing.

The institutional response to the Gulf of Khambhat announcement was itself revealing. The Indian government, eager to extend the antiquity of Indian civilization, funded the dredging operation and promoted the results before any peer review had occurred. The **Archaeological Survey of India** was not involved in the initial investigation. The NIOT, an ocean-technology institute with no archaeological expertise on staff, conducted the survey and the artifact recovery. The methodological objections came later, from archaeologists who had not been consulted, and they were treated by the press and the government as obstruction rather than as quality control. This pattern, the announcement-before-review, the political investment in the result, the dismissal of methodological criticism as institutional gatekeeping, is the mirror image of the same pattern found in the alternative community. The mechanism differs (government prestige versus lost-civilization narrative), but the epistemic error is identical: the conclusion preceded the evidence, and the evidence was then curated to fit.

### The Pavlopetri Exception

The irony of the underwater-ruins debate is that there is a genuine, fully documented, professionally excavated submerged city in the Mediterranean, and it has almost no presence in the alternative literature.

**Pavlopetri**, off the southern coast of **Laconia** in Greece, was discovered in 1967 by **Nicholas Flemming** of the **Institute of Oceanography** and systematically surveyed by a team from the **University of Nottingham** beginning in 2009. It lies in three to four meters of water and consists of at least fifteen buildings, streets, tombs, and what appear to be garden enclosures, spanning an area of roughly 50,000 square meters. The settlement dates to the **Bronze Age**, approximately 2800 BCE, and was inundated by a combination of sea-level rise and tectonic subsidence along the Laconian coast.

Pavlopetri is not mysterious. Its pottery types, building layouts, and construction techniques are consistent with known **Mycenaean** and pre-Mycenaean settlements in the region. It contributes important data to the understanding of Bronze Age urban planning, trade networks, and coastal settlement patterns, but it raises no questions about lost civilizations or anomalous timelines. The University of Nottingham team mapped the entire site using photogrammetry, producing a three-dimensional model of the settlement at centimeter resolution. They identified domestic structures, a possible administrative building, and what appeared to be a planned street grid, evidence of a level of urban organization that was not previously documented for this period in the Aegean. The pottery assemblage spanned from the early Bronze Age through the late Mycenaean period, roughly 2800 to 1200 BCE, suggesting continuous occupation for over fifteen hundred years.

This is precisely why Pavlopetri is absent from the alternative canon and precisely why it matters for this discussion: it demonstrates what a real submerged city looks like when it is investigated properly. It has walls with measurable thicknesses. It has streets that connect buildings in a recognizable layout. It has pottery embedded in the floors of rooms whose function can be inferred from their size, shape, and contents. It has tombs containing human remains with datable grave goods. It has a stratigraphy that connects it to a known culture, in a known period, using known construction techniques. It has, in short, everything that an archaeological site must have to be called a site rather than a curiosity.

Every one of these features is absent at Bimini, present in ambiguous form at Yonaguni, and unverifiable at the Gulf of Khambhat. The difference is not mysterious. It is methodological. Flemming and his successors spent decades mapping, photographing, and cataloguing every square meter of the site. The alternative sites have been visited, filmed, narrated over in baritone voiceover, and added to a catalogue of anomalies without the kind of systematic investigation that would determine whether they are anomalies at all.

### The Drowned Shelf

The factual substrate beneath the alternative claims is, paradoxically, both stronger and weaker than the claims themselves suggest.

The stronger part: sea levels at the Last Glacial Maximum, approximately 20,000 years ago, were roughly 120 meters lower than today. This is not in dispute. It is established by coral-reef dating in Barbados, by oxygen-isotope records in deep-sea sediment cores, by ice-core analysis in Greenland and Antarctica, and by the geomorphology of drowned river valleys visible in every ocean basin. The continental shelves that are now submerged were dry land. Millions of square kilometers of habitable terrain, stretching outward from every modern coastline in a broad apron of shallow seabed, were above water. Human populations lived on those shelves. They had to; some of the richest terrestrial ecosystems during the Pleistocene were in the coastal zones. Any settlements they built are now underwater, and the great majority of them will never be found, because marine conditions, currents, sedimentation, biological erosion, and the chemical dissolution of building materials, destroy architecture far more efficiently than terrestrial conditions do.

Richards, approaching this from the middle ground, pointed out what both sides of the debate tend to ignore. The gradualists, he noted, were too quick to dismiss the possibility that anything significant lay beneath the waves. The alternative researchers were too quick to claim that every anomalous sonar return was a city. The data from Barbados coral records and the **meltwater pulse** chronology established that post-glacial sea-level rise was not uniform. It came in pulses, including **Meltwater Pulse 1A** around 14,600 years ago (a rise of 16 to 25 meters over several centuries, or 40 to 60 millimeters per year) and **Meltwater Pulse 1B** beginning around 11,500 years ago (a rise of roughly 28 meters over five centuries, approximately 40 millimeters per year, though more recent studies suggest about half that rate). These were not overnight floods, as Hancock sometimes implied. They were also not the slow, imperceptible creep that the strict gradualist picture suggested. A rise of 40 to 60 millimeters per year, roughly two inches, is visible within a human lifetime. A fisherman who built his hut twenty meters from the waterline would see it lapping at his doorstep before he was an old man. Coastal communities would have experienced it as a generational crisis, not as an abstract geological process.

The weaker part: none of the specific submerged sites in the alternative literature has produced the kind of evidence that would survive a hostile peer-review committee. No artifact has been recovered in situ from a demonstrably artificial structure older than the known chronology of urban settlement. The Bimini Road is natural beachrock. The Yonaguni Monument is probably natural sandstone. The Gulf of Khambhat artifacts were dredged, not excavated. The sonar anomalies off Cuba, announced by **Paulina Zelitsky** in 2001 at depths of 600 to 750 meters (far deeper than any known submerged coastal site, and at a depth that would require a sea-level lowering not seen since the Miocene), were never followed up with the kind of systematic investigation that would confirm or refute the initial finding.

The sea floor almost certainly contains evidence of human habitation older than anything currently in the archaeological record. The specific claims in the alternative literature do not constitute that evidence. Both of these statements can be true simultaneously, and the tension between them defines the actual state of knowledge on this subject.

### The Unfound Floor

Hancock's argument in *Underworld* rested on two propositions. The first was that post-glacial sea-level rise inundated vast coastal areas where early civilizations could have developed, and that mainstream archaeology had systematically underinvested in underwater investigation of those areas. This proposition is difficult to argue with. Marine archaeology is expensive, technically challenging, dependent on specialized equipment and trained divers, and historically underfunded relative to its potential. The continental shelves of South Asia, Southeast Asia, and the western Mediterranean have been surveyed in only the most cursory fashion for archaeological purposes. The **Persian Gulf**, which was dry land during the Last Glacial Maximum and would have been one of the most fertile and habitable environments on Earth, has never been subjected to a systematic underwater archaeological survey. If pre-Holocene coastal settlements existed, the places most likely to preserve their remains are exactly the places that have been least explored.

The second proposition was that specific formations, including the Bimini Road, the Yonaguni Monument, and a series of submerged features off the coasts of India, Malta, and Taiwan, were the remnants of those civilizations. Here the argument collapsed under the weight of its own ambition. Each site, examined individually, turned out to be either demonstrably natural (Bimini), probably natural with possible minor modification (Yonaguni), or insufficiently investigated to support any conclusion at all (the Gulf of Khambhat, the Malta cart ruts, the **Penghu** underwater formation off Taiwan). The accumulation of ambiguous sites does not constitute cumulative evidence. Five question marks do not make an answer.

S.R. Rao's work at Dwarka was the closest thing to a genuine underwater archaeological success in the South Asian context, and it demonstrated both what was possible and what was required. Rao spent decades working the site, recovering artifacts with documented spatial positions, matching pottery styles to known **Harappan** and post-Harappan ceramic traditions, and building a chronology based on stratigraphy rather than sonar. The Dwarka he documented was a real settlement, underwater because tectonic subsidence and sea-level change had put it there, but it dated to a period well within the established chronology of Indian civilization, roughly the second millennium BCE. It was not evidence of a pre-diluvian superculture. It was evidence of a known culture living in a place that later sank: exactly the kind of modest, documentable, unsensational finding that real underwater archaeology produces, and exactly the kind of finding that disappears from the alternative literature because it confirms the mainstream chronology rather than overturning it.

The alternative literature wants Atlantis. The sea floor offers something less dramatic and more important: the ordinary remains of ordinary settlements in places that are no longer dry land, built by people whose lives were shaped by the same forces, weather, trade, warfare, subsistence, that shape coastal communities today. Finding them requires the kind of patient, expensive, methodologically rigorous work that produces papers, not documentaries. The Bimini Road was not a road. The Yonaguni Monument was probably not a monument. The Gulf of Khambhat has not been properly investigated. The Persian Gulf, the Sunda Shelf, the drowned river valleys of the English Channel, and the continental shelves stretching across millions of square kilometers of shallow water where no archaeologist has ever placed a trowel, remain the largest unexplored archaeological frontier on Earth.

The gap between what has been found and what could be found is enormous, and it is the gap that should define this field rather than the Bimini Road or the Yonaguni Monument.

Consider the **Doggerland** case. During the Last Glacial Maximum, the area now occupied by the southern **North Sea** was dry land, a broad, flat, marshy terrain connecting Britain to continental Europe. As sea levels rose, this territory was progressively inundated, and by roughly 6,000 BCE it had largely disappeared beneath the waves. In the decades since trawlers began dredging the North Sea floor, they have brought up thousands of artifacts: Mesolithic flint tools, animal bones, and in one celebrated case, a fragment of a **Neanderthal** skull. The **University of Bradford**'s **Doggerland Project**, launched in the early 2000s, used seismic survey data from oil companies to reconstruct the ancient landscape in detail, mapping river channels, lake basins, and coastlines beneath the modern seabed. No one has found a submerged city in Doggerland (the occupants were Mesolithic hunter-gatherers, not urban planners), but the project demonstrated what systematic marine archaeological investigation can achieve when it is funded, staffed, and methodologically rigorous.

The continental shelves of **South Asia**, the Persian Gulf, the **Sunda Shelf** of Southeast Asia, and the drowned river valleys of the western Mediterranean have not received anything comparable. These are precisely the areas where coastal settlements of the early Holocene or late Pleistocene would be expected, if they existed. The fact that they have not been found is, under these circumstances, not evidence that they did not exist. It is evidence that no one has properly looked.

The alternative literature fills this evidentiary vacuum with speculation. The mainstream fills it with silence. Neither response is adequate. The sea floor remains, by a considerable margin, the largest unexplored archaeological frontier on Earth, and the debate over underwater ruins will not be resolved by arguing about the Bimini Road. It will be resolved, if it is resolved at all, by the painstaking, expensive, unglamorous work of sending trained archaeologists to places where no archaeologist has been, with the equipment to document what they find and the institutional backing to publish the results regardless of whether those results confirm or deny anyone's preferred narrative.

The evidence is not where the alternative researchers say it is. It may well be where no one has looked.
