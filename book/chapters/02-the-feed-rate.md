## Chapter 2: The Feed Rate – Stone Cutting Technology and the Drill Core Debate
The granite cylinder is about four inches long and sits in a glass case at the **Petrie ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)) Museum of Egyptian Archaeology** at University College London. It is labeled **Core #7**. It was recovered from the **Giza ([see Chapter 6](06-the-wadi-al-jarf-papyri.md)) plateau** in 1881 by Petrie during the survey that would make him the most meticulous archaeologist of his generation. The core is a waste product: the central plug left behind when a tubular drill bores a circular hole into stone. In modern manufacturing, cores like this accumulate in bins by the hundreds and are discarded. In Petrie's time, the core from Giza was already unique, because it had been produced in red granite roughly 4,500 years ago, and it bore marks on its surface that Petrie could not explain.

Specifically, it had grooves. They spiraled around the exterior surface, or appeared to, wrapping the cylinder in what looked like the thread of a screw. Petrie measured the pitch of these grooves and calculated that the drill had been advancing into the granite at a rate of approximately one-tenth of an inch per revolution. He published this measurement in ***The Pyramids and Temples of Gizeh*** (1883), a book whose meticulous survey of the Giza plateau remains the foundational reference for the dimensions of the pyramids. The measurement of the core's groove pitch occupied only a few pages, but those pages would prove more contentious than anything else in the volume. Petrie sat with the number for a while, because the implication was troubling.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

One-tenth of an inch per revolution is not a number that belongs in a discussion of hand tools. A modern diamond-tipped core drill cutting granite in a well-equipped workshop achieves **a feed rate** orders of magnitude slower under comparable pressure. Petrie was too careful a scientist to overstate his case, but he was also too honest to ignore what he had measured. He suggested that the ancient Egyptians must have used fixed cutting points, perhaps emery or corundum crystals set into the rim of a copper tube, pressed against the stone with considerable downward force. His contemporary **A. Lucas** disagreed, proposing loose wet quartz-sand as the abrasive instead. The two positions would not be reconciled in either man's lifetime.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

They remain unreconciled today.

### The Number That Launched a Thousand Arguments

For nearly a century after Petrie, Core #7 sat in its case and attracted almost no attention. Then, in the 1990s, **Christopher Dunn**, an American manufacturing engineer with experience in the aerospace industry, discovered Petrie's measurements, verified them to his own satisfaction, and amplified the feed-rate argument into a claim that the ancient Egyptians must have used powered machinery.

Dunn's reasoning ran as follows. The spiral groove pitch on Core #7 implied a feed rate of 0.1 inches per revolution. If the drill was turning at, say, 500 RPM (a modest speed for a machine tool), then the drill was advancing into the granite at 50 inches per minute. This is, by any standard of modern stoneworking, an astonishing rate. Dunn's figure of "500 times faster" than what modern drills achieve became a staple of alternative-history presentations, repeated by **Ben van Kerkwyk** ([see Chapter 3](03-the-second-builder.md)) on the **Joe Rogan Experience** podcast, by speakers at the Cosmic Summit conferences, and by dozens of secondary commentators.<sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

The number was electrifying. It was also, as the mainstream response would eventually demonstrate, built on an assumption that Petrie himself had not made: the rotational speed. Petrie measured the pitch. He did not, and could not, measure the RPM of a drill that had stopped turning four and a half millennia ago. The feed rate per revolution (pitch) and the feed rate per minute (pitch times RPM) are entirely different quantities. The first is geometric. The second requires a clock.<sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

This distinction, obvious to any machinist, took decades to enter the popular debate.<sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

### The Antropogenez Replication

In Russia, the response came from a direction that neither Dunn nor his critics expected.

**Nikolai Vasyutin** (Николай Васютин), working with the Antropogenez team, conducted a series of experiments drilling granite with a copper tube and quartz-sand abrasive, replicating the conditions that mainstream archaeology attributes to the ancient Egyptians. The experiment was documented in detail and published as both a video and an article by **Oleg Kruglyakov** (Олег Кругляков), who compared the results directly to Core #7.

The comparison was devastating to the alternative position. Vasyutin's modern experimental core, produced with nothing more than a copper tube, sand, and human-powered rotation, exhibited grooves visually indistinguishable from those on Core #7. When Kruglyakov published an unwrapped photograph of Vasyutin's core alongside an unwrapped photograph of Core #7, the images were so similar that, as Antropogenez drily noted, even Ben van Kerkwyk, who had shown the experimental images on the Rogan podcast believing them to be Petrie's original core, could not tell the difference.<sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

The implication was pointed. If an advocate of the "lost high technology" position could not distinguish between the ancient core and a modern replication made with copper and sand, then his repeated claim that "the tool marks produced by mainstream archaeology look nothing like what we see in the ancient examples" was, in Antropogenez's assessment, "simply a bluff."

The experiment also addressed the feed-rate argument directly. The grooves on Vasyutin's core were produced not by a drill advancing at a fixed rate per revolution but by the intermittent mechanics of abrasive cutting. When hard grains of quartz or corundum are carried by a rotating copper tube against granite, the cutting is not continuous. Grains lodge, fracture, roll, and are replaced by new grains tumbling into the contact zone. The resulting marks are not true spirals in the geometric sense; they are the accumulated artifacts of many individual abrasive events, whose apparent pitch depends on downward pressure, grain size, and the rate at which fresh abrasive enters the cut. A true spiral would require the drill to advance at a precisely constant rate while rotating at a precisely constant speed. The marks on Core #7, and on Vasyutin's replication, are consistent with variable, human-applied pressure on a bow drill or pump drill, which is exactly the technology the mainstream attributes to the ancient Egyptians.

**Aleksandr Sokolov** (Александр Соколов), the founder of Antropogenez, summarized the episode with evident satisfaction during a public lecture titled "Joe Rogan, the Out-of-Place Artifact, and the Technologies of the Gods." Sokolov noted that Dunn himself had been shown the Antropogenez article and the Vasyutin core comparison years earlier, had promised to study them and respond, and had never produced a substantive answer. «Неужели вы боитесь именно того, в чём обвиняете официальных учёных?» Sokolov asked (Are you afraid of exactly the thing you accuse official scientists of?) The question was rhetorical. The answer was left to the audience.

### The Dunn Acceleration

Christopher Dunn entered the debate from an unusual direction: he was a machinist, not an archaeologist, an egyptologist, or a YouTube personality. His career had been spent in American aerospace manufacturing, where tolerances are measured in ten-thousandths of an inch and the difference between a good part and scrap is a dimension too small for the eye to see. When he encountered Petrie's measurements of Core #7, he did what machinists do: he calculated.

Dunn's book ***The Giza Power Plant*** (1998) proposed that the Great Pyramid was not a tomb but an energy-generating device, a claim that the vast majority of Egyptologists and physicists regard as unsupported. But embedded within that speculative framework was a narrower, more defensible argument about manufacturing evidence. Dunn visited Egypt, examined cores and saw marks firsthand, and confirmed Petrie's original measurements. His experience in aerospace gave him a vocabulary and a set of intuitions that Egyptologists lacked: he could look at a tool mark and infer, from its geometry, the characteristics of the tool that had made it. The kerf width of a saw cut tells you the blade thickness. The curvature of a cut surface tells you the diameter of the disc. The depth of a single-pass cut tells you the downward force multiplied by the cutting speed.

Dunn's 500-times-faster claim, which became the most cited (and most attacked) number in the entire alternative-history literature, derived from his calculation of feed rate per revolution multiplied by an assumed RPM. The assumed RPM was the problem. Dunn chose a value consistent with powered machinery because the entire thrust of his argument was that powered machinery had been used. But the mathematical operation he performed was valid; only the input was contested. If you change the RPM assumption from 500 to 50, the feed rate drops by a factor of ten. If you change it to 5, it drops by a factor of a hundred. The core's grooves are compatible with both interpretations, because grooves alone cannot tell you how fast the drill was spinning.

Richards, who discussed the feed-rate argument in multiple videos, summarized the epistemological problem with characteristic bluntness: "You can't determine the speed of a drill from the marks it left any more than you can determine the speed of a car from the tire tracks." The analogy was imperfect (tire tracks do carry information about speed, under certain conditions), but the core point was sound. The groove pitch is geometric evidence. The drill speed is a kinematic variable that leaves no direct trace.

### The Saw Marks in the Basement

Core #7 is the most famous piece of evidence in the stone-cutting debate, but it is not the most interesting. The most interesting evidence is the one that neither side discusses very much, because it is too large, too widespread, and too ambiguous to support a clean narrative.

Across Egypt, on stone blocks at dozens of sites, there are marks left by saws. Not metaphorical saws. Literal saws: straight cuts running through granite and basalt, with kerf widths and cutting profiles consistent with flat or circular blades. Petrie found them on the so-called **sarcophagus** inside the **Great Pyramid** itself, and concluded that it had been cut from a granite block by straight saws approximately three meters long. He wrote: "He not blunted or hesitated; the workmen did not hesitate to saw through a piece of granite."

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)) documented these saw marks extensively during his filming expeditions. On the basalt paving blocks beside the Great Pyramid, in the area now attributed to a **Valley Temple**, German archaeologists conducting restoration work in the early twentieth century had arranged the blocks so that the saw marks on their edges faced outward, deliberately placing the evidence where visitors could see it. Sklyarov noted the marks on a black basalt sarcophagus at Saqqara, attributed to the **Sixth Dynasty** pharaoh **Teti** (approximately 2320 BCE), where the curvature of the cuts was unmistakable: a rotating disc, not a flat blade. «Кривизна этих следов не оставляет сомнений в том, что использовался именно диск» (The curvature of these marks leaves no doubt that what was used was specifically a disc), he wrote in ***Древние боги: кто они*** (Ancient Gods: Who Are They).<sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

The cuts were also, he observed, conspicuously careless. The disc had been driven into the basalt at varying depths along a single pass, leaving an uneven kerf. The entire sarcophagus showed rough, irregular walls, as though the operators had been hacking off chunks with an angle grinder and did not care about the finish. «Возникает впечатление, что изготовители саркофага просто отхватывали болгаркой кусок за куском от базальтового блока так, как заблагорассудится» (One gets the impression that the makers of the sarcophagus were simply chopping off piece after piece from the basalt block with an angle grinder, as the mood took them). This is not the language of reverence. The operators had access to a tool that could cut black basalt in a single pass to a depth of ten to fifteen centimeters, a depth that a modern angle grinder with a diamond-tipped disc achieves at roughly one millimeter per pass, or a hundred times less.

And then there is the question of where the saws went. Sklyarov pressed this point with a logic that the mainstream has not formally addressed. The surface of the earth preserves ancient stone structures containing hundreds of millions of tons of worked stone. The tools that cut this stone left marks. The marks indicate power tools. But the tools themselves have never been found. «Следы инструментов есть, а следов их создания нет» (The traces of instruments exist, but traces of their creation do not), Sklyarov wrote, pointing toward the same conclusion about the colossal two-level technology gap he documented in Egypt and repeated in his analysis of Mesoamerican sites. His own explanation for the missing manufacturing base is a speculation so extravagant that it requires more evidence than has been adduced. But the observation that precedes it, that Egypt contains tool marks without tools, is a factual statement.

### The Inca Contradiction

The stone-cutting evidence is not confined to Egypt, and the non-Egyptian examples are, in some ways, more troubling than the Egyptian ones, because they occur in cultures with even less documented metalworking capability.

At **Ollantaytambo** in Peru, Sklyarov's team found a small horizontal platform cut into a nearly vertical granite cliff face, several meters above the valley floor. The platform itself was unremarkable; what drew Sklyarov's attention was its surface. Cut into the granite was a grid pattern, a series of intersecting straight grooves forming a regular crosshatch. The grooves were clean, even, and each appeared to have been made in a single pass. In some places, each line was a double cut, two parallel grooves separated by a sliver of uncut stone, consistent with a tool carrying two blades or a single blade making two adjacent passes.

Sklyarov recognized the pattern immediately. On the steps of Moscow metro stations, in underground passages where wet granite makes footing treacherous, maintenance crews cut identical crosshatch patterns into the stone using angle grinders with diamond-tipped discs. The Ollantaytambo grid served the same evident purpose: traction on wet stone. But the Inca had no angle grinders, no diamond discs, no powered tools of any kind. The Spanish conquistadors, who documented Inca technology with reasonable thoroughness, never described anything resembling a saw or a grinding tool. «Пил у индейцев не было» (The Indians had no saws), Sklyarov noted flatly.

And at **Sacsayhuaman**, also in Peru, his team photographed what he described as traces of a disc-cutting tool on the surface of a granite rock face. The marks showed the characteristic curvature of a rotating disc: an arc, not a straight line, with a uniform kerf width that indicated a tool of consistent thickness. The depth of the cut suggested that the disc had penetrated ten to fifteen centimeters of granite in a single pass, a feat that, as established in the Egyptian context, exceeds by roughly a hundred times the per-pass depth achievable with a modern diamond-tipped angle grinder on the same material.

### The Obsidian Rings of Anatolia

The stone-cutting evidence is not confined to Egypt and the Americas. Ben van Kerkwyk drew attention to a series of **obsidian** artifacts from **Turkey**, dated to the Neolithic period (approximately 9000 to 7000 BCE), that present a different version of the same puzzle.

Obsidian is volcanic glass. It is typically worked by **knapping**: striking the material to create controlled fractures that produce sharp flakes and blades. This is the technique that made obsidian valuable to pre-agricultural societies across the Americas and the Near East. Obsidian can be fractured to a molecular edge sharper than a surgical scalpel. It cannot, by any known knapping method, be turned into a smooth, round ring with a uniform cross-section and a polished interior surface.

Yet such rings exist. They date to the same period as the earliest monumental architecture in the region, overlapping with the construction horizon of **Gobekli Tepe**. Their surfaces show no facets from striking, no ripple marks from percussion, and no irregularities consistent with hand-grinding against another stone. They appear to have been cut from a block of obsidian and finished on a lathe, which is a sentence that should not be writable about Neolithic Anatolia.

The mainstream has not published a detailed manufacturing study of these rings. They sit in museum collections, catalogued by period and provenance, without an accompanying explanation of how they were produced. The silence is not evidence of conspiracy. It is evidence that the question has not been asked in a venue where it would be answered.

### The Pointing Machine

Richards approached the stone-cutting question from a direction that most commentators had missed entirely, focusing not on the tool marks themselves but on the measurement infrastructure that would have been required to produce the objects bearing them.<sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-2)</sup>

His analysis began with a different kind of stone: the precisely fitted **polygonal masonry** found at sites from **Cusco** to **Giza**. How, Richards asked, do you measure a twenty-ton stone with twelve faces, each of which must mate perfectly with its neighbor, when your measuring instruments consist of string, straight sticks, and your own eyeballs?

The answer, he argued, was the **pointing machine**. In ancient Rome, Greek statues were in such demand that when the supply of originals ran out, Romans began producing reproductions. This required a method of transferring precise three-dimensional measurements from one stone to another. The solution was a framework of fixed reference points from which measurements could be taken to any surface on the original, then transferred to the copy. The technique was refined during the **Renaissance** into a specialized device, the pointing machine, which remained in use by sculptors until the twentieth century.

Richards proposed that the stone **nubs** found on megalithic blocks at sites worldwide, those small, seemingly purposeless protrusions that are usually removed when a block is finished but sometimes left in place on unfinished specimens, were not decorative, structural, or religious. They were measurement reference points: fixed locations on the surface of a stone from which a pointing machine or equivalent framework could take precise measurements of all other surfaces. The nubs at precisely joined masonry sites like Sacsayhuaman and the temples of Egypt served the same function as the fixed reference pegs on a Renaissance pointing machine: they allowed the craftsman to translate a measured shape from one context to another with repeatable accuracy.

This hypothesis does not resolve the tool-marks question. A pointing machine tells you where to cut, not what to cut with. But it addresses a piece of the puzzle that the alternative and mainstream positions both tend to ignore: the question of how precision was verified. Modern CNC machining requires both a cutting tool and a measurement system. The cutting tool is what contacts the material. The measurement system is what tells the cutting tool where to go. The absence of any discussion of ancient measurement systems in the stone-cutting debate is, in Richards's view, a gap that both sides share.

### The Sarcophagus at Teti

A separate class of evidence links the drill-core debate to the larger question of stone-cutting technology in Egypt, and it comes not from cores but from the objects the drills were used to produce.

At Saqqara, in the pyramid attributed to the Sixth Dynasty pharaoh Teti (approximately 2320 BCE), there is a sarcophagus carved from a single block of black basalt. Basalt registers 6 to 7 on the Mohs scale, comparable to granite in hardness, and its fine, uniform grain makes it more resistant to fracture under stress. Cutting basalt requires either abrasive grinding over extended periods or a tool harder than the basalt itself (diamond, corundum, or a synthetic equivalent).

Sklyarov photographed the Teti sarcophagus in detail and noted that its surfaces bore the unmistakable marks of a rotating disc, the same curved-kerf signature he had documented on the basalt paving blocks near the Great Pyramid. The marks were conspicuously disordered, as though the disc had been guided by hand, and carelessly at that.

The carelessness was, for Sklyarov, the most diagnostic feature. The disc had been driven into the basalt at varying angles and depths, leaving overlapping arcs that conveyed no concern for aesthetics. The entire sarcophagus had rough, uneven walls. Whoever made it was not producing a work of art. They were roughing out a rectangular box from a basalt block, using a powered disc cutter, and they did not care what the surface looked like. This is the behavior of someone using a powerful tool for utilitarian purposes, the way a modern construction worker uses an angle grinder: fast, effective, and ugly.

Sklyarov compared the depth of the Teti sarcophagus cuts to what a modern angle grinder with a diamond-tipped disc achieves on the same material. A modern grinder, pressed hard by an operator putting full body weight behind it, penetrates about one to one and a half millimeters per pass in basalt. The ancient marks showed single-pass penetration of ten to fifteen centimeters, roughly a hundred times deeper. Either the ancient tool was far more powerful than a modern angle grinder, or the ancient operator made a hundred passes in exactly the same line without the slightest deviation, which is mechanically implausible for a hand-guided tool.

The Teti sarcophagus sits in a pyramid securely dated to the Old Kingdom. It is not a pre-dynastic artifact of contested provenance. It is not in a private collection. It is in situ, in a pyramid, bearing the name of a known pharaoh. Whatever tool made those marks was in use during the conventional timeline of Egyptian civilization.

### The Penn Museum and the Metabunk Thread

The scholarly literature on ancient Egyptian drilling is smaller than one might expect. The principal experimental study remains that of Denys Stocks, whose work at the **University of Pennsylvania** was published in *Experiments in Egyptian Archaeology* (Routledge, 2003). Stocks demonstrated that copper tube drills with quartz-sand slurry can penetrate granite over extended periods. His measured ratio of copper consumed to depth drilled was less than 1:1, meaning the drill loses less than its own weight in copper for every unit of depth gained in stone. This makes copper drilling physically feasible at scale: the amount of copper required is large but not impossibly so.

Earlier experiments at the University of Pennsylvania, published in the Penn Museum's ***Expedition*** magazine, tested both the Petrie hypothesis (fixed emery cutting points) and the Lucas hypothesis (loose wet sand). Dry loose abrasives produced no concentric lines on the core surface. Emery or corundum used as a wet slurry did produce the telltale marks. This supports the abrasive-slurry model over the fixed-point model, though both remain plausible.

The **Metabunk** forum, in a detailed thread titled "Claim: Ancient Egyptians Could Not Work Granite Without High-Tech Diamond Tools," compiled primary references showing that quartz-sand abrasion, not metal cutting, was the primary mechanism, and that no evidence of diamond or advanced machined tooling has been found at any Egyptian drilling site. The Metabunk analysis also noted that Petrie's measurements of spiral groove pitch were estimates, not precision measurements, and that the feed rate inferred from the pitch depends entirely on assumed rotational speed, a figure Petrie did not and could not measure. The assumption that consistent spiral pitch requires constant RPM, which would imply a machine, is not supported by the experimental data: human-powered bow drills can produce consistent downward pressure over extended periods, and the apparent regularity of the grooves falls within the range of what skilled manual operation can achieve.

The mainstream position on Core #7, consolidated across the Stocks experiments, the Vasyutin replication, and the Metabunk analysis, is therefore that the feed-rate argument is an artifact of unstated assumptions. The pitch of the groove is real. The extraordinary drilling speed is not, because the speed was never measured; it was calculated from the pitch using an assumed RPM that was chosen to make the result look impressive. Change the assumed RPM from 500 to 5, and the feed rate drops from astonishing to mundane.

### The Niuserra Cut

In **Abusir**, about twenty kilometers south of Giza, stands the badly eroded pyramid complex of **Niuserra** (also spelled Nyuserre), a Fifth Dynasty pharaoh who reigned approximately 2400 BCE. On a basalt block set into a side wall of the temple passageway, Sklyarov found what he considered one of the clearest examples of disc-saw evidence in all of Egypt.

The mark was a single curved cut, running along the face of the block for roughly thirty centimeters before the operator had apparently decided the cut was deep enough and withdrawn the tool. The remaining stone showed where the block had been snapped off rather than sawed through: the cut served as a score line, a deliberate weakening of the stone at a chosen plane, after which the unwanted portion was broken away by percussion.

This is a technique that any modern stone-cutter would recognize. Score and snap is the standard method for dividing stone slabs too large for a single saw pass. The score line concentrates stress along a defined plane, ensuring that the break follows the intended path. It requires two capabilities: a tool that can score the stone to a controlled depth, and enough knowledge of the material's fracture mechanics to predict where the break will propagate.

The Abusir cut showed variable depth along its length, indicating that the disc had not maintained a constant position relative to the stone surface. Sklyarov interpreted this as evidence that the tool was handheld rather than machine-guided: a person pressing a rotating disc against a stone surface will naturally vary the pressure, producing exactly this kind of uneven kerf. The cut was not clean. It was not precise. It was, in every practical sense, the kind of sloppy, utilitarian mark that a construction worker leaves when using a powerful tool in a hurry.

The disc that made this mark would have needed to be at least forty centimeters in diameter, based on the curvature of the cut. Its cutting edge would have needed to be harder than basalt. No such disc has been found at any Egyptian site. The mainstream does not dispute the existence of the mark. It attributes it to copper saws with sand abrasive, a mechanism that can score basalt but that has not been experimentally demonstrated to achieve the observed depth in a single or even a few passes.

### The Teotihuacan Scrap

Sklyarov found something in a small museum in **Teotihuacan**, Mexico, that he considered more telling than Core #7 or the saw marks at Giza. It was a block of granite, unremarkable in appearance, bearing several saw cuts and a clearly visible trace of a tubular drill. The block was not a finished artifact. It was a scrap piece, a workpiece support, the sort of thing a modern machinist would call a fixture block.

The saw cuts and drill marks on it were, in Sklyarov's assessment, incidental: the result of a tool overshooting the material being worked and scoring the support underneath. This, in a manufacturing context, is diagnostic. When a tool overruns the workpiece and marks the fixture, it tells you the operator was working fast and did not care about the fixture's surface. It implies power tooling, because hand-operated tools do not overshoot; the operator's own muscles provide the feedback that stops the cut.

But what made the Teotihuacan find especially significant was its geographic location. The museum was in Mesoamerica. No Spanish chronicler ever recorded the use of saws by any indigenous American civilization. No archaeological excavation has recovered anything resembling a saw from any pre-Columbian context. Tubular drilling, while documented in Egypt, was entirely unknown in Mesoamerica according to the archaeological record. Yet the marks were there, on a piece of granite that was not an artifact but a discard: the least likely object to be faked, because no one fakes garbage.

### What Core #7 Actually Tells Us

The honest accounting of the stone-cutting evidence is less dramatic than either side of the debate prefers.

Core #7 exists. The grooves on it are real. The feed-rate argument derived from those grooves was built on an unstated assumption about rotational speed and has been undermined, though not definitively refuted, by the Vasyutin replication experiment. The mainstream position, that copper tubes with quartz-sand abrasive can produce grooves matching those on Core #7, is supported by experimental evidence. The alternative position, that the grooves require powered machinery, rests on the feed-rate calculation, which requires an assumption that has not been independently validated.

The saw marks across Egypt are real. They indicate tools capable of cutting granite and basalt in single passes at depths that modern handheld power tools do not achieve. The mainstream has not published a detailed experimental replication of these specific marks. The alternative position attributes them to machine tools but has not explained where those machines were manufactured, fueled, maintained, or discarded.

The absence of tools is real. Neither copper saws of the length Petrie described nor any power equipment has been recovered from any Egyptian site. The copper-and-sand model predicts that tools would be consumed in use, which partly explains the absence; but it does not explain the absence of the infrastructure needed to manufacture replacement tools at the scale the stonework implies.

The obsidian rings of Anatolia exist. They have not been studied with the rigor the stone vessels have received.

The Teotihuacan scrap exists. It has not been independently verified or subjected to materials analysis.

And Core #7 sits in its case in London, four inches of red granite wrapped in grooves that have launched a hundred thousand hours of argument. The grooves are there. What made them is a question that copper and sand can partly answer, that power tools can partly answer, and that no one has fully answered.

The drill stopped turning forty-five centuries ago. The argument about its speed has not.
