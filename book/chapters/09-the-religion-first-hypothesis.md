## Chapter 9: The Religion-First Hypothesis – Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe, and Pre-Agricultural Monuments
In the 1950s, a team of archaeologists from the University of Istanbul and the **University of Chicago** surveyed a limestone ridge in southeastern Turkey, fifteen kilometers northeast of the city of **Sanliurfa**. They found worked stone protruding from the hillside. The stone was too refined, the surfaces too deliberately shaped, for the period they were investigating. The team classified the site as a **Byzantine** cemetery and moved on.

Forty years later, in 1994, a German archaeologist named **Klaus Schmidt** from the **German Archaeological Institute** visited the same ridge. Schmidt was looking for Stone Age settlements, not Byzantine graves. What he found when he began to dig was neither. The worked stone was the top of a **T-shaped pillar**, nearly six meters tall and weighing an estimated ten tons. It was carved with animal reliefs of extraordinary sophistication: foxes, boars, vultures, scorpions, snakes, and what appeared to be extinct **aurochs**. The pillar was one of a pair standing at the center of an oval enclosure, ringed by smaller T-shaped pillars set into low walls. The enclosure was not a cemetery. It was not a house. It contained no pottery, no grain storage, no animal pens. The faunal remains found in the surrounding soil were exclusively from wild species. The radiocarbon dates, when they came back, placed the oldest material at approximately 9,530 BCE, plus or minus 200 years.

Schmidt would spend the next twenty years of his life at this site. He named it what the locals called it: Gobekli Tepe, the "pot-bellied hill." He died in 2014, while swimming near his home in Germany, before even fifteen percent of the site had been excavated. The sentence he is most remembered for is the one that inverted a foundational assumption of his own discipline. "First the temple," Schmidt said, "then the city."

The idea that monumental architecture could precede agriculture, that people who hunted wild gazelle and gathered wild grain could organize the labor required to quarry, transport, carve, and erect multi-ton limestone pillars, was, in 1994, close to unthinkable. The standard model held that agriculture came first: settled farming communities produced food surpluses, surpluses enabled population growth and labor specialization, specialization produced priestly classes and administrators, and only then did societies have the resources and organizational capacity to build monuments. Gobekli Tepe inverted the sequence. The monument came first. The question was what that inversion meant, and it is a question that, three decades later, has produced more answers than consensus.


### The Hill and Its Layers

The first thing that complicates the standard narrative about Gobekli Tepe is its stratigraphy. The site is not a single construction. It is a palimpsest that accumulated over millennia, with at least three distinct cultural layers identified by Schmidt.

**Layer III**, the deepest and oldest, dates to the mid-tenth through late ninth millennium BCE, the **Pre-Pottery Neolithic A** period. This is where the large T-shaped pillars and the circular enclosures are found. The excavated area contains five major oval enclosures, labeled A through E, each defined by rings of pillars set into low stone walls, with two larger pillars standing at the center. The central pillars in some enclosures reach heights of five to six meters. Ground-penetrating radar surveys of the broader hillside suggest that the excavated area represents a small fraction of the total site; the unexcavated portion may contain dozens more enclosures.

**Layer II**, dating to the late ninth through early eighth millennium BCE (**Pre-Pottery Neolithic B**), shows smaller rectangular rooms with smaller pillars. The architectural ambition appears to diminish over time, not increase. The earliest phases of construction are the most monumental. This is the opposite of what the standard developmental model predicts.

**Layer I** is the surface deposit, a thick accumulation of soil, rock, and debris that covered the entire site. For decades, the nature of this layer was the subject of debate. Schmidt believed the site had been intentionally buried, that the builders or their successors had deliberately filled in the enclosures with rubble and soil. This view gained support from the discovery that Karahan Tepe, a nearby and potentially older site, was also identified by its excavators as intentionally buried. More recent analysis, as discussed by **Ben van Kerkwijk** (UnchartedX) at the 2025 Cosmic Summit classroom session, suggests the debate is shifting: some mainstream archaeologists now argue that the burial is more consistent with **midden**, the gradual accumulation of occupational debris (bones, broken pottery, discarded stone, organic waste) over thousands of years. Van Kerkwijk noted that sites like Elephantine Island in Egypt show ten to fifteen meters of midden accumulation, and that the material filling Gobekli Tepe's enclosures is consistent with this process.

The dating question matters. The radiocarbon dates for Gobekli Tepe come from organic material found within the cobblestone walls that surround the T-shaped pillars. But van Kerkwijk and others have pointed out that these cobblestone walls are themselves rebuilds: fragments of broken T-pillars have been found embedded in the walls, indicating that the pillars were already damaged or fallen when the cobblestone walls were constructed. The radiocarbon dates, therefore, may date the reconstruction of the site rather than its original construction. If the Layer III enclosures are older than the material in their rebuilt walls, then the earliest phase of Gobekli Tepe extends into the **Younger Dryas** period (roughly 12,800 to 11,600 years ago), and possibly into the late **Paleolithic**.

**Martin Sweatman**, a chemical engineer at the **University of Edinburgh** who has published extensively on Gobekli Tepe's astronomical symbolism, makes precisely this argument. He notes that the oldest radiocarbon dates from **Enclosure D**, around 9,530 BCE, come from samples taken near Pillar 43 in the wall and from a fire pit near the bedrock, but these locations do not correspond to the oldest construction phase of the enclosure. The oldest phase, visible in the stratigraphy as a dark-blue section at the base of the enclosure, has yielded no radiocarbon samples. Sweatman's position is that the enclosure likely originated in the Younger Dryas period, before approximately 9,600 BCE.

The site's excavators at the German Archaeological Institute prefer to describe Gobekli Tepe as a Neolithic construction, built entirely after the Younger Dryas. Both positions are extrapolations from the same data. The difference is a judgment call about what the stratigraphy implies versus what the radiocarbon dates directly demonstrate.


### The Vulture and the Scorpion

Sweatman's most controversial contribution is not the dating question but the interpretation of **Pillar 43**, one of the most densely carved stones at Gobekli Tepe. The pillar, located in the northwest wall of Enclosure D, bears a complex arrangement of animal reliefs: a vulture with outstretched wings, a scorpion, a disc held at the center of the vulture's wing, a headless human figure at the base, and several other animals including what appear to be a boar, a bird, and a fox.

In a 2017 paper that drew global media attention, Sweatman and his colleague **Dimitrios Tsikritsis** proposed that the animal symbols on Pillar 43 represent constellations similar to those in the later Greek tradition, and that the disc represents the sun's position relative to the summer solstice constellation. If the vulture corresponds to **Sagittarius** (or a precursor constellation in the same region of sky), then the pillar encodes a date, using the phenomenon of precession of the equinoxes, that corresponds to approximately 10,950 BCE, within the range of the Younger Dryas onset.

The headless human figure at the base of the pillar, in Sweatman's reading, represents death or catastrophe. The combination of a specific astronomical date and a death symbol leads to his central claim: Pillar 43 is a memorial to the **Younger Dryas impact event**, the hypothesized cosmic impact or airburst that may have triggered the Younger Dryas cooling period.

The pushback from the site's archaeologists was swift. **Jens Notroff** and colleagues at the German Archaeological Institute argued that interpreting Neolithic animal carvings as specific constellations requires projecting modern or classical constellation boundaries onto an iconographic tradition that predated those systems by eight thousand years. It is, they wrote, "highly unlikely" that Neolithic hunters in Upper Mesopotamia recognized the same celestial patterns as later Egyptian, Arabian, and Greek astronomers.

Sweatman's response to this criticism is layered. He points to the work of Russian astronomer **Alexander Gurshtein**, who predicted in the 1990s that early agriculturalists (and, by extension, complex hunter-gatherers) would have recorded world ages using four constellation symbols corresponding to the solstices and equinoxes. Gurshtein made this prediction before Gobekli Tepe was widely known, and Sweatman argues that the pattern visible on Pillar 43 confirms Gurshtein's theoretical framework. He also marshals evidence from the **Master of Animals** motif, a widespread iconographic tradition showing a central figure flanked by two animals, that appears across cultures from Gobekli Tepe through Catalhoyuk, the Indus Valley, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Classical Greece, with the flanking animals consistently corresponding to zodiacal symbols.

The statistical argument is where the debate gets technical. Sweatman's 2017 paper attempted to quantify the probability that the animal symbols on Pillar 43 would match the expected constellation pattern by pure chance. He concluded that the correlation was too strong to be coincidental. Critics argue that the assignment of animals to constellations involves enough subjective choices that a motivated researcher could find correlations in any sufficiently complex set of carvings. This is the constellation-matching problem: with enough symbols and enough constellations, pattern-matching becomes a form of projection rather than discovery.

The question remains genuinely open. Nobody has proven that Pillar 43 is an astronomical record. Nobody has proven that it is not. The carvings are there. The animals are there. What they meant to the people who made them, twelve thousand years ago, is a question that the carvings themselves cannot answer.

Sweatman has continued to develop his interpretation, extending it to other features of the site. He compared **Pillar 18**, another richly carved stone in Enclosure D, to the **Nebra Sky Disc**, the Bronze Age artifact found in Germany that is generally accepted as an astronomical object showing the sun, moon, and the **Pleiades** star cluster. The top of Pillar 18 bears a disc and a crescent; the bottom bears seven small birds. In mythology, the Pleiades are frequently represented as six or seven sisters, or six or seven birds. The parallel with the Nebra disc is suggestive, though the chronological gap of roughly eight thousand years between the two objects is the kind of distance that makes archaeologists profoundly uncomfortable.

Richards, characteristically, occupied the middle ground on the astronomical interpretation. He compared the enterprise to building the Hoover Dam star map, which was explicitly designed to encode an astronomical date for future civilizations: "a message to future generations. A message saying when this building was built using celestial alignments." Whether the builders of Gobekli Tepe had the same intention is unprovable, but the existence of modern astronomical time markers proves that the impulse to encode dates in stone is not, in itself, extraordinary. The question is whether the specific encoding Sweatman proposes is correct, or whether the statistical matching between carved animals and constellations is an artifact of the researcher's expectations.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>


### The Zero Minus Five Hundred Point

When **UNESCO** designated Gobekli Tepe a World Heritage Site, the organization described it as "the zero point in time" for monumental architecture. This description became obsolete almost immediately.

Karahan Tepe, located roughly thirty-five kilometers southeast of Gobekli Tepe, was identified through surface surveys in 1997 but not excavated until the 2010s. The site proved to be approximately five hundred years older than Gobekli Tepe, with T-shaped pillars, megalithic construction, and bedrock-carved features that, if anything, exceed the sophistication of the older site. The Brothers of the Serpent podcast researchers joked that their Gobekli Tepe merchandise, emblazoned with "First Temple," was now "scientifically inaccurate."

Karahan Tepe introduced features not found at Gobekli Tepe. The most striking is a rock-cut chamber containing a series of carved anthropomorphic pillars emerging directly from the living bedrock, with a carved human head, life-sized and three-dimensional, positioned at the far end of the chamber. **Hugh Newman** and **Andrew Collins**, archaeoastronomy researchers who have studied both sites extensively, identified an alignment between the winter solstice sunrise and a porthole stone at Karahan Tepe. On the shortest day of the year, sunlight enters the chamber through the porthole and illuminates the carved head. Newman and Collins tested this alignment with the collaboration of 3D graphic artist **Kevin Eslinger**, who reconstructed the site digitally using the software **Stellarium** set to 9,400 BCE. The alignment held even when hypothetical roof structures were added to the model, though the roof question itself (whether these enclosures were originally roofed or open to the sky) remains unresolved.

The discoveries did not stop at Karahan Tepe. Between 2018 and 2025, researchers identified as many as forty additional sites in the region displaying T-shaped pillars, megalithic construction, or both. The cluster has been tentatively designated the **Tas Tepeler** civilization (from the Turkish for "stone hills"). **Tatyana Kornienko**, an archaeologist who has studied the Gobekli Tepe cultural layer in detail, described the site as sitting on a limestone plateau above the **Harran Plain**, with the artificial mounds (the "tel" or "tepe") reaching heights of fifteen meters. The megalithic enclosures were found not at the tops of these mounds but in the depressions between them, a detail that complicates simplistic narratives about the site's function.

What the Tas Tepeler cluster demonstrates is that Gobekli Tepe was not an isolated anomaly. It was part of a regional tradition of monumental construction by pre-agricultural communities that persisted for at least two thousand years and covered a geographic area of several hundred square kilometers. This tradition involved quarrying limestone pillars from bedrock (a technique analogous to how the Great Sphinx of Giza ([Chapter 6](06-the-wadi-al-jarf-papyri.md)) was carved: cutting the surrounding rock away to leave the pillar standing), transporting them to construction sites, carving them with animal reliefs of considerable artistic skill, and erecting them in purposeful architectural arrangements.

The people who did this hunted wild boar and wild gazelle. They gathered wild grain. They did not plant crops or domesticate animals. They had no pottery. They had no metal tools. They built monuments that, as one archaeologist admitted off the record to a Cosmic Summit researcher, would be estimated at 600 to 1,000 BCE if found in isolation, based on their sophistication alone.

The construction technique itself deserves attention. At both Gobekli Tepe and Karahan Tepe, the builders cut the bedrock down to a flat surface, leaving protruding pedestals carved from the living rock, into which the T-pillars were socketed. This is the same technique, scaled differently, used to create the Great Sphinx of Giza: carve away the surrounding stone and leave the desired form standing. Van Kerkwijk drew the parallel explicitly. The enclosures at Gobekli Tepe are not assembled from transported blocks (though some pillars were quarried and moved short distances). The foundational structures are carved in place, which requires a fundamentally different approach to construction, one that demands careful planning before the first chip of stone is removed, because mistakes in subtractive carving cannot be corrected by adding material.

The T-pillars themselves appear to represent stylized human figures. The "T" shape corresponds to a head (the horizontal top) on shoulders (the vertical shaft). Some pillars bear carved arms, hands, belts, and loincloths, confirming the anthropomorphic interpretation. The central pillars in the enclosures are the largest and most elaborately carved. They face each other across the enclosed space, their "faces" (if the T-shape is a head) looking at the ring of smaller pillars in the walls. The arrangement has the spatial grammar of a gathering: two presiding figures, surrounded by a circle of attendants or witnesses. Whether this is a council, a ritual, or something else entirely is unknown. But the architectural intentionality is unmistakable.


### What Changed and What Didn't

The mainstream archaeological response to Gobekli Tepe has evolved considerably since 1994. The initial reaction was incredulity: pre-agricultural monumental construction contradicted the **Neolithic Revolution** model that had organized archaeological thinking for a century. By the 2010s, the mainstream had adapted. Schmidt's aphorism, "first the temple, then the city," was incorporated into a revised model in which ritual activity and communal monument-building are now understood to have driven social complexity, which in turn produced the conditions for agricultural adoption. This is the **religion-first hypothesis**: the construction of sacred sites required large-scale cooperation, which required reliable food supplies, which incentivized the domestication of wild grains and animals. The temple did not follow the farm. The farm followed the temple.

This revised model is itself now considered mainstream. It is taught in university archaeology courses. It appears in textbooks. It is, in a real sense, the single most important revision to the human timeline that Gobekli Tepe produced.

What the mainstream has not accepted is the further claim, advanced most visibly by Hancock ([see Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)), that Gobekli Tepe was built under the direction of survivors from a lost advanced civilization that predated the Younger Dryas. The German Archaeological Institute's position is unambiguous: the site was built by Neolithic hunter-gatherers using stone tools and cooperative labor. The "lost civilization" hypothesis is rejected because it requires positing a civilization with no physical trace anywhere on Earth: no cities, no distinct artifact assemblages, no writing, no metals. As the institute's researchers have noted, you cannot invoke an entity whose existence generates no testable predictions and leaves no recoverable evidence; that is not a hypothesis but an unfalsifiable assertion.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

Richards, who has investigated Gobekli Tepe from a position sympathetic to the alternative community but skeptical of its more expansive claims, occupies a middle position. He argued that the mainstream's acceptance of "first the temple, then the city" has not fully reckoned with its own implications. If hunter-gatherers could organize the labor, logistics, and specialized skill required to build Gobekli Tepe without agriculture, then they could plausibly have developed other capabilities that the previous model denied them: navigation, long-distance trade, astronomical observation, and construction techniques that do not require the infrastructure of a settled agricultural society. The alternative community, Richards argued, is not wrong to look at Gobekli Tepe and conclude that pre-agricultural humans were more capable than twentieth-century archaeology assumed. Where the alternative community goes wrong is in insisting that those capabilities must have been taught by someone else, some vanished civilization, rather than developed independently by the populations who left the evidence behind.<sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

Richards used an analogy from his own video series to make the point concrete. The fact that Gobekli Tepe exists means that "all of a sudden you guys are saying that we don't need agriculture in order to develop low-level technology. Not just megalithism. If these guys can figure out how to stack all these rocks and build enclosures and do all this without just decimating their population, they could probably figure out how to do other things, like build a boat." The concession that pre-agricultural societies could build monuments implies a broader revision to the capabilities attributed to hunter-gatherers, one that the mainstream has made selectively (accepting monument-building) while resisting the downstream implications (navigation, long-distance contact, astronomical record-keeping). Each capability, when proposed in isolation, can be accommodated by the revised model. The cumulative picture is harder to reconcile with the assumption that these were cognitively simple people leading cognitively simple lives.<sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

The Brothers of the Serpent, **Kyle and Russ Allen**, construction professionals who analyze ancient sites from a builder's perspective, drew attention to a detail at Gobekli Tepe that most commentators overlook: the layering. The cobblestone walls that surround the T-pillars are built over and around broken pillar fragments. "These T-pillars were already broken when somebody came along and started to build these cobblestone walls," van Kerkwijk confirmed. This means that the monumental phase (Layer III, with the large pillars) was followed by a phase of destruction or decay, then a reconstruction phase using different, simpler techniques. The reconstruction workers inherited the ruins and did what they could. "They were doing this cobblestone wall work to rebuild the site," van Kerkwijk explained. The implication is that the sophistication decreased over time, which is the opposite of what the standard developmental model predicts but entirely consistent with a pattern visible at sites across the ancient world: a high phase of construction followed by later inhabitants who maintain or rebuild with inferior techniques.<sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

The Cosmic Summit researchers made a related point. When archaeologists found Gobekli Tepe, the articles asked "First temple?" with a question mark. Then the question mark disappeared. Then Karahan Tepe was found, and the label "zero point" was quietly retired. The pattern is consistent: each new discovery pushes the timeline back and expands the catalog of what hunter-gatherers were capable of. The mainstream absorbs each revision individually but resists the cumulative implication, which is that the standard narrative about the emergence of complex human societies has been wrong in its details more often than it has been right.<sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>


### The Feast That Built the Temple

One of the most underappreciated findings at Gobekli Tepe is the faunal evidence. The bones found at the site come overwhelmingly from wild species, particularly **gazelle** and aurochs. This is the primary evidence that the builders were hunter-gatherers. But the quantity of animal bone is enormous, far exceeding what a small resident population would produce. The emerging interpretation, supported by the ethnographic data that Sweatman cited on complex hunter-gatherer societies, is that Gobekli Tepe functioned as a periodic gathering site, a place where dispersed hunter-gatherer bands came together for ritual feasting events, possibly timed to the solstices or equinoxes.<sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

This model has direct ethnographic parallels. **Brian Hayden** and **Suzanne Villeneuve** published a 2011 paper surveying relatively modern hunter-gatherer societies and found that most such groups were interested in astronomy, that more complex groups tracked the solstices and equinoxes, and that communal gatherings were frequently organized around these astronomical dates. If modern hunter-gatherers do this, the argument goes, then ancient hunter-gatherers almost certainly did as well. Sweatman cited Hayden and Villeneuve's ethnographic survey explicitly in his arguments about Gobekli Tepe: "most hunter-gatherer tribes, relatively modern hunter-gatherer tribes, were interested in astronomy. They had names of stars and they had constellations. But more than that, the more complex hunter-gatherers actually tracked the solstices and equinoxes and had communal gatherings on those related dates."<sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

The site's location supports the astronomical interpretation. Gobekli Tepe sits on a hilltop with commanding views to the south over the Harran Plain, an ideal position for naked-eye astronomy. Sweatman described the view from the enclosures as "fantastic views over the landscape to the south, great for naked-eye astronomy." This is not a site chosen for its proximity to water, game, or arable land (it has none of these in abundance). It is a site chosen for its elevation and its sightlines. If the builders were not interested in the sky, their choice of location makes very little practical sense.<sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

The labor required to build Gobekli Tepe's enclosures has been estimated at hundreds of person-hours per pillar, which translates to dozens of workers over weeks or months. A seasonal gathering of multiple bands, timed to a solstice feast, could have provided the necessary labor pool. The feasting itself, the slaughter of wild animals in quantities sufficient to feed hundreds of people, would leave exactly the kind of faunal debris found in the site's midden layers. In this reading, the monument and the feast are inseparable: the gathering enabled the construction, and the construction justified the gathering.<sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

This is the religion-first hypothesis in its most concrete form. Not religion as theology, but religion as social technology: the ritual event that draws scattered populations together, creates shared identity, and mobilizes collective labor for projects that no single band could accomplish alone. The temple is not the product of civilization. The temple is the mechanism by which civilization produced itself.<sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

There is an additional dimension to this model that Schmidt himself identified but that has received less attention than it deserves. The site's location, at the northern edge of the **Fertile Crescent**, places it precisely in the zone where the wild ancestors of domesticated wheat and barley grow naturally. The gathering of large groups for monument-building and ritual feasting would have placed intense pressure on local wild grain resources. If hundreds of people converged on a hilltop for weeks at a time, the surrounding landscape's carrying capacity for wild grain would have been strained. The logical response, over generations, would have been to begin managing those grain stands: protecting them from competing species, selecting for desirable traits, and eventually planting them deliberately. In this model, agriculture did not enable monument-building. Monument-building, by concentrating population beyond what wild resources could sustainably support, created the selective pressure that drove the adoption of agriculture.<sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

This is a hypothesis, not an established fact. But it is consistent with the archaeological evidence from the broader region. The earliest domesticated grain species appear in the archaeological record within a few hundred kilometers of Gobekli Tepe, within a millennium or two of the site's construction period. The geography and the chronology align. The question is whether the alignment is causal or coincidental, and the answer to that question lies in the ninety percent of Gobekli Tepe that remains underground.<sup>[16](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup><sup>[17](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>


### What the Hillside Holds

Gobekli Tepe is twelve thousand years old. This is not in dispute. It was built by people who did not practice agriculture. This is not in dispute. It represents a level of social organization, technical skill, and artistic achievement that the previous model of human development did not predict. This is not in dispute.<sup>[18](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

What is in dispute is everything else. Whether the site's animal carvings encode astronomical knowledge. Whether the oldest enclosures date to the Younger Dryas period or slightly after it. Whether the site was intentionally buried or gradually covered by midden. Whether the Tas Tepeler cluster represents a coherent "civilization" or a looser cultural tradition. Whether the existence of Gobekli Tepe implies the existence of a predecessor culture that taught the builders what to do, or whether it demonstrates that no such predecessor is needed.<sup>[19](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

The German Archaeological Institute continues its work at Gobekli Tepe, slowly and methodically. Eighty-five to ninety percent of the site remains unexcavated. Karahan Tepe's excavation is in its early phases; van Kerkwijk noted that construction crews digging footings for a tourist roof structure at Karahan Tepe had to drill through thirty feet of accumulated material before hitting bedrock, with stone artifacts and cultural debris visible throughout the entire depth. The other sites in the Tas Tepeler cluster are largely unexamined. Every major excavation season since 1994 has produced findings that required revisions to the previous year's conclusions. There is no reason to expect this pattern to stop.<sup>[20](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup><sup>[21](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

The discovery of **Urfa Man** reinforces the point. This life-sized stone statue, the oldest known full-sized human sculpture in the world, was found during construction work in the city of Sanliurfa, a thirty-minute drive from Gobekli Tepe. The statue dates to the same period as the Gobekli Tepe enclosures. Schoch ([Chapter 7](07-the-rain-that-wasnt.md)), who visited the site and discussed the findings with Schmidt personally, described Urfa Man as "maybe my favorite statue from antiquity." The statue's obsidian eyes, set into a face of deliberate simplicity, stare across twelve thousand years with an expression that resists interpretation. It was found by accident, during routine urban development, in a city that sits atop layers of archaeological deposits reaching back to the Gobekli Tepe era. The implication is straightforward: the builders of Gobekli Tepe did not live in a cultural vacuum. They inhabited a landscape saturated with their works, most of which have not been found and many of which have been built over by the modern city.<sup>[22](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-9)</sup>

The roof question, which might seem trivial, is in fact central to the astronomical interpretation. Newman and Collins have argued that the main enclosures at Gobekli Tepe and Karahan Tepe were open to the sky, allowing direct celestial observation. The site's archaeologists counter that roofing structures would have been necessary, though they acknowledge that no physical evidence for roofing materials has been found in the main enclosures. Newman noted that "there is literally zero evidence for any roofs on the main enclosures, any of these sites," and expressed concern that the construction of a modern tourist roof over Karahan Tepe's pillar shrine could permanently block the winter solstice alignment before it is fully documented. The archaeologists' insistence on roofing, Newman suggested, is motivated in part by discomfort with the astronomical alignments that open-sky architecture implies, because "they don't like the fact we keep finding these astronomical alignments that they didn't discover."

Whether this is fair to the archaeologists or not, the alignment at Karahan Tepe is difficult to dismiss. On the winter solstice, sunlight passes through a porthole stone, traverses the length of the chamber, and strikes the carved human head at the rear wall. Newman described this as "the one time of the year, no other time of year" when the light "hits the face and the mouth." Sweatman extended the analysis to propose that the pillar shrine incorporates a lunisolar calendar, with the numbers of pillars and other structural elements encoding the periods of the lunar and solar cycles. Similar calendrical structures have been proposed for Stonehenge, which is roughly five thousand years younger than Karahan Tepe.

If Gobekli Tepe and Karahan Tepe encode astronomical knowledge, and if that knowledge includes an awareness of precession of the equinoxes, then the builders possessed a scientific tradition far more sophisticated than the label "hunter-gatherer" typically implies. This is Sweatman's strongest argument and also his most speculative: that the animal symbols at Gobekli Tepe represent a system of astronomical record-keeping that persisted, through cultural transmission, from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic through the Sumerian, Babylonian, Hittite, and ultimately Greek traditions. The chain of evidence he marshals, from the Master of Animals motif through Catalhoyuk to the Nebra Sky Disc to classical constellation maps, is long and suggestive. Whether it is also airtight is a question that may take another generation of excavation to resolve.

Schmidt's sentence still stands. First the temple, then the city. What he did not live to see is how many more temples there were.
