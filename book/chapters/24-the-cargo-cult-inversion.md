## Chapter 24: The Cargo Cult Inversion – Sklyarov's Extraterrestrial Civilization Theory
On a cluster of islands in the southwestern Pacific, sometime in the mid-1940s, the war ended and the Americans went home. They left behind concrete landing strips, empty Quonset huts, and a population of Melanesian islanders who had spent the previous three years watching pale-skinned strangers perform a ritual that, from a Stone Age perspective, looked exactly like magic. The strangers did not carve bows, did not plant sweet potatoes, did not weave baskets. They stood in straight lines and marched while their chiefs shouted incomprehensible words. They laid out mysterious flat surfaces in the jungle, strung "ropes" between poles, clamped devices to their ears, and screamed into metal boxes. Then they pointed lights at the sky and waved flags, and iron birds descended from the clouds bearing boxes filled with unimaginable riches: steel knives, tinned jam, bottled liquor, clothing, boots, flashlights. The strangers called these boxes "cargo."

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)), in ***Предметы богов и их копии*** (Objects of the Gods and Their Copies), described what the Americans left in the islanders' minds with the precision of an engineer cataloguing component failure. The pale-skinned visitors, from the islanders' perspective, did not work. They did not carve wood into bows and arrows, did not plant yams, did not grind grain in mortars, did not weave baskets. Instead they performed mysterious actions: they set up poles connected by ropes, put devices on their ears, and shouted strange words into metal boxes. Then the iron birds came, and the boxes of cargo fell from the sky. Part of the cargo sometimes reached the islanders, in exchange for services as guides and for coconuts, shells, and the favor of young women. The islanders were astonished. They prayed to their own gods by every method they knew, but their gods never sent them anything this good. Either they were praying wrong, or they were praying to the wrong gods.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup>

When the anthropologists arrived, a few years after the military had departed, they found something that had never been documented before. The islanders had built landing strips of packed earth. They had erected bamboo control towers strung with hemp "wires." They had constructed life-sized airplane models from bamboo and woven mats, accurate enough in silhouette to fool a squinting observer at a distance. Their bodies were painted to resemble military uniforms, complete with the letters USA and badges representing medals. They sat in coconut-shell headphones and shouted into bamboo microphones. And they waited for the sky to open.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup>

On the island of **Tanna** in **Vanuatu**, one variant became the religion of **John Frum**. Sklyarov explained the origin: «Кто-то из американцев подшутил над местными островитянами и сказал: "Зачем вам трудиться? Скоро прилетит Джон из Америки (... John from America) и все вам привезет так!"» (One of the Americans joked with the local islanders and said: "Why do you bother working? John from America will come soon and bring everything you need!") The phrase "John from" was garbled into "John Frum." The islanders believed it literally. John Frum became a messiah. His prophesied return date is February 15, year unknown. The priests of his cult claim to communicate with him "by radio" through a woman wrapped in wires who enters a trance and speaks in tongues, which the priest then interprets.<sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup>

The cargo cults were not unique to the Second World War. The earliest documented instance was the **Tuka movement**, which appeared in **Fiji** in 1885. But the wartime examples were the most vivid, and for Sklyarov, the most analytically useful. Because the cargo cults happened in the twentieth century, within documented history, every element of the cognitive sequence was observable: the encounter with superior technology, the misinterpretation of that technology as supernatural, the departure of the visitors, the attempt to reproduce the technology using local materials, the ritualization of the reproduction, and the gradual transformation of the ritual into religion.

Sklyarov did not describe any of this because he was interested in Melanesia. He described it because he intended to apply it in reverse.


### The Inversion

Erich von Daniken, the Swiss hotelier whose 1968 book ***Chariots of the Gods?*** launched the modern ancient-astronaut genre, argued that ancient myths record literal encounters with extraterrestrial visitors. His work was enormously popular and almost universally dismissed by scholars. **Carl Sagan**, whose assessment remains the standard scientific position, summarized the genre's evidentiary problem: "In the long litany of 'ancient astronaut' pop archaeology, the cases of apparent interest have perfectly reasonable alternative explanations, or have been misreported, or are simple prevarications, hoaxes and distortions."

Sklyarov, who found von Daniken's work shallow and sensationalized, took the argument in a fundamentally different direction. The myths, Sklyarov proposed, were not records of encounters. They were the cargo cult itself. The stone structures, the temple rituals, the elaborate priesthoods, the objects buried in tombs: these were not evidence of alien technology. They were the bamboo airplanes. They were the human copies, made in local materials, of things the "gods" had possessed. Where the Melanesian islanders built bamboo airplanes and waited for cargo, the Egyptians built stone pyramids and waited for their pharaohs to ascend to the realm of the gods. The scale was different. The materials were different. The underlying cognitive operation, reproducing the outward form of a technology without understanding its mechanism, was identical.

This is what makes Sklyarov distinct from the rest of the ancient-astronaut literature, and what makes dismissing him more difficult than dismissing von Daniken. Sklyarov was a physicist by training, educated at the **Moscow Physical-Technical Institute** (known informally as PhysTech, the MIT of the Soviet Union), and he approached ancient sites the way an engineer approaches a failed prototype: by examining the manufacturing evidence. He organized over a dozen expeditions to Egypt, Peru, Bolivia, Mexico, Syria, Lebanon, Japan, and Easter Island, bringing film crews and measurement equipment. He published more than twenty books in Russian, none of which has been officially translated into English, and maintained a research archive at **LAH.ru** (the Laboratory of Alternative History, or Лаборатория Альтернативной Истории) until his death in 2016 at the age of fifty-five.


### The Two Layers

Sklyarov's core observation, repeated across nearly all his works, was that ancient sites worldwide exhibit what he called «колоссальная разница двух уровней технологии» (a colossal difference between two levels of technology). At the same site, on the same wall, using the same type of stone, you find blocks cut with what appears to be machine precision alongside blocks shaped with primitive hand tools. The two types of work are not separated by time in any way that the conventional chronology can explain. They are interleaved, often in the same structure, sometimes in the same course of masonry.

In *Цивилизация древних богов Египта* (Civilization of the Ancient Gods of Egypt), Sklyarov documented this two-layer pattern at Giza ([Chapter 6](06-the-wadi-al-jarf-papyri.md)), Saqqara, Abusir, and Karnak. At the temple of Teti at Saqqara, he photographed a black basalt sarcophagus bearing cut marks whose geometry, he argued, was consistent with a power-driven disc saw. The marks showed a smooth, curved trajectory and a kerf width uniform enough to suggest a rotating blade of fixed diameter. At the temple near the **Pyramid of Userkaf** at Saqqara, basalt floor blocks displayed similar saw marks. At Abusir, near the **Pyramid of Niuserre**, the marks appeared again. Sklyarov was blunt about what he saw: «Следы инструментов на разных каменных блоках указывают на колоссальную разницу двух уровней технологии, которая не может быть объяснена каким-либо "выбросом" умений египтян» (The tool marks on different stone blocks indicate a colossal gap between two levels of technology that cannot be explained by any "burst" of Egyptian skill).

At the Karnak temple complex, on a granite lintel of one of the pylons, Sklyarov found a circular depression approximately one meter in diameter. The depression was smooth-walled and geometrically regular, consistent with a core drill of a size that, he noted, was not manufactured by any civilization until the late twentieth century. In the same complex, on granite surfaces, he documented what he described as evidence of tube drilling, polishing, and surface grinding at tolerances inconsistent with manual labor using dolerite pounders, the tool conventionally attributed to Egyptian stone workers.

The same pattern, Sklyarov reported, appeared in Peru. At Ollantaytambo, at Sacsayhuaman, and throughout the **Sacred Valley**, enormous polygonal blocks fitted with sub-millimeter precision sit alongside crude dry-stone infill. The precision blocks show no tool marks. The infill blocks show abundant evidence of manual shaping with stone hammers. The mainstream explanation attributes both to the Inca. Sklyarov's counter was pointed: the Inca themselves said they did not build the precision layer. Their own oral histories, recorded by Spanish chroniclers in the sixteenth century, attributed the great stone walls to beings who predated them. The Inca built on top of what was already there. They were, in Sklyarov's framework, the second layer.

His summary was characteristic: «Следы дисковых пил есть не только в Старом Свете. Мы нашли их и в Западном полушарии в ходе своей экспедиции в Перу и Боливию» (Disc-saw marks exist not only in the Old World. We found them in the Western Hemisphere during our expedition to Peru and Bolivia). The claim is that the same type of tool left marks at sites separated by 12,000 kilometers and attributed to civilizations that, according to mainstream archaeology, had no contact with each other.


### The Egyptian Bird

In 1898, archaeologists excavating a burial complex at Saqqara recovered a small winged figurine carved from a hard wood. It measured 14.2 centimeters in length with a wingspan of 18.3 centimeters. Its nose was shaped like a beak, and an eye was painted on one side. Papyri found alongside it contained the phrase "I want to fly," and hieroglyphs on the artifact itself translated as "gift of **Amun**." The burial was dated to the third century BCE. Archaeologists catalogued the figurine under inventory number 6347, labeled the storage box "Wooden Bird Figures," and sent it to the basement of the Cairo Museum, where it was displayed in Room 22 and ignored by visitors for seven decades.

In 1969, the same year Santillana and von Dechend published *Hamlet's Mill* and the islanders of Tanna were still waiting for John Frum, a physician named **Khalil Messiha**, who held a secondary degree in archaeology and was devoted to the study of Egyptian antiquities, noticed something about exhibit 6347 that no one had remarked upon in half a century. The figurine's tail was vertical. No bird in nature has a vertical tail. The figurine's tail was shaped like the vertical stabilizer, or keel, of an aircraft. Its wings and fuselage displayed aerodynamic, "streamlined" contours. The only missing element was a horizontal stabilizer, which Messiha proposed had broken off in antiquity.

Messiha built a replica, added the missing stabilizer, and tested it. The model glided smoothly across several meters. When fitted with a small motor and propeller, it flew.

Sklyarov treated the Saqqara bird as exhibit A in his cargo cult argument. The figurine was not evidence that ancient Egyptians possessed aircraft. It was evidence that ancient Egyptians had seen something that flew, something that was not a bird, and had attempted to reproduce it in wood. The papyrus inscription "I want to fly" was, in this reading, not a whimsical afterlife wish but a literal one: the person buried with this figurine wanted to do what the gods could do.

Sklyarov extended the same argument to the so-called "Colombian golden airplanes," small cast-metal figurines found in tombs across Colombia, **Venezuela**, **Costa Rica**, and Peru. The figurines, made from **tumbaga** (a gold-copper alloy containing roughly 30% copper), were first discovered in the nineteenth century during an archaeological expedition in Colombia and transferred to the **Colombian State Bank**, which catalogued them as "zoomorphic figures." They have eyes, teeth, and scales carved into their bodies, attributes of living creatures. They also have delta wings, vertical tail fins, and fuselage shapes that resemble no known animal. In the 1990s, German aeronautical engineers Algund Eenboom and Peter Belting built scaled-up models of several of these figurines, fitted them with engines, and flew them. The models were aerodynamically functional. This does not prove that the originals were intended to represent aircraft. It proves that their shapes are compatible with powered flight, a property that is not shared by any actual fish, bird, or insect.

Sklyarov's argument was never that these objects were planes. His argument was that they were copies: «Острова Тихого океана не единственное место, где имеются аналоги карго-культа» (The Pacific Islands are not the only place where cargo cult analogs exist). Where the Melanesian islanders built bamboo airplanes, the Egyptians carved wooden ones. Where the islanders constructed coconut-shell headsets, the Colombians cast golden delta-winged figurines and buried them with their dead. The cognitive operation was identical across millennia.


### The Gods Who Taught

The cargo cult inversion gains its force from one further observation that Sklyarov assembled systematically in ***Создание древних цивилизаций*** (The Creation of Ancient Civilizations). In every ancient civilization that left a textual record, the gods are credited not merely with existing but with founding the civilization itself.

The **Sumerians** attributed the invention of agriculture, metallurgy, writing, law, medicine, and astronomy to the **Anunnaki**. The Egyptians credited **Thoth** with writing, Osiris with agriculture, and the **Shemsu Hor** (Followers of Horus) with establishing the first divine dynasties thousands of years before the pharaohs. The Maya attributed their calendar and cosmology to **Itzamna** and **Kukulkan**. The Inca credited Viracocha with creating human civilization after the Flood. Hindu tradition attributes metallurgy to the gods explicitly, a claim Sklyarov explored at length in *Металлы: дар небесных богов* (Metals: A Gift of the Heavenly Gods).

Sklyarov drew these threads together: «Боги не только правили людьми. Они дали людям знания в медицине и астрономии, обучили их земледелию и животноводству, металлургии и гончарному искусству. По сути, вся человеческая цивилизация как таковая была создана богами» (The gods did not merely rule over humans. They gave humans knowledge of medicine and astronomy, taught them agriculture and animal husbandry, metallurgy and pottery. In essence, all of human civilization as such was created by the gods).

Mainstream anthropology interprets these attributions as mythological: cultures retroactively ascribe their own innovations to divine authority in order to legitimize existing social structures and priestly hierarchies. The gods are projections, not memories. Sklyarov reversed the causality. The attributions, he argued, were accurate, or at least preserved an accurate memory: «Современная академическая наука относит древние легенды и предания в разряд мифологии, считая их выдумками и фантазиями наших далеких предков. А реальное существование в древности "богов" категорически отрицается историками» (Modern academic science relegates ancient legends and traditions to the category of mythology, considering them inventions and fantasies of our distant ancestors. And the actual existence of "gods" in antiquity is categorically denied by historians). The denial, Sklyarov observed, functions as dogma rather than conclusion: it is not the result of investigating the myths and finding them empty, but a prior assumption that prevents the investigation from occurring.


### The Metals Puzzle

Sklyarov devoted an entire book to metallurgy. *Металлы: дар небесных богов* (Metals: A Gift of the Heavenly Gods) surveyed the origin myths of metalworking across cultures and found the same pattern he had identified everywhere else: the gods gave metals to humans. The Sumerians attributed metalworking to the Anunnaki. The Egyptians attributed it to Ptah. The Greeks attributed it to **Hephaestus**. Hindu tradition assigns the discovery of metals to divine instruction. In virtually every culture that smelts metal, the knowledge of smelting is credited not to human ingenuity but to divine gift.

Mainstream archaeology explains this as retroactive mythologization: cultures attribute their own innovations to gods in order to invest those innovations with sacred authority. This is a plausible interpretation. But Sklyarov pointed to something the retroactive-mythologization hypothesis does not explain. The archaeological record of metallurgy shows a peculiar gap between the first appearance of certain metals in worked form and the first evidence of the smelting infrastructure required to produce them. Copper appears in the archaeological record at several sites in the Near East and Anatolia as early as 9000 BCE in its native (unworked) form. Smelted copper appears around 5000 BCE. The intervening four thousand years show worked native copper but no smelting furnaces, no slag heaps, no evidence of the high-temperature technology required to extract metal from ore. The transition from cold-worked native copper to smelted copper is treated in mainstream metallurgical archaeology as a gradual development. Sklyarov asked why it took four thousand years.

The answer, in the conventional framework, is that smelting is not an obvious invention. You do not discover how to extract metal from rock by accident, and the temperatures required (roughly 1,100 degrees Celsius for copper, higher for iron) are not achievable in an ordinary cooking fire. The development of smelting required the prior development of high-temperature kilns, which were initially developed for pottery. This is a reasonable sequence. Sklyarov did not dispute it. What he disputed was the assumption that humans developed it independently. He noted that the myths say otherwise: the gods taught metallurgy. And the gap in the archaeological record, four thousand years between native copper and smelted copper, is compatible with a scenario in which the knowledge of smelting was not developed locally but introduced from an external source.

This is, of course, the cargo cult model applied to technology rather than ritual objects. The gods (whoever they were) smelted metal. Humans observed the process. The gods departed. Humans attempted to reproduce the process with local materials. It took four thousand years to figure it out. The interpretation is speculative. The gap is real.


### The Mainstream Objection

The mainstream objection to Sklyarov is devastating in its simplicity, and it must be stated plainly: there is no physical evidence of non-terrestrial manufacture at any archaeological site on Earth.

No anomalous isotopic signatures. No materials whose composition cannot be explained by terrestrial geology. No artifacts whose function cannot, in principle, be replicated with period-appropriate tools. No non-human DNA at any ancient site. No communication device. No power source. Nothing that could not have been made by humans, however ingeniously.

Alexander Sokolov (Александр Соколов), editor of **Antropogenez.ru** and Russia's most prominent public critic of alternative history, addressed the ancient-astronaut hypothesis directly at the ***Ученые против мифов*** (Scientists Against Myths) conference series. Sokolov coined the term **"nemogliki"** (не-мог-лики, roughly "couldn't-have-done-its"), referring to those who assume that ancient peoples could not have solved certain engineering problems on their own. The ancient-astronaut hypothesis, Sokolov argued, reduces to this single assumption: humans could not have built what they built, therefore someone else must have. The assumption is neither supported by the evidence nor free of a patronizing implication about the capabilities of non-modern humans. Experimental archaeology has demonstrated that every known ancient monument can, in principle, be constructed with the tools and labor available to its attributed builders. The experiments are sometimes grueling, sometimes ingenious, and always more mundane than the alien alternative.

Sokolov also addressed the specific claim about the "Abydos helicopter," a panel at the Temple of Seti I in Abydos, Egypt, that appears to show images of a helicopter, submarine, and airplane. The panel, he demonstrated, is a palimpsest: the cartouche of Seti I was partially overwritten by the cartouche of his son **Ramesses II**, and the overlapping hieroglyphs produce shapes that, to a modern eye primed by technology, resemble vehicles. The explanation is banal. The effect is a typographical accident.

The Abydos case is instructive because it illustrates the fundamental methodological problem with the ancient-astronaut approach. If you begin with the assumption that ancient gods were real extraterrestrials, then every ambiguous artifact becomes potential evidence. A wooden figurine with a vertical tail is an airplane. An overlapping hieroglyph is a helicopter. A golden figurine with delta wings is a jet. The interpretation drives the evidence rather than the reverse, and the pool of ambiguous artifacts in the global archaeological record is effectively infinite, which guarantees that the hypothesis can never run out of material.


### The Taxonomy That Survives

There is a way to read Sklyarov that most of his critics have not attempted, because it requires separating his observations from his conclusions, and his conclusions are so dramatic that they tend to consume all available attention.

Strip away the extraterrestrial hypothesis. Ignore the gods. Ignore the spaceships. What is left?

An observational taxonomy. At dozens of sites, on multiple continents, two distinct levels of stone-working technology coexist in the same structures. The more primitive level is consistent with the tool kits and labor capacities of the civilizations to which these sites are attributed. The more sophisticated level is not, or at least has not been satisfactorily explained. The tool marks on the sophisticated layer, according to Sklyarov's measurements and photographic documentation, are consistent with powered machinery: disc saws, core drills, and surface grinders operating at speeds and with tolerances that exceed what can be achieved by hand with dolerite pounders and copper tubular drills.

This taxonomy does not require aliens. It does not even require a lost civilization in the Hancock ([Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)) sense. It requires only that someone, at some point, possessed manufacturing capabilities far beyond those of the dynastic Egyptians or the Inca. The identity of that someone is a separate question from the existence of the evidence.<sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup>

Sklyarov's own identification, that the "someone" was an extraterrestrial civilization whose members were the original "gods" of human mythology, is extraordinary and requires extraordinary evidence. No such evidence has been produced. The ancient-astronaut hypothesis remains unfalsifiable in any meaningful scientific sense. It cannot be tested and therefore it cannot be science. This is its most damning characteristic.<sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup>

But Sklyarov's documentation of the two-layer phenomenon at specific sites does not depend on the extraterrestrial hypothesis for its validity. The disc-saw marks at Saqqara either exist or they do not. The metric tolerances at Ollantaytambo either exceed what stone hammers can produce or they do not. These are empirical questions, and the mainstream has been slow to address them directly, preferring to dismiss the researcher rather than engage with his data. Sokolov's "nemogliki" critique is effective against the general claim that aliens built the pyramids. It is less effective against the specific claim that a basalt sarcophagus in the Pyramid of Teti displays a kerf pattern consistent with a rotating blade of fixed diameter.<sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup><sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-24)</sup>


### The Copies Everywhere

Sklyarov did not limit the cargo cult model to a handful of famous artifacts. He applied it systematically across the archaeological record, finding what he considered analogs everywhere.

In India, the **Shiva lingam**, an aniconic stone form found in temples across the subcontinent, is revered as a representation of **Shiva's** creative power. The standard scholarly interpretation treats it as a phallic symbol associated with fertility worship. Sklyarov noted that the physical form of the lingam, a smooth cylinder with a rounded top set into a grooved base called a **yoni**, bears no close anatomical resemblance to male genitalia but does resemble a specific class of industrial object: a pestle or grinding tool seated in a mortar. He cited an Indian myth in which Shiva, in passionate pursuit of the goddess **Mohini** (an avatar of Vishnu), dropped his seed twelve times, and at each point where it struck the earth, a sacred lingam materialized with special luminous force. Twelve temples were built at these sites, each housing a **jyotirlinga**, a lingam of particular sanctity. Millions of Hindus make pilgrimage to these twelve sites, from the foothills of the Himalayas in the north to Rameshwaram in the south. Sklyarov observed that the variation in ritual between the twelve temples was analogous to the variation between cargo cult rituals on different Pacific islands: «На разных островах перечень "магических" ритуалов мог отличаться, но в целом все сводилось к единой основе» (On different islands the list of "magical" rituals could differ, but overall everything reduced to a single foundation). The twelve jyotirlingas, like the bamboo airplanes, were reproductions of an original whose function had been lost and whose form had been preserved in ritual. Whether this reading is correct is unknowable. The point is methodological.

Sklyarov also applied the model to architecture. The step pyramids of Mesoamerica, the ziggurats of Mesopotamia, the pyramids of Egypt, the stepped platforms of Southeast Asia: these structures share a common form (a truncated pyramid or stepped tower with a temple at the apex) but are attributed to civilizations that, according to mainstream archaeology, developed independently. The conventional explanation is convergent cultural evolution: a stepped structure is the simplest way to build something tall out of stone, and any civilization that wants to build tall will discover this independently. Sklyarov's response was consistent with his framework: the convergence was not coincidental because the forms were not independent inventions. They were copies, more or less faithful, of something the gods had built. The original structure served a function. The copies served a ritual one. Across millennia and continents, the shape persisted because the awe it inspired persisted.

The same logic applied, in his framework, to the institution of the priesthood itself. Every ancient civilization maintained a class of specialists whose function was to serve as intermediaries between humans and gods. These specialists performed rituals, maintained temples, interpreted omens, and preserved sacred knowledge. Sklyarov argued that the priesthoods were the equivalent of the cargo cult's ritual specialists: the men who sat in coconut-shell headphones shouting into bamboo microphones. They were reproducing the actions of the gods' intermediaries without understanding the underlying technology. The robes were copied from garments the gods wore. The ritual objects were copied from devices the gods operated. The chants were copied from words the gods spoke. And the temples were copied from buildings the gods inhabited.

The internal consistency of this model is impressive. Its weakness is that it is consistent with everything, which means it explains nothing specific. A model that can absorb any data point without revision is not a model. It is a narrative.


### What Remains

Sklyarov died in 2016. His archive at LAH.ru remains accessible. His books remain untranslated into English, which means that the most detailed observational work in the ancient-astronaut tradition is inaccessible to the English-speaking scholars who dismiss the tradition most vocally. His expedition footage, shot at sites from Baalbek ([Chapter 10](10-the-trilithon-problem.md)) to **Easter Island**, documents observations that no mainstream archaeologist has publicly replicated or refuted in detail.

The mainstream position on the ancient-astronaut hypothesis is clear and, in its broad strokes, correct. Comparative mythology and cognitive science explain cross-cultural mythological similarities through universal human psychology, shared responses to natural phenomena, and cultural diffusion through trade and migration. None of these mechanisms requires extraterrestrial contact. The specific interpretations offered by Sklyarov, von Daniken, and similar authors involve selective emphasis on ambiguous artifacts and the application of modern technological vocabulary to objects that were almost certainly created within entirely different conceptual frameworks.

Sklyarov acknowledged, repeatedly, that his hypothesis was speculative. In his work on elongated skulls, he wrote explicitly that certain conclusions «стоило бы перепроверить» (ought to be re-checked). He flagged gaps in his own evidence. He did not fabricate data, did not claim to have found alien artifacts, and did not, unlike von Daniken, treat ancient peoples as incapable of their own achievements. His argument was subtler: that certain specific features of certain specific sites resist explanation by the tool kits and methods attributed to their builders, and that the cargo cult model provides a coherent framework for understanding why human civilizations worldwide developed strikingly similar mythological, architectural, and ritual traditions around the concept of gods who created civilization and then departed.

Whether that framework is correct matters less, at this point, than whether the observations it organizes are accurate. The two-layer construction phenomenon has not been systematically studied by mainstream archaeology. The disc-saw marks at Saqqara have not been formally measured and published in a peer-reviewed journal. The circular drill holes at Karnak have not been subjected to tribological analysis. These are not theoretical questions. They are empirical ones. They could be answered with equipment that exists today.

The cargo cult on Tanna continues. The priests of John Frum still broadcast on their coconut-shell radios. The bamboo airplanes have not attracted any actual cargo. But the airplanes are shaped like airplanes.

That part was never imaginary.
