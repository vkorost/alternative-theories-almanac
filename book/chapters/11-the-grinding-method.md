## Chapter 11: The Grinding Method – Peru, Bolivia, Tiwanaku, and Megalithic Precision
The wall at **Sacsayhuaman** had been Protzen's subject since 1986. He had already demonstrated the core method. But his work at Sacsayhuaman and Ollantaytambo went further than the argument about two-layer construction discussed in his published challenge to the two-builder hypothesis. It constituted an independent experimental program: a systematic attempt to show that the most demanding features of Inca masonry could be produced with tools the Inca themselves had left behind.

The massive fortress complex overlooking Cusco at an altitude of 3,700 meters had become a fixture of alternative-history presentations. The blocks were enormous, some weighing over a hundred tons, one estimated at 128 tons. They were fitted together in complex, interlocking shapes with joints so tight that a knife blade could not be inserted between them. No mortar had been used. The stones were not rectangular; they were polygonal, each one a unique shape, each one fitting its neighbors like a piece of a jigsaw puzzle designed by someone with no interest in making the assembler's life easy. The popular conclusion, advanced with increasing confidence by writers from **Erich von Daniken** to Hancock ([see Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)), was that this level of precision required technologies unavailable to the Inca civilization: machine tooling, laser cutting, or, in the more speculative versions, intervention by an advanced precursor civilization whose identity remained conveniently unspecified.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup>

Protzen ([Chapter 3](03-the-second-builder.md)) did not find the argument convincing. He found it testable.

He went to the Inca quarry sites at **Rumiqolqa** and **Kachiqhata**, where stone hammers of varying sizes still lay scattered on the ground, abandoned when the Spanish conquest interrupted the work. The hammers ranged from fist-sized to nearly twenty kilograms, and their surfaces bore the scars of repeated impact against harder stone. Protzen collected these hammers. He studied the marks they left. And then, working with his collaborator **Stella Nair**, he set about replicating the characteristic precision of Inca masonry using nothing but the tools the Inca themselves had left behind.

The technique they documented was not mysterious. It was laborious to a degree that modern sensibilities find almost incomprehensible. A block was rough-shaped by pounding, using the stone hammers swung in controlled arcs. The target block was then lowered repeatedly onto the block it was to join. Each time it was lowered, the high points on the contact surface scraped against the receiving stone, leaving visible marks. These high points were then ground away with smaller stones and abrasive sand. The block was raised, lowered, checked, ground, raised again. The process was iterated dozens, perhaps hundreds of times, until the two surfaces mated completely. The result was a joint of extraordinary precision, achieved through nothing more sophisticated than patience, gravity, and the laws of friction.

For the **H-blocks** at Puma Punku, a site in Bolivia whose geometric precision is the single most cited piece of evidence in the alternative-history case for ancient machine tooling, Nair demonstrated that the cross-shaped negative spaces and interlocking recesses could be produced with stone tools available in the region: blades, flakes, and thin chisels made from **flint**, **agate**, **jasper**, obsidian, and **hematite**. She successfully replicated a half-cross-shaped design to the same precision visible on the original blocks. Unfinished stones at the quarry site at Puma Punku show the progressive stages of pecking and grinding used to shape them, a manufacturing sequence frozen mid-process, leaving no unexplained gap between the raw material and the finished product. The claim that the heaviest blocks at Puma Punku weigh 440 tons, a figure that circulates endlessly in alternative-history presentations, has been corrected by archaeologists: the heaviest block is approximately 130 tons, and most are far lighter.

This is **the Grinding Method**: the slow, iterative fitting of stone to stone through repeated contact, abrasion, and removal of material at high points. It requires no metal tools. It requires no wheels or pulleys. It requires only stone, sand, gravity, and an amount of organized human labor that modern sensibilities find difficult to imagine but that the Inca Empire, with its **mit'a** labor-tax system commanding the work of tens of thousands of drafted workers, could deploy without strain. The mit'a was not slavery. It was a labor tax, rotational and obligatory, that channeled the productive capacity of an empire with no currency and no market economy into state projects. When the state wanted a wall built, the state got its wall.

The Spanish chroniclers described it. Protzen and Nair demonstrated it. The quarry sites preserve its intermediate stages. The method is not in dispute. What is instructive is how long it took to be demonstrated. For decades, the alternative-history presentation of Inca masonry went essentially unanswered. The joints were photographed, measured, marveled at, and declared impossible by writers who had never attempted to replicate them. Protzen's experiment ended that line of argument. The implication is not that alternative historians were wrong about the impressiveness of the joints; the joints are genuinely impressive. The implication is that "impressive" and "impossible" are different words, and the distance between them is the width of an experimental test.

What is in dispute is whether the method explains everything that stands in the Peruvian and Bolivian highlands. And that question leads to places where the neat experimental results begin to develop cracks of their own.


### The Tired Stone

The conventional history of monumental construction in the Andes is organized around two civilizations separated by roughly five centuries and a thousand kilometers.

The **Inca Empire**, which rose to dominance in the fifteenth century CE under **Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui**, built Sacsayhuaman, Ollantaytambo, **Machu Picchu**, and the fortress walls of Cusco. The construction of Sacsayhuaman alone is estimated to have required 20,000 or more workers over decades. Spanish colonial sources, including the chronicles of **Cieza de Leon** and Garcilaso de la Vega, documented the construction methods in detail: wooden rollers, earthen ramps, rope teams, and the iterative grinding technique that Protzen would experimentally confirm five centuries later. Garcilaso, the son of a Spanish conquistador and an Inca princess, wrote his ***Royal Commentaries of the Incas*** in 1609, and his account is both detailed and conflicted, a man struggling visibly to reconcile his admiration for Inca civilization with his Catholic education's demand that pagan cultures be regarded as inferior. The cultural conflict shows through the centuries of prose and can bring a reader, as Richards noted, close to tears.<sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup>

Garcilaso named the four master architects who directed the construction of Sacsayhuaman: **Huallpa Rimachi Inca**, **Inca Maricanchi**, **Acahuana Inca**, and **Calla Cunchuy**. The work is attributed to the reign of Pachacuti, who undertook a massive building program across the empire. The labor was supplied by the mit'a system, a rotational draft that required all able-bodied subjects to contribute work to state projects.

But Garcilaso also reported something that Hancock would later seize upon. An Inca king had attempted to emulate the achievements of earlier builders at Sacsayhuaman by dragging a single immense boulder from a quarry several miles away. The attempt mobilized more than 20,000 workers. At a steep point along the route, the boulder slipped from its restraints, rolled down a precipice, and crushed more than 3,000 men. The stone was never installed. The Inca called it the "Tired Stone" and said it wept tears of blood.<sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup>

Garcilaso corrected the myth even as he recorded it. It was not the stone that wept. It was the workers, and the slaves, who wept blood. The story is not a parable about divine punishment. It is a work-site accident report.

The story suggests two things. First, the Inca were capable of organizing enormous labor forces for stone transport. Second, even with 20,000 workers, moving the largest stones at Sacsayhuaman was a task that pushed their capabilities to catastrophic failure. The polygonal walls that Protzen replicated in miniature were built at the same site where that failure occurred. The question of whether the Inca could have built all of Sacsayhuaman using the methods documented in the chronicles has a clear answer for the moderate-sized blocks and a far less clear answer for the very largest ones.


### The Water Engineers

The Tiwanaku civilization, centered near the southern shore of Lake Titicaca in modern Bolivia, is the more enigmatic of the two Andean builders, and the one where the gap between the conventional explanation and the physical evidence is widest.

Tiwanaku flourished from approximately 300 CE to 1000 CE. Its capital, situated at an altitude of 3,850 meters in a landscape that is simultaneously beautiful and punishing, was home to an estimated 10,000 to 20,000 people at its peak. **Radiocarbon dating** of organic materials at Puma Punku (reeds, charcoal, human bone) consistently returns dates between 536 CE and 600 CE for the primary construction phase. The sandstone blocks were quarried from a source approximately ten kilometers away; the andesite blocks from a source ninety kilometers away, a distance manageable with organized labor and rafts on Lake Titicaca. A 2018 study published in ***npj Heritage Science*** used 3D printing to reconstruct Tiwanaku architectural elements and confirmed that the construction was consistent with human-scale modular design. Nothing about the logistics is impossible.

But the totality of what the Tiwanaku achieved at a single site, and the way they achieved it, has no close parallel in the pre-Columbian Andes.

Richards, who examined the Tiwanaku complex in his review of Hancock's ***Fingerprints of the Gods***, identified several features that set it apart. The arsenic-bronze **metal clamps** used to join stone blocks at Tiwanaku and Puma Punku, whose technical profile is examined in detail in the discussion of the two-layer construction pattern, represented the most structurally anomalous of these features: Richards described them as looking «like standard operating procedure, not like something that was troubleshot on the site.» They appeared, fully formed, without precedent in the region and without successors after Tiwanaku's collapse.

The water-management system was equally distinctive. The Akapana pyramid, the largest structure at Tiwanaku, served as the hub of a hydraulic network. Its surface was covered in channels that directed rainwater and snowmelt through the pyramid's interior into enormous subterranean reservoirs. These reservoirs stored fresh water for distribution to surrounding farms and settlements. Some researchers have proposed that the Akapana was part of a top-down water management system for the entire surrounding region, feeding farms and towns with a constant supply of fresh water stored at altitude.

The nearby **suka kollus**, raised agricultural platforms separated by water-filled channels, used a technique of thermal regulation that was both simple and brilliant. The water absorbed solar heat during the day and radiated it back at night during the vulnerable early-morning hours, protecting crops from the killing frosts that are a constant threat at 3,850 meters above sea level. This was not a hedge against occasional bad weather. This was a designed agricultural system that turned the physics of thermal mass into a frost-protection mechanism, allowing cultivation at altitudes where unprotected crops would fail every year. Modern researchers who have reconstructed the suka kollu system have found that it substantially outperforms conventional farming methods at these altitudes, producing higher yields with greater reliability. The technique was abandoned after the Tiwanaku collapse and was not independently reinvented by the Inca or by subsequent cultures in the region. When archaeologists reintroduced it experimentally in the 1980s and 1990s, local farmers were astonished by the results. The knowledge had been gone for a thousand years.

The style of art at Tiwanaku was different from anything else in the Andes. The **Gateway of the Sun**, a single block of andesite weighing approximately ten tons, bears a frieze centered on a figure that Hancock and others have identified with **Viracocha**, the civilizing god of Andean mythology. The figure holds two staffs and is surrounded by winged attendants. Hancock saw in the freeze depictions of extinct animals, including what he interpreted as toxodons and elephants. Richards was not impressed by the animal identifications (his children, he observed, drew shapes like the supposed toxodons all the time), but he acknowledged that the site's overall character was genuinely anomalous compared with everything else in the region. «You're not going to find spaceships here,» Richards concluded, «but this is a pretty good candidate for a place that did things a lot different than anybody else in the region.» He distinguished carefully between legitimate anomalies and overreach. The metal clamps, the water engineering, the modular architecture: these are real features that have no regional precedent. The supposed depictions of extinct animals on the Gateway of the Sun, by contrast, are a case of pattern-matching that does not survive scrutiny. «Grabbing low-quality evidence only hurts your case,» Richards argued, and the elephant interpretations, which rely on negative space and generous imagination, are the kind of thing that «when somebody attacks this section of Fingerprints of the Gods, they'll point out and say look, he found elephants where there were no elephants.» The distinction matters because legitimate anomalies get buried under discredited ones, and the site's genuine strangeness gets lost in the noise of bad arguments made on its behalf.


### The Language Nobody Expected

Hancock, in *Fingerprints of the Gods*, highlighted a detail about the Tiwanaku region that has nothing to do with stone and everything to do with the question of what these builders knew.

The **Aymara** language, spoken by the indigenous population around Lake Titicaca, possesses a syntactic structure so regular, so internally consistent, and so devoid of the irregularities that characterize most natural languages that in the 1980s, a Bolivian mathematician named **Ivan Guzman de Rojas** was able to use it as the basis for a multilanguage computer translation program. Aymara's grammar functioned as a bridge language, a structural intermediary that could translate between unrelated languages with unusual accuracy. The program exploited the fact that Aymara's syntax is almost entirely regular; there are virtually no exceptions to its grammatical rules, a property that natural languages, shaped by millennia of organic evolution, almost never possess. Until the arrival of neural-network translation systems like Google Translate, Guzman de Rojas's Aymara-based translator was one of the most effective automated translation tools in existence.

This is not evidence of advanced technology in the conventional sense. But it is evidence of something. A language whose logical architecture is so precise and so unlike the natural messiness of most human speech that it functions, in effect, as a programming language. Whether this structure arose naturally through millennia of refinement or was deliberately designed by people with an unusual grasp of linguistic logic is a question that remains unanswered. Richards, who tends toward the skeptical end of the spectrum on most questions, called it «mind-blowing» and «enigmatic» and added that it «shows an understanding that is very out of place for its time.» He paused, as he rarely does, before adding: «It's the kind of enigma that keeps me coming back to this kind of stuff.»


### The Sklyarov Assessment

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)) visited Peru and Bolivia in the autumn of 2007 with a small research team from the **Foundation for the Third Millennium** (Фонд «III тысячелетие»). The team, consisting of Sklyarov, **Aleksandr Dymnikov**, **Andrey Zhukov**, **Aleksey Teslenko**, **Vyacheslav Krasakov**, and **Maksim Lyamenkov**, spent weeks traveling from Cusco to the Sacred Valley, from the Pacific coast to the Bolivian altiplano, documenting everything with the obsessive attention to surface detail that characterized all Sklyarov's expeditions. The resulting six-part documentary and book, ***Перу и Боливия задолго до инков*** (Peru and Bolivia Long Before the Incas), represented Sklyarov at his most characteristic: meticulous observation wrapped in a framework of sweeping speculation.<sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup>

Sklyarov was not shy about his conclusions. «Ну не могли это сделать инки... Не могли!» (The Inca simply could not have done this... They couldn't!) he wrote in the book's introduction, with a directness that left no room for qualification. His argument, applied at Sacsayhuaman, at Ollantaytambo, at Puma Punku, and at a dozen other sites across the Andes, was always the same: when you see two visually distinct styles of masonry at the same location, with the more precise work at the bottom and the cruder work on top, the simplest explanation is two different builders separated by time. The Inca, in his view, inherited the lower walls from a much earlier civilization, the «боги» (gods), and built their own additions on top of them. He documented the evidence carefully: the enormous polygonal blocks of the lower fortress walls at Sacsayhuaman, fitted with sub-millimeter precision, surmounted by smaller, rougher stonework. The lower blocks showed no tool marks consistent with the stone hammers found at Inca quarry sites. The upper blocks showed exactly such marks.

Sklyarov also noted the metal clamps at Tiwanaku and traced a connection to similar joinery techniques in the Old World. His book on metallurgy, *Металлы: дар небесных богов* (Metals: A Gift of the Celestial Gods), developed this observation into a broader argument about the transmission of technical knowledge from a pre-human civilization to early human cultures. The Tiwanaku clamps, he argued, were not invented locally but inherited from the same source that provided the technique to Greece, India, and Egypt. The argument is not independently verifiable; it depends on the existence of the advanced precursor civilization that is Sklyarov's central thesis. But the observation that the clamp technique appears at Tiwanaku without precedent in the region and disappears after Tiwanaku's collapse is a datum that the conventional explanation has not fully addressed.<sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup>

Sklyarov's team documented one detail at Ollantaytambo that complicates even his own two-layer argument. At the **Temple of the Sun**, six massive monoliths of pink rhyolite stand in a row, separated by narrow slabs of a different stone that serve as spacers. The monoliths were quarried from a source on the opposite side of the valley, several kilometers away and at a lower elevation. Transporting them required crossing the Urubamba River, climbing several hundred meters of steep terrain, and placing them with a precision that leaves no gap between monolith and spacer. The quarry itself still holds partially finished blocks, abandoned mid-process, with clear evidence of the progressive shaping techniques that Protzen documented. What the quarry does not explain is why the Ollantaytambo builders chose a material source on the opposite side of a valley from their construction site when adequate stone existed much closer. The decision implies either a specific material requirement that the local stone could not satisfy, or a level of logistical confidence that treating a river crossing and a mountain climb as minor inconveniences. Both possibilities suggest a culture operating at a scale of organization that belies the image of simple stone-age builders improvising with available materials.

The mainstream response to Sklyarov's two-layer argument is that any construction project produces varying levels of finish depending on the visibility and structural importance of each section. The foundations of Roman buildings are rougher than the facades; the backs of Egyptian statues are less polished than the fronts. A difference in finish does not, by itself, require a difference in authorship. Protzen's experimental work demonstrated that the grinding method can produce the precision visible in the lower walls. The Spanish chroniclers documented a labor force large enough to execute such projects. The quarry sites at Rumiqolqa and Kachiqhata preserve unfinished stones at every stage of the manufacturing process; there is no missing step in the chain of production between raw quarry block and finished wall. The mainstream explanation is mechanically complete.

What Protzen's experiments do not address, and what the chronicles do not explain, is how the technique was discovered in the first place. The grinding method is simple in principle but deeply counterintuitive in practice. No one facing a hundred-ton block of andesite for the first time would arrive at the idea of raising and lowering it hundreds of times against another stone to achieve a perfect fit. The technique requires not just labor but a specific conceptual insight into how surfaces mate under iterative abrasion, an insight that presupposes either a long history of experimentation or transmission from someone who already possessed the knowledge. Protzen demonstrated that the method works. He did not, and could not, explain how the Inca, or their predecessors, arrived at it. The question of origin, as opposed to the question of mechanism, is one that experimental archaeology cannot reach.


### The Colonial Filter

Richards, who reviewed Hancock's treatment of the Andes in a multi-part series on *Fingerprints of the Gods*, was careful to distinguish between the genuinely anomalous features of Tiwanaku and the evidence that does not survive scrutiny. The supposed depictions of extinct animals on the Gateway of the Sun, for instance, rely on negative-space interpretations that Richards called "low-quality evidence" that "only hurts your case." The elephant identifications, which require generous imagination and a willingness to ignore the more obvious reading of the carvings, are "the kind of thing that when somebody attacks this section of Fingerprints of the Gods, they'll point out and say look, he found elephants where there were no elephants." The distinction between real anomalies and imagined ones is not academic. It is strategic: legitimate puzzles get buried under discredited claims, and the site's genuine strangeness disappears into the noise of bad arguments made on its behalf. Richards made the case that the metal clamps, the water engineering, and the modular architecture deserve serious investigation precisely because they do not require pattern-matching or imaginative interpretation. They are physical features with measurable properties that have no regional precedent.<sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[16](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup><sup>[17](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-11)</sup>

The destruction of the Inca's own records complicates the picture beyond what most accounts acknowledge. The Inca used **quipu**, knotted string devices, for record-keeping, and the vast majority were destroyed during the Spanish conquest. The surviving colonial sources are filtered through Catholic ideology, personal agendas, and a cultural distance that repeatedly produced misunderstanding.

Garcilaso's ***Royal Commentaries*** are the most detailed and most conflicted of these sources. Richards highlighted several passages that deserve more attention than they typically receive. Garcilaso described the Inca religion's worship of the sun, including a ritual in which the high priest used a large, carefully polished concave bracelet to concentrate the sun's rays onto cotton tinder, producing fire. Fresh fire, «given to them by the hand of the sun,» was required for sacrificial rituals. The bracelet's central motif was «a very carefully polished concavity as big as half an orange.» No such artifact has been found in the archaeological record, and if it existed, it would almost certainly have been looted during the **Conquistador** period. But the description, if accurate, represents a functional **concave mirror** used for solar ignition, a piece of optical technology that Richards called «out-of-place tech» and noted that «if you want to talk about out-of-place tech, this definitely fits the bill.»

Garcilaso also documented cultural practices that parallel Old World traditions. The Inca kings, like the Egyptian pharaohs, claimed descent from the sun. Like the pharaohs, they practiced royal sibling marriage to preserve the purity of the divine bloodline. Like the Egyptians, they placed their dead in elaborate tombs and maintained traditions of ancestor veneration. They set their dead adrift on reed boats on Lake Titicaca, a motif that echoes the Osiris myth with uncomfortable precision: Viracocha's body was placed in a boat of **totora** reed and set drift on the lake, sailed away at such speed that pursuers could not catch it, struck land at Cochamarca with such force that it created the **Rio Desaguadero**, and was carried down to the sea coast at **Arica**. Richards noted these parallels without claiming they proved contact, but he acknowledged that «you get enough of these correlations and it does kind of start to look like evidence.»

The Viracocha myth itself has been thoroughly contaminated by Spanish overlay. Garcilaso described Viracocha as «a tall, well-built man wearing a beard as long as your hand» dressed in a tunic reaching his feet. But Garcilaso, Richards noted, never described the Viracocha as white-skinned, only as bearded. The popular image of a pale-skinned civilizing god was largely a product of later retellings, influenced by Catholic missionaries who had every incentive to incorporate Christian motifs into indigenous belief systems. The flood narrative, the civilizing deity, the departure and promised return: all could be original, all could be imported, and separating the layers is a task that the destruction of the primary sources has rendered permanently incomplete.


### The Detoxification Puzzle

The early inhabitants of the Lake Titicaca basin domesticated a suite of high-altitude plants that are, in their natural state, poisonous. **Potatoes** at high altitude contain **solanine** and digestive enzyme inhibitors. **Quinoa** and **kaniwa** contain **hydrocyanic acid** and the alkaloid **saponin**. **Oka** has significant amounts of **oxalates**. **Tarwi** contains the poisonous alkaloid **lupinine**. **Amaranth** is a nitrate accumulator. Every one of these plants required the development of specific detoxification techniques, including freeze-drying, leaching, and sun-drying, before it could be safely consumed.

The case of the potato is illustrative. The species most resistant to frost, and therefore most valuable at high altitude, are also the most toxic. The **altiplano** solution, developed at some unknown point in the deep past, was **chuno**: potatoes freeze-dried by repeated cycles of freezing, leaching in water, and drying in the sun. The initial explanation for this process was food preservation; chuno can be stored for six years or more, allowing surpluses from good years to buffer against bad ones. But the process also serves as detoxification: leaching removes the solanine, and sun-drying destroys the digestive enzyme inhibitors. Both functions are present, and neither alone explains the complexity of the technique.

A study cited by Hancock concluded that «there is as of yet no satisfactory explanation for why the early inhabitants of the Titicaca Basin continued to experiment with plants that must have given ample gastrointestinal distress during unsuccessful tests until they achieved a practical technology.» The concentration of detoxification innovations at a single location, applied to an entire suite of unrelated plant species, each requiring a different technique, is a puzzle about how knowledge accumulated in the Titicaca basin at a rate and level of integration that has no documented parallel in other early agricultural societies.

Trial and error can explain it. Trial and error explains most things, given enough time. But the trial-and-error explanation requires many generations of people systematically experimenting with toxic plants, recording the results (without writing, since the Inca had none in the conventional sense), and transmitting the accumulated knowledge with enough fidelity that each generation started where the last one left off. The concentration of detoxification innovations at a single location, applied to an entire suite of unrelated plant species, each requiring a different technique, is a puzzle that Richards acknowledged had no close parallel in other early agricultural centers. It requires, in short, exactly the kind of deep institutional knowledge that a collapsed precursor civilization might leave behind and that a successor culture might inherit without fully understanding.


### The Inverse of Technology

The pattern that emerges from the Peruvian and Bolivian evidence is the inverse of what a standard technological trajectory would predict. The oldest construction is the most precise. The newer work is cruder. The agricultural systems at Tiwanaku, which predate the Inca by centuries, are more sophisticated than anything the Inca built. The metal-clamp technique appears at Tiwanaku without precedent and disappears from the region after Tiwanaku's collapse around 1000 CE, likely driven by prolonged drought. The Aymara language is more structurally regular than any language spoken in the surrounding area.

This inverse trajectory is not unique to the Andes. It appears in Egyptian architecture (the Old Kingdom pyramids versus the later, inferior copies), in Mesopotamian writing (**Sumerian**, one of the earliest written languages, has a complexity that later cuneiform systems did not match), and at Gobekli Tepe, where the oldest enclosures are the most elaborately carved and the most recent are comparatively crude. It does not require the existence of a lost civilization to explain. Civilizations collapse, and when they do, the most resource-intensive technologies, the ones that require the largest labor forces, the most specialized knowledge, and the most stable social organization, are the first to disappear. The Tiwanaku collapse around 1000 CE would have destroyed the social infrastructure required to maintain the hydraulic systems and the precision stone-cutting program.

But the pattern does complicate the standard narrative, in which technology always progresses upward from simple to complex. In the Andes, the arrow points the other way.

Protzen proved the grinding method works. He did not prove the Inca invented it. The Inca themselves, in every account that survives the colonial filter, said they did not. They said the walls were already there when they arrived. They said the builders were the Viracocha, who came from the water, taught what they knew, and departed.

Whether the Viracocha were gods, refugees from a collapsed civilization, or a narrative invention of a culture trying to make sense of structures it had inherited but could not replicate is a question the evidence does not resolve. Whether the inverse trajectory reflects the collapse of a single upstream civilization, as Hancock and Sklyarov argue, or the independent rise and fall of multiple unrelated cultures, as the mainstream insists, is a question about parsimony rather than physics. Both explanations are compatible with the physical evidence. Neither can be confirmed without data that has been destroyed, submerged, or never collected.

The walls remain. The method that built them has been experimentally confirmed. The hands that first conceived both the method and the design are beyond the reach of the surviving record, and the quipu that might have told us who they were are ashes.
