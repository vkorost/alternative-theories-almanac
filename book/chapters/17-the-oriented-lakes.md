## Chapter 17: The Oriented Lakes – Carolina Bays and Impact Hypotheses
**Chris Cottrell** teaches environmental science to high schoolers on the coast of Georgia, a job he has held for over twenty years. His weekday world is nutrient cycles and watershed dynamics and the cheerful indifference of teenagers to both. His other world begins when the school day ends. Cottrell holds degrees in geology and environmental geography from **Troy State University** and geoscience from **Mississippi State University**, and he has spent the better part of a decade driving to bogs. Specifically, he drives to elliptical bogs, the kind that show up on aerial photographs as pale ovals in the dark coastal-plain forest, each one oriented in roughly the same direction, like a field of fossilized compass needles. He photographs them. He measures their rims. He takes GPS coordinates and soil samples. He maps their internal drainage patterns. And he arrives at conferences with a presentation, a projector, and the quiet intensity of a man who believes the entire geological establishment has been looking at half a million holes in the ground and seeing the wrong thing.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-17)</sup>

When Cottrell took the stage at the **Cosmic Summit 2023** in Asheville, he opened not with a theory but with a question directed at the audience: "Who's ready to talk about some Carolina Bays?" The response was enthusiastic. The **Carolina Bays** are one of the signature topics of the catastrophist community, a piece of terrestrial geomorphology that has been folded into the alternative narrative of ancient cataclysm since the 1930s. Cottrell's approach, however, was not the sweeping cosmic drama that the conference circuit usually delivers. He showed photographs of individual bays he had visited in person. He described the soil under his boots. He noted where the textbook descriptions matched the field observations and where they did not. His grandmother, he mentioned, was from the Azores, and he had recently taken his family to **Pico Island**, the tallest peak in the Atlantic chain that some alternative researchers have identified as a candidate for **Plato**'s Atlantis. The biographical detail was offered casually, but it placed Cottrell at a specific intersection: a credentialed geoscientist, a field worker, a family man, and someone whose personal curiosity had led him to the same questions that drew everyone else in the room.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-17)</sup>

The holes are real. From the air, flying over eastern North Carolina or **South Carolina** at altitude, they are unmistakable: elliptical depressions, varying in size from a few dozen meters to several kilometers across, scattered across the **Atlantic Coastal Plain** from southern New Jersey to northern Florida, with the densest concentration in the Carolinas. There are approximately 500,000 of them. Most are shallow, their rims barely elevated above the surrounding terrain. Many have been plowed over, drained, ditched, or developed into farmland; a few retain standing water and function as seasonal wetlands, supporting specialized plant communities adapted to the wet-dry cycle. Their most striking characteristic is not their shape but their orientation. The vast majority are aligned northwest to southeast, with their long axes pointing in a direction that varies by only a few degrees across the entire distribution. From thirty thousand feet, they look like a swarm of raindrops frozen in mid-splash across a continent-sized window, all blown by the same wind.

**The Oriented Lakes** is the phrase that captures the central problem. Individual elliptical depressions occur everywhere; wind, water, karst dissolution, and permafrost thaw carve basins into soft sediment in every climate zone on Earth. But when half a million of them point the same direction, the pattern demands a mechanism. The question is not what Carolina Bays are. They are depressions, filled with organic sediment, hosting wetland ecology, slowly silting up and disappearing under farmland and suburban development. The question is why they are all facing the same way.

The name is misleading. "Bays" suggests water, and indeed many of the features contain seasonal or permanent wetlands, but the majority are dry for most of the year and are recognized primarily by the subtle elevation of their rims above the surrounding terrain and by the characteristic vegetation, often **pocosins** (evergreen shrub bogs) or **Carolina bay forests**, that fills their interiors. The name also suggests the Carolinas, but the distribution extends far beyond: bays have been mapped in Georgia, Virginia, Maryland, Delaware, New Jersey, and northeastern Florida. The densest concentration is in **Bladen County, North Carolina**, where they cover a significant fraction of the landscape and can be seen on any satellite image, a field of pale ovals in the dark green of the coastal forest, all tilted at the same angle, like an aerial photograph of a school of fish frozen mid-turn.

### The 1933 Hypothesis

The first people to notice the pattern were not geologists on the ground but pilots in the air. Aerial photography was a new technology in the 1930s, and the bays, invisible at ground level (where they are simply bogs and ponds in flat coastal forest), were spectacular from altitude. In 1933, **Frank A. Melton** and **William Schriever**, geologists at the **University of Oklahoma**, published a paper in the *Journal of Geology* proposing that the Carolina Bays were the result of a meteor shower. The hypothesis was straightforward: a swarm of extraterrestrial fragments had struck the eastern seaboard at a low angle from the northwest, gouging out elliptical craters whose orientations reflected the common trajectory of the incoming debris. The shallow depth and low rim height of the bays were explained by the low angle of impact; the consistent orientation was explained by the common entry vector; the geographic distribution was explained by the fragmentation of a parent body into a wide debris field.

The hypothesis was elegant, visually compelling, and consistent with the aerial photographs that had just become widely available. It was also the only hypothesis on the table in 1933 that explained the orientation, which was the feature that had prompted the investigation in the first place. The paper was widely discussed. It entered the popular literature. It gave the Carolina Bays a narrative: these were impact scars, the fingerprints of a cosmic bombardment, half a million craters recording a rain of fire from the northwest.

For several decades, the impact hypothesis was the default explanation in textbooks and popular science writing. Then the laboratory tests came back, and they came back negative on every count.

The impact hypothesis predicted specific physical evidence, the kind of markers that separate a real impact structure from a hole in the ground. It predicted **shocked quartz**, the high-pressure mineral polymorph that forms only under the extreme pressures of hypervelocity impact (pressures exceeding roughly 10 gigapascals) and that is the definitive fingerprint of impact cratering. Shocked quartz confirmed the **Chicxulub crater** as the dinosaur-killing asteroid. It confirmed the **Ries crater** in Germany, the **Sudbury basin** in Canada, and every other verified impact structure on Earth. At the Carolina Bays: none. Zero.

It predicted **coesite** and **stishovite**, the ultra-high-pressure polymorphs of silica that form only under impact conditions and that persist in the geological record for millions of years. At the Carolina Bays: none.

It predicted meteoritic material: fragments of the impactor, or at minimum, a geochemical signature (iridium anomaly, siderophile enrichment, osmium-isotope excursion) indicating the delivery of extraterrestrial material. At the Carolina Bays: none.

It predicted gravity and magnetic anomalies beneath the rims, the signatures of disrupted subsurface rock that every impact structure produces. At the Carolina Bays: none.

The **South Carolina Department of Natural Resources**, in its official geological summary, states the conclusion flatly: the impact hypothesis for Carolina Bays has been conclusively rejected on multiple grounds, including the absence of shocked quartz, the absence of high-pressure mineral phases, the absence of meteoritic material, and the absence of geophysical anomalies. The bays are morphologically too shallow and their rims too low for their diameter to be consistent with impact structures at any velocity or angle.

And then there is the problem that destroyed the hypothesis more completely than any missing mineral. If the bays were formed by a single shower event, a one-time catastrophic rain of fragments, all of them should date to the same period. They do not. Radiocarbon dating and **optically stimulated luminescence** (**OSL**) dating of bay sediments and rim deposits have established that the bays formed at different times. **Andrew Ivester**, a geomorphologist at the **University of West Georgia**, and his colleagues published OSL dates from multiple bay sites across the Carolinas showing formation ages ranging from 40,000 to 60,000 years before present for some rim deposits, while other bays yielded ages of only 15,000 to 20,000 years. **Mark Brooks** of the **Savannah River Archaeological Research Program** documented bay sediment ages at **Flamingo Bay** in South Carolina that demonstrated phases of active formation interspersed with periods of stability spanning thousands of years. **Singletary Lake** in Bladen County, one of the few bays with a continuous sediment record, yielded basal radiocarbon dates of approximately 32,000 years before present, while **Jones Lake**, barely twenty kilometers away, produced dates several thousand years younger. The spread in formation dates is not a minor scatter around a central value that might be attributed to dating uncertainty. It is a distribution that rules out any single event as the cause, whether that event is a meteor shower, a comet fragment swarm, or any other one-time catastrophe.

The morphological argument is equally damaging. Impact craters, regardless of size, have a characteristic depth-to-diameter ratio that reflects the physics of hypervelocity excavation. Small simple craters on Earth have depth-to-diameter ratios of roughly 1:5 to 1:3. Carolina Bays have depth-to-diameter ratios of 1:100 or less: they are, in engineering terms, barely dimples. Their rims are too low, their floors too flat, and their profiles too gentle to be consistent with any impact process at any velocity, including the low velocities of Carlson's proposed ice-ejecta scenario. The physics of crater formation does not permit structures this shallow to form by impact.

This is the datum that separates the Carolina Bay debate from most other controversies in this book. The impact hypothesis is not underdetermined by the evidence. It is contradicted by the evidence, on multiple independent grounds, using standard geological methods that are not in dispute.

### The Wind and the Water

If not impact, then what? The mainstream geological consensus, refined over decades and supported by a 2024 paper in the journal ***Marine Geology***, is that Carolina Bays are **aeolian-lacustrine** features: shallow depressions formed and shaped by the interaction of wind and water over thousands of years.

The mechanism is not exotic, and it has been observed in real time at latitudes far from the Carolinas. During the late **Pleistocene**, the **Wisconsin glaciation** locked so much water in continental ice sheets that sea level dropped roughly 120 meters below its present position. The Atlantic Coastal Plain was broader by tens of kilometers, drier, and more exposed to wind than it is today. Vegetation was sparser. The surface was flat, sandy, and vulnerable to aeolian processes that are largely suppressed in the modern forested landscape. Strong **southwesterly winds**, whose direction has been independently reconstructed from the orientations of **parabolic sand dunes** in river valleys across the same region, blew across these broad, flat, poorly vegetated surfaces for thousands of years.

Where shallow depressions existed, from dissolution of underlying limestone, from subtle differences in sediment compaction, from the natural irregular topography of any flat terrain, wind-driven water circulation within the depression scoured sediment preferentially in the direction of the prevailing wind. The mechanism is simple fluid dynamics: wind pushes water to the downwind shore of a shallow lake, where it piles up, erodes the bank, and returns along the bottom to the upwind side. Over centuries and millennia, this asymmetric circulation elongates the basin in the wind direction, building up a rim of eroded material on the downwind margin and creating the characteristic elliptical, oriented shape.

This is not a theoretical reconstruction. It is a process observed in real time, today, at **oriented thermokarst lakes** in the **Arctic**, where shallow lakes on flat permafrost terrain develop the same elliptical, consistently oriented morphology in response to prevailing wind. The process has been modeled computationally, documented by satellite imagery, and described in dozens of peer-reviewed papers. The Arctic thermokarst analogs have the same length-to-width ratios as Carolina Bays, the same asymmetric rim profiles, the same relationship between long-axis orientation and the direction of prevailing wind, and the same internal sediment distribution. The only difference is latitude and substrate: permafrost in the Arctic, unconsolidated sand and silt in the Carolinas. The physics is identical.

The consistent orientation, the feature that first attracted Melton and Schriever's attention in 1933 and that continues to fascinate the alternative community nine decades later, is not evidence of an extraterrestrial origin. It is evidence of a prevailing wind direction. The bays point the same way because the wind blew the same way, steadily, for thousands of years, across a flat, exposed, poorly vegetated coastal plain. This is the kind of explanation that feels disappointing after the drama of a meteor shower, which is perhaps why it has never gained traction outside of the geological literature.

### The Thirty Thousand Bays

The wind hypothesis explains the orientation. It also explains a feature that the impact hypothesis cannot: the spectrum of bay ages.

If half a million bays were formed by a single catastrophic event, whether a meteor shower or an ice-sheet ejecta field, they should all be the same age, or at least cluster tightly around a single date. The dating data show the opposite. A 2011 study by **Daniel Fain** and colleagues at the **University of Cincinnati** applied OSL dating to four Carolina Bay sites in South Carolina and found rim construction ages ranging from approximately 80,000 years before present to less than 12,000, with the youngest features showing active modification well into the Holocene. **White Lake** in Bladen County has a radiocarbon chronology extending to roughly 35,000 years before present, while adjacent smaller bays show no evidence of activity before the late Pleistocene. The spread in formation ages is not noise; it is signal. It tells a story of ongoing landscape evolution, of depressions forming, filling, re-forming, and migrating across the Coastal Plain over tens of thousands of years as climate oscillated, vegetation advanced and retreated, and the wind kept blowing from the southwest.

The aeolian model also explains distribution. The formation process is not instantaneous; it operates over centuries and millennia, with individual bays forming, filling with sediment, stabilizing under vegetation, and occasionally re-activating when drought or fire reduces the vegetation cover and exposes the basin floor to renewed wind erosion. This cyclical formation-and-fill dynamic explains why the bays span a wide range of ages and why some bays appear "fresh" (well-defined rims, open water, little infill) while others are barely detectable (rims eroded, basin filled with peat and organic sediment, vegetation cover complete). The bays are not a single generation. They are many generations, some still forming, some filled and forgotten.

The model also explains why the bays are concentrated where they are. The Coastal Plain from New Jersey to Florida is underlain by unconsolidated Cretaceous and Tertiary sediments, sands and silts and clays, that are easily eroded by wind and water. The terrain is flat. The water table is high, which means that any depression in the surface fills with water and becomes a shallow lake, which is exactly the starting condition that the wind-scour process requires. The Gulf Coast, which has similar flat terrain but different substrates (heavier clay soils, higher organic content, denser vegetation cover), does not have the same density of bays. This is exactly what the aeolian model predicts: the process requires a specific combination of substrate, topography, vegetation, and wind, and the Atlantic Coastal Plain of the Carolinas provides that combination in optimal form.

The bay rims are asymmetric, higher and better-defined on the southeastern (downwind) margin than on the northwestern (upwind) margin. This is the signature of wind-driven sediment transport: material scoured from the basin is deposited on the downwind rim, building it up over time. The pattern is identical to the rim asymmetry observed in oriented thermokarst lakes in Alaska and Siberia. If the bays were impact craters, the rim should be symmetric or, for oblique impacts, higher on the uprange side (northwest, in the Melton-Schriever scenario), which is the opposite of what is observed.

None of these observations is new. They have been in the geological literature for decades. They are not disputed by any researcher who has examined the bay sediments in the field. The impact hypothesis persists not because it accounts for the data better than the wind hypothesis, but because it accounts for the imagination better. A meteor shower is dramatic. Wind erosion is not. In the popular literature, drama wins.

### The Carlson Variation

Carlson ([Chapter 4](04-the-cubit-coincidence.md)) does not accept the wind explanation. His argument is not the same as Melton and Schriever's original impact hypothesis, and the distinction is important.

Carlson does not propose a conventional meteorite shower. He proposes that the Carolina Bays were formed by **secondary impacts**: ejecta from a primary comet or asteroid impact into the **Laurentide Ice Sheet** at the onset of the Younger Dryas, approximately 12,800 years ago. In this scenario, a large body (or swarm of bodies) struck the two-kilometer-thick ice sheet covering most of northern North America. The impact blasted enormous volumes of ice and water into suborbital trajectories. These fragments, ranging from house-sized ice blocks to smaller debris, followed ballistic arcs southward and struck the soft, saturated sediments of the Atlantic Coastal Plain, creating the elliptical depressions. The consistent orientation reflects the common trajectory from the impact zone (somewhere in the Great Lakes region or further north) to the landing zone (the southeastern United States). The shallow depth and low rims are explained by the impactors being ice and water rather than rock or metal: soft projectiles hitting soft ground.

The hypothesis is creative, and it elegantly solves several problems that destroyed the original Melton-Schriever proposal. Secondary impacts from ice-sheet ejecta would not produce shocked quartz, because the impact pressures of ice-on-sediment are far below the threshold for shock metamorphism. They would not leave meteoritic iron or iridium anomalies, because the projectiles were ice, not extraterrestrial rock. They would not create gravity anomalies, because the impacts were shallow, into unconsolidated sediment, at velocities far below those of primary cosmic impacts. The absence of traditional impact markers, which decisively refuted the original hypothesis, is exactly what one would expect if the impactors were chunks of glacial ice.

But the hypothesis introduces a problem of its own, and it is the same problem that killed the original: timing. If the bays were formed by ejecta from a single impact event, they should all date to approximately 12,800 years ago, the proposed date of the Younger Dryas impact. The OSL and radiocarbon data show they do not. The formation dates span tens of thousands of years.

Carlson and his supporters have questioned the reliability of the dating methods as applied to bay sediments, arguing that the dynamic, water-saturated, biologically active environment of the bays could reset luminescence signals (by exposing buried grains to sunlight during bioturbation or erosion) and contaminate carbon samples (by introducing younger organic material into older deposits). This is not an unreasonable objection in principle. OSL dating in wet, reworked sediments is complicated, and there are well-documented cases where bioturbation has produced anomalously young ages in known-age deposits. In practice, the dates come from multiple independent laboratories using multiple techniques (both OSL and radiocarbon), and the distribution of ages is too wide to attribute to systematic error in a single direction. The dating problem has not been resolved in Carlson's favor, and it remains the strongest argument against any single-event formation model.

### The LiDAR Revolution

One development has changed the Carolina Bay debate in ways that both sides are still absorbing: the availability of high-resolution **LiDAR** topographic data.

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) uses aircraft-mounted lasers to penetrate forest canopy and map the ground surface at centimeter-scale resolution. The **USGS** and state geological surveys have been acquiring LiDAR coverage of the eastern United States for flood-mapping and coastal-erosion purposes, and the resulting digital elevation models have revealed Carolina Bays in extraordinary detail, including thousands of previously unmapped features that were invisible on aerial photographs because they had been completely overgrown by forest or because their rims were too subtle to detect without centimeter-scale elevation data.

The LiDAR data have done two things. First, they have dramatically increased the known count of Carolina Bays. The commonly cited figure of 500,000 may be a significant undercount; some researchers using LiDAR have identified bays that are barely a few meters across, features that would never have been noticed on even the highest-resolution aerial photograph but that are clearly present in the LiDAR terrain models, with the same elliptical shape and the same orientation as their larger siblings. The continuum of bay sizes, from tens of meters to several kilometers, suggests a scale-invariant process, one that operates the same way at every size, which is characteristic of aeolian and hydrological processes and not of impact cratering (where the relationship between impactor size and crater diameter follows a very different scaling law).

Second, LiDAR has revealed that some bays overlap, with one bay partially superimposed on another, their rims intersecting. This is exactly what the aeolian model predicts: bays form, fill with sediment, and new bays form on top of or adjacent to the old ones over thousands of years. It is difficult to reconcile with a single-event formation model, in which all bays should be contemporaneous and none should overlap.

Carlson and his supporters have used the LiDAR data for their own purposes, pointing to features in the terrain that they interpret as ejecta flow patterns or secondary impact strings. The same data set supports both interpretations depending on what the viewer expects to see, which is a characteristic of evidence that does not discriminate between hypotheses, not because both hypotheses are equally good, but because the evidence, however detailed, is insufficient to force a unique conclusion. LiDAR maps the surface. It does not date it. It does not analyze its composition. It does not determine whether a rimmed oval in the terrain was scoured by wind or gouged by ice. For that, someone has to go to the site with a shovel and a soil auger and a luminescence dating laboratory. Cottrell is one of the very few people doing that.

### The Field and the Theory

Cottrell, at the Cosmic Summit, presented his own field observations with a level of detail that distinguished his work from the more sweeping claims typical of the conference circuit. He had measured rim elevations at specific bays, documented sediment compositions with core samples, and mapped individual features at GPS precision. He showed photographs of bays with unusually well-defined rims and steep interior walls that did not match the textbook description of wind-scoured basins. He showed bays that had been bisected by road cuts, exposing their internal structure in cross-section. His approach was empirical rather than theoretical: less interested in defending a grand hypothesis than in establishing what the physical features actually looked like when you stood in them with a transit and a soil auger.

This gave his presentations a groundedness that the Carolina Bay debate often lacks. Most participants on both sides argue from aerial photographs, satellite imagery, and theoretical models. Cottrell argued from the mud on his boots. Whether his observations ultimately support, complicate, or refute the mainstream model is less important than the fact that he was collecting data in a field where data collection is rare. The Carolina Bays have been debated for ninety years, and the number of researchers who have done systematic, bay-by-bay fieldwork on them can be counted on two hands.

The distinction between the two arguments that converge on the Carolina Bays is worth stating explicitly, because the confusion between them has generated more heat than light for nearly a century.

The first argument is geological: what process formed these features? Here the evidence, as of 2024, strongly favors the aeolian-lacustrine model. The wind hypothesis explains the orientation, the morphology, the rim structure, and the varying ages. It does not explain every individual bay (some have features that fit the wind model less comfortably than others), but it explains the ensemble, which is what a geological explanation is required to do.

The second argument is cosmological: was there a major impact event at the onset of the Younger Dryas? This is a separate question, examined in Chapter 18, with its own body of evidence. The temptation to link the two questions, to treat Carolina Bays as "proof" of the **Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis**, has been powerful and persistent. Carlson's secondary-impact model is the most sophisticated version of this linkage. But even if the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis proves correct (a question that remains genuinely open), it would not follow that Carolina Bays were formed by that impact. The bays' formation dates do not cluster at 12,800 BP. Their morphology is consistent with known aeolian processes. The connection between the two is a narrative association, not an evidentiary one.

### The Nebraska Analogs

A further piece of evidence against the impact hypothesis comes from an unexpected quarter: the landscape of the Great Plains.

In western **Nebraska** and eastern **Colorado**, a network of oriented depressions similar in morphology to Carolina Bays has been documented in the **Sand Hills** region. These depressions, called **blowout basins** or oriented lakes, have the same elliptical shape, the same consistent orientation (in this case, parallel to the prevailing westerly wind), and the same shallow, rimmed profile. They are smaller on average than the Carolina Bays, but the morphological similarity is unmistakable. No one has ever proposed that the Nebraska blowouts were formed by a meteor shower. Their aeolian origin is not disputed.

The existence of the Nebraska analogs is a problem for the impact hypothesis because it demonstrates that the wind mechanism can produce the exact features, elliptical shape, consistent orientation, elevated downwind rim, that are supposed to be diagnostic of impact. If wind can make these features in Nebraska, the burden shifts to the impact proponents to explain why the same features in the Carolinas require a different mechanism. The response has been to argue that the Carolina Bays are morphologically distinct from the Nebraska blowouts in ways that are not captured by simple measurements of shape and orientation. Perhaps. But the distinction has not been established by systematic morphometric comparison, and until it is, the Nebraska analogs stand as a control group that the impact hypothesis cannot easily dismiss.

### The Unsolved Details

The alternative community's attachment to Carolina Bays as evidence of catastrophic impact has a consequence that is rarely discussed: it distracts from what is genuinely unusual about the bays.

Half a million consistently oriented depressions across the eastern seaboard of the United States represent, regardless of their origin, one of the largest-scale geomorphological patterns on Earth. Their relationship to Pleistocene climate, wind patterns, vegetation cover, and hydrological systems is incompletely understood. Their distribution, why the Carolinas and not, say, the Gulf Coast, which had similar flat terrain and similar wind exposure, has not been fully modeled. Their ecology, as seasonal wetlands supporting specialized plant and animal communities including endemic species found nowhere else, has been degraded by agriculture and development with minimal documentation. Some of these wetlands are vanishing, plowed under or drained for field crops, and the geological and biological record they contain is being destroyed faster than it is being studied.

The geological establishment's position on Carolina Bays is almost certainly correct in its broad outlines. The wind-and-water model explains the orientation, the morphology, and the varying dates. The impact hypothesis fails on shocked quartz, fails on dating, and fails on every geochemical test that has been applied to it. But "almost certainly correct in its broad outlines" is not the same as "completely understood," and the confidence with which some geologists dismiss any further investigation of the bays has the quality of a conclusion reached one step before the finish line. The bays are not evidence of a catastrophic impact. They may be evidence of Pleistocene climatic and hydrological processes that are more complex, more variable, and more interesting than the current model fully captures.

Cottrell, driving to his bogs on weekends, measuring rims that no one else is measuring, photographing features that no one else is photographing, is doing the kind of work that does not resolve grand debates but that makes grand debates honest.

The ecological dimension of the bays deserves mention, because it is being destroyed faster than it is being studied. Carolina Bays that retain their natural vegetation support plant communities found nowhere else in the region: pocosins, bay forests dominated by **loblolly bay** (*Gordonia lasianthus*), **sweet bay** (*Magnolia virginiana*), and **red bay** (*Persea borbonia*), and open-water bays hosting populations of the **Venus flytrap** (*Dionaea muscipula*), which is native to a narrow range in the Carolinas and grows preferentially in the nutrient-poor, acidic soils of bay interiors. The flytrap's global distribution is, in effect, a map of Carolina Bay habitat. As the bays have been drained, plowed, and developed, the flytrap's range has contracted proportionally. The ecological loss is real and irreversible, and it is proceeding with minimal documentation.

The sediment cores from undisturbed bays also contain pollen records, charcoal layers, and isotopic data that provide a continuous record of local climate and vegetation change spanning thousands of years. These records are complementary to the better-known records from lake sediments and peat bogs, and they cover a geographic range (the southeast Atlantic Coastal Plain) that is underrepresented in the Quaternary paleoclimate literature. The scientific value of the bays as paleoecological archives has been recognized in the academic literature but has not generated the kind of systematic coring and analysis that the resource warrants. The attention has gone to the impact debate, which is photogenic and controversial, rather than to the paleoecology, which is not.

The Carolina Bays are not a mystery. They are a solved problem with unsolved details, a geomorphological pattern whose broad outlines are well understood and whose fine structure has barely been examined. The difference between those two descriptions is the difference between scientific closure and scientific complacency, and the alternative community's insistence on seeing craters where there are basins has, ironically, made it harder to fund the kind of careful, undramatic, weekend-by-weekend fieldwork that would map those details and extract the real information that the bays contain.

Half a million oriented lakes. No shocked quartz. No meteoritic iron. No single formation date. The wind, it turns out, is sufficient. Whether it is complete is a different question, and the man most likely to answer it teaches high school in Georgia.
