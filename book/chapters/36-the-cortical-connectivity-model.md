## Chapter 36: The Cortical Connectivity Model – Consciousness, Psychedelics, and Ancient Knowledge
In the spring of 1953, a thin, restless American named **William S. Burroughs** stepped off a cargo plane in **Pucallpa**, a small city on the banks of the **Ucayali River** in the Peruvian Amazon, and immediately felt his fortunes change. He had spent the previous months in Colombia, chasing a psychoactive brew called **yage** through a succession of failures that would have been comic if they had happened to someone else. He had obtained **caapi vine** from the botanical collection at the **Institute** in Bogota, subsisted by stealing laboratory-grade alcohol and drinking it mixed into bottles of **Pepsi** at sidewalk cafes, and managed to produce an extract of the vine that, after weeks of effort, merely made him sleepy. The brew he drank with a shaman near **Puerto Asís** produced nausea, vomiting, and only the faintest shimmer of visionary activity. His visa was about to expire. His letters to **Allen Ginsberg** had become increasingly despondent.

The manager of the **Hotel Pucallpa**, where Burroughs checked in upon arrival, knew exactly what his guest was looking for. In this region, yage was called **ayahuasca**, and the manager connected Burroughs to a local shaman named **Saboya** who ran small group ceremonies on the outskirts of town. When Burroughs arrived at Saboya's hut, six locals were already gathered under a tree, waiting for the ceremony to begin. The young shaman brought out the brew. Burroughs drank it. And for the first time, after months of failed preparations and disappointing extracts, the visions arrived.

What Burroughs described in his subsequent letters to Ginsberg, published in 1963 as ***The Yage Letters***, was not the gentle kaleidoscopic display that Westerners sometimes associate with psychedelics. He reported seeing composite animal-insect forms, landscapes of alien geometry, and autonomous entities that he called "Soulless Insect People," beings that appeared to operate according to their own logic and to regard him with something between curiosity and indifference. Burroughs was a man who had accidentally shot his wife in a drunken game of William Tell, survived years of heroin addiction, and spent time in the company of people who made his fictional characters seem mild. He was not easily frightened. The ayahuasca experience, by his own account, left him shaken in a way that nothing else in his considerable catalogue of extreme experiences had managed.

Burroughs could not have known, drinking that brew in a hut outside Pucallpa in 1953, that the same molecule would one day be studied using brain-imaging equipment that had not yet been invented, by researchers at institutions that had not yet been founded, in a scientific context that would not exist for another six decades. He could not have known that his "Soulless Insect People" would turn out to be a consistent feature of the experience, reported independently by thousands of users across different countries and decades, or that this consistency would become one of the central puzzles of consciousness research. He was just a writer looking for new states of mind, and he found one that neither he nor anyone else could adequately explain.

Seventy years later, the brew that Burroughs encountered has become the subject of brain-imaging studies at **Imperial College London**, pharmacological modeling at **Oxford University**, clinical trials at **Johns Hopkins University**, and a cultural phenomenon that draws tens of thousands of Western participants to Amazonian retreat centers every year. The question that Burroughs could not have known to ask, and that science is only now beginning to address, is deceptively simple: what is actually happening in the brain when someone drinks ayahuasca?


### The Molecule in the Scanner

The active compound in ayahuasca is **N,N-dimethyltryptamine**, universally known as **DMT**. It is one of the simplest psychoactive molecules in nature: a tryptamine with two methyl groups, structurally similar to **serotonin**, the neurotransmitter that regulates mood, perception, and sleep. DMT is found in hundreds of plant species across multiple continents, in the venom of certain toads, and, in trace amounts, in the human body itself, where it is synthesized by the enzyme **tryptophan decarboxylase** and where its endogenous function remains unknown. The molecule was first synthesized in 1931 by the Canadian chemist **Richard Manske**, but its psychoactive properties were not recognized until 1956, when Hungarian psychiatrist **Stephen Szara** decided to inject himself with it.

Szara's decision had a Cold War backstory. He had written to **Sandoz Laboratories** in Switzerland, requesting a sample of **LSD-25** for psychiatric research, and had been refused. Sandoz, which held the patent on LSD, declined to ship the compound behind the Iron Curtain. Szara, undeterred, turned to DMT, which could be synthesized from readily available precursors without requiring materials from the West. He prepared a solution, injected it intramuscularly in his office at the **Budapest Institute**, and waited.

Within minutes, Szara experienced rapidly moving geometric patterns that gave way to fully formed visual scenes, autonomous entities, and a subjective sense that the experience was not a distortion of ordinary reality but access to a different reality entirely. The effect lasted approximately fifteen minutes, then vanished completely, leaving Szara alert, cognitively intact, and in possession of an experience that his training in mid-twentieth-century psychiatry had not remotely prepared him for. He published his findings in a Hungarian medical journal. The paper attracted modest attention and then disappeared into the growing literature on psychedelic compounds that, within fifteen years, would be effectively banned from research by the international drug-control regime that followed the 1971 **UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances**. DMT, along with LSD, psilocybin, and mescaline, was classified as a Schedule I controlled substance, and clinical research came to an abrupt halt that would last, in most countries, for over two decades.

**Rick Strassman**, a psychiatrist at the **University of New Mexico**, broke the moratorium in 1990. He obtained the first US government approval in over twenty years to administer a psychedelic to human volunteers, and between 1990 and 1995, he injected sixty participants with varying doses of intravenous DMT while meticulously recording their subjective reports in clinical interviews. The results, published in his 2001 book ***DMT: The Spirit Molecule***, were consistent across participants and strange by any measure. At threshold doses, volunteers reported geometric patterns and emotional intensification. At higher doses, the reports entered territory that clinical language struggled to contain. Volunteers described being transported to fully realized environments populated by autonomous entities that communicated, that appeared to have intentions distinct from the human participant, that displayed consistent behavioral patterns across independent reports, and that, in approximately forty-five percent of cases, were described as feeling "more real than reality." Several volunteers independently reported that the entities seemed pleased that humans had "discovered this technology," a phrasing that Strassman found striking because it came from multiple participants who had no knowledge of each other's reports.

Strassman's study was rigorous in design but limited in one critical respect: he could measure subjective reports but not the neural activity underlying them. The volunteers described astonishing experiences, but the brain remained a black box during the crucial fifteen minutes. The technology to look inside it while a subject was experiencing the DMT state, with sufficient temporal resolution to track the rapid changes that DMT produces, did not yet exist in a form compatible with the drug's extremely fast onset and short duration. The fMRI scanners available in the 1990s required subjects to lie motionless inside a magnetic bore for extended periods, and the temporal resolution (the speed at which the scanner can detect changes) was measured in seconds, not milliseconds. DMT's peak effects occur within thirty to sixty seconds of injection and resolve within fifteen minutes. The entire experience could begin and end between two fMRI data points.

Beyond the imaging limitation, Strassman confronted a phenomenological puzzle that his clinical framework could not resolve. The experiences his volunteers described did not resemble anything in the existing psychiatric literature. Schizophrenic hallucinations are typically auditory, fragmented, and experienced as intrusions into ordinary reality. LSD and psilocybin produce distortions of existing perception, walls breathing, colors intensifying, faces morphing. DMT did something categorically different. It appeared to replace the perceptual world entirely, substituting a fully realized alternative environment with its own spatial properties, its own inhabitants, and its own apparent physics. Strassman's volunteers were not describing a modification of reality. They were describing a different reality, entered abruptly and exited just as abruptly, with the ordinary world waiting unchanged on the other side.

The question of what the brain was doing during those fifteen minutes would not be answerable for another two decades.

That changed in 2019.


### Seeing with Eyes Shut

The team at the **Centre for Psychedelic Research** at Imperial College London, led by **Robin Carhart-Harris** and including PhD student **Chris Timmermann** and French computational neuroscientist **Andrea Alamia**, designed an experiment that would become one of the most cited studies in psychedelic neuroscience. Thirteen volunteers received intravenous DMT at various doses, the highest being 20 milligrams (similar to Strassman's medium dose), while wearing **EEG** (electroencephalography) caps that measured electrical activity across the cortex in real time with millisecond precision.

The rationale was straightforward but the implications were not. When the human brain processes visual information from the eyes, electrical waves flow from the back of the cortex (where the **primary visual cortex**, V1, is located) toward the front and sides of the brain. These forward-flowing waves carry what neuroscientists call **prediction errors**: information about the external world that does not match the brain's existing model, which the cortex uses to update and refine its representation of reality. When the eyes are closed, these forward-flowing waves diminish sharply, because the primary visual cortex has no new sensory data to process. Backward-flowing waves, carrying the brain's existing model predictions down toward the sensory cortices, continue uninterrupted. This bidirectional flow, predictions going down and prediction errors going up, is the fundamental mechanism by which the brain constructs its model of the world.

The experiment's central question was: what happens to these directional waves under DMT?

The answer was startling. Timmermann's team decomposed the EEG signals into their different frequency components and monitored waves flowing in both directions simultaneously. When subjects lay with their eyes closed before receiving the drug, the expected pattern appeared: backward-flowing (model-prediction) waves were dominant, and forward-flowing (sensory-processing) waves were minimal. When subjects opened their eyes to view an image on a screen, the pattern reversed: forward-flowing waves increased sharply as the cortex absorbed visual information. Normal, predictable, exactly as the neuroscience textbooks described.

But when subjects lay with their eyes closed and were injected with DMT, something happened that the textbooks had not predicted. Almost as soon as the drug reached the brain, mere seconds post-injection, downward-flowing waves sharply decreased and upward-flowing waves increased. The pattern was, in the researchers' carefully worded conclusion, "almost indistinguishable from that observed when a subject who had not taken the drug opened their eyes and viewed an image on a screen." The brains of volunteers who were lying in a quiet room, with their eyes closed, with no visual stimulation of any kind, were processing information as though they were seeing something real.

Timmermann's team concluded that DMT must "engage the visual apparatus in a fashion that is consistent with actual exogenously driven visual perception." Every word in that sentence was chosen with care. It does not say that DMT allows access to an external reality. It says that the brain, under DMT, behaves as if it is receiving external visual information, even when the eyes are closed and there is nothing to see. The distinction between these two statements is the entire distance between neuroscience and metaphysics.

The Imperial College finding was not isolated. Spanish pharmacist **Jordi Riba**, who had spent over a decade studying ayahuasca's effects on neural activity at his laboratory in **Barcelona**, had shown a similar pattern using ayahuasca rather than pure DMT: activity in the rear EEG electrodes (over the primary visual cortex) exerted a stronger influence on frontal electrodes than vice versa, peaking around two hours post-ingestion as the intensity of visual effects and blood DMT levels reached their maximum. Brazilian neuroscientist **Draulio de Araujo**, using **fMRI** (which provides higher spatial resolution at the cost of temporal precision), placed subjects in a brain scanner after administering ayahuasca and measured activity in the primary visual cortex directly. Before administration, visual cortex activation was strong with eyes open and weak with eyes closed, exactly as expected. After ayahuasca, activity in the visual cortex was just as high with eyes closed as with eyes open. De Araujo titled his paper with a question: were ayahuasca visions "seeing with the eyes shut"?

The data from three independent labs, using three different drugs (pure DMT, whole ayahuasca brew, and ayahuasca extract), three different brain-imaging techniques (EEG, fMRI, and MEG), and subjects from three different countries, converged on the same finding. Under the influence of DMT, the human brain processes information through its visual cortex as though visual information is arriving from the outside. The researchers noted that this pattern is qualitatively different from the brain activity observed during psychotic hallucinations in schizophrenic patients, where activity in the primary visual cortex is absent and the imagery appears to be generated from hippocampal memory systems flowing top-down. DMT hallucinations look, from the brain's perspective, less like an internal malfunction and more like the processing of an external signal.

The question of what is producing that signal, if "signal" is even the right word, is where the science ends and the speculation begins. Riba suggested the activity was merely "system noise" spontaneously generated by the primary visual cortex, but this explanation sits uncomfortably with the complexity, coherence, and structural sophistication of the experiences that subjects describe. System noise does not typically build worlds.

**Andrew Gallimore**, a British pharmacologist and computational neuroscientist who would later collaborate with both Strassman and the Imperial College team, articulated the problem with precision in ***Death by Astonishment***, written with Hancock ([Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)). The brain, Gallimore argued, is fundamentally a world-building machine. Its primary function is not to passively receive sensory data but to actively construct a model of reality, updating that model continuously using prediction errors flowing up from the sensory cortices. Under normal conditions, the model is constrained by sensory input: the world the brain builds is the world you see when you open your eyes, because the prediction errors arriving from your retinas keep the model anchored to external reality. Remove the sensory input (close your eyes, enter a flotation tank, fall asleep), and the model drifts: you dream, you hallucinate, you experience the unconstrained products of the brain's generative machinery.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup>

DMT, in Gallimore's analysis, does something more radical than simply removing the constraint. It appears to redirect the brain's world-building machinery toward a different set of inputs, inputs whose origin is the central unresolved question. The brain does not merely dream under DMT. It builds, with the same crisp efficiency it brings to modeling the waking world, environments and inhabitants that have no correspondence to anything in the subject's waking life. This is the core puzzle, and it has not been solved.<sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup>


### The Neural Network Model

In 2020, a team at Oxford University published a paper in *Oxford Academic* that approached the DMT visual phenomena from a computational angle. Using neural network architectures consistent with known cortical processing, the researchers modeled the characteristic visual hallucinations reported under DMT: the spirals, tunnels, lattices, and geometric forms that users have described since the 1950s. They found that these patterns, first catalogued by German-American psychologist **Heinrich Kluver** in the 1920s and called **form constants**, could be generated by specific perturbations of the serotonergic system within the existing architecture of the visual cortex.

This is what researchers call **the cortical connectivity model**. The idea is that DMT does not introduce new information into the brain; rather, it radically reorganizes the connections between existing neural populations, breaking down the hierarchical structure that normally constrains perception and allowing normally suppressed patterns of activity to propagate unchecked across the cortex. The form constants (spirals, tunnels, lattices, cobwebs) are not arbitrary; they correspond to the intrinsic spatial organization of the primary visual cortex. The cortex generates these patterns naturally as part of its baseline activity, but under normal conditions, the hierarchical filtering system suppresses them in favor of the stable, predictable model of reality that keeps you oriented in space, able to distinguish your hand from the table it rests on, and generally functional as a biological organism.

Under DMT, the filtering fails. The cortex becomes, in Carhart-Harris's terminology, **hyperconnected**: brain regions that do not normally communicate begin exchanging information at rates far above baseline. A **2023 Imperial College brain imaging study** quantified this effect, showing that DMT substantially increases functional connectivity between regions associated with imagination, higher-order cognition, emotional processing, and visual perception. The normal boundaries between these systems, the boundaries that keep your imagination separate from your perception, your emotions separate from your visual field, your sense of self separate from your model of other minds, become porous. The result is a subjective experience of extraordinary vividness, complexity, emotional charge, and apparent autonomy.

The cortical connectivity model explains the geometric patterns (intrinsic cortical architecture made visible). It explains the sense of heightened reality (hyperconnectivity produces more vivid, more detailed, more emotionally saturated perceptual experiences than the brain normally generates). It explains the cross-cultural consistency of the experience (the cortical architecture is universal; every human brain will produce the same form constants when the same neuropharmacological perturbation is applied). It even explains why the experiences feel external rather than internally generated (the normal mechanisms by which the brain tags its own productions as "imagination" rather than "perception" are precisely the mechanisms that DMT disrupts; the sense of externality is a failure of source monitoring, not evidence of an actual external source).

What the model does not fully explain is the narrative complexity and apparent autonomy of the entity encounters. Geometric patterns can be generated by cortical perturbation. Vivid environments can be explained by hyperconnected visual and spatial processing systems. But complex, coherent, apparently intelligent beings that communicate in structured ways, display consistent behavioral patterns across thousands of independent reports spanning decades and continents, and exhibit what participants describe as emotions, intentions, and knowledge, that is a harder problem. The model can account for the form of the experience without fully accounting for its content.


### The Amazonian Technology

Hancock encountered ayahuasca in the early 2000s and subsequently made it a central element of his intellectual project. In ***Supernatural*** (2005), *America Before* (2019), and *Death by Astonishment* (his most recent book on DMT, drawing on the work of neuroscientist and pharmacologist Andrew Gallimore), Hancock argued that the ayahuasca experience provides genuine access to an external reality populated by non-human intelligences, and that ancient shamanic cultures understood this access as a technology, not a metaphor.<sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup><sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-36)</sup>

Hancock's argument draws on multiple strands of evidence, but its core claim rests on a phenomenological observation: the DMT experience is qualitatively different from dreaming, from imagination, and from other altered states of consciousness. Dreams, Hancock notes (following Gallimore's analysis), are vague, emotionally colored, draw on personal memory, and lack the crisp coherence of waking perception. DMT visions are crystalline, hyper-detailed, and bear "absolutely no apparent relationship" to the participant's personal or cultural experience. The entities encountered under DMT display behavioral patterns (welcoming the participant, seeming pleased, communicating structured information, controlling access to the space) that are consistent across independent reports from different cultures and different centuries.

Hancock draws particularly on the ethnographic work of Scottish anthropologist **Peter Gow**, who studied the **Piro people** of the Peruvian Amazon. The Piro call the entities encountered during ayahuasca ceremonies **kayiglu**, a word that means "the ones who cause visions to be seen." The kayiglu, in Piro cosmology, do not reveal themselves during the ayahuasca state; they actively generate the visions that the shaman observes. This is the Piro's own interpretive framework for their ceremonial experience. Hancock's move was to treat this indigenous interpretation as corroboration of a neuroscientific hypothesis: that the DMT state involves the direction of information into the brain by an intelligent agent. But the Piro framework and the Imperial College data are describing different things. The brain-imaging studies show that the brain processes DMT input as though it were external sensory data. The Piro cosmology asserts that the input is external. These are not the same claim. One is a measurement. The other is a metaphysical commitment. That both point in the same direction is suggestive; that Hancock treats the convergence as confirmation is the point where his argument outruns the evidence available to support it.

Israeli psychologist **Benny Shanon**, who published an analysis of over 2,500 ayahuasca reports in his 2003 book ***Antipodes of the Mind***, reached a similar conclusion with more academic caution. Shanon argued that the content of ayahuasca visions could not be adequately explained by personal memory, cultural conditioning, or **Jungian archetypes**. The collective unconscious, as conceived by **Carl Jung**, reflects universal human experience in symbolic form. But DMT visions, Shanon observed, frequently transcend both the personal and the universal, generating imagery that bears no connection to any human experience, ancestral or modern. "Appealing to either, or even both," Shanon wrote, "leaves the vast majority of the DMT experience simply unaccounted for."

Hancock, characteristically, goes further than either Gow's ethnography or Shanon's phenomenology warrants. In *America Before*, he speculated that the advanced civilization he hypothesizes for the Ice Age may have "transcended leverage and mechanical advantage and learned to manipulate matter and energy by deploying powers of consciousness that we have not yet begun to tap." This is, by Hancock's own explicit admission, a speculation that he does not attempt to prove or support with evidence. He presents it "merely for consideration." The speculation rests on premises that are themselves speculative: that an advanced Ice Age civilization existed (unsupported by archaeological evidence, as the rest of this book has documented), and that ayahuasca-like states provide access to genuine external intelligences that could teach techniques unknown to modern science (a metaphysical claim that the brain-imaging data does not require or support).

The distinction between "the brain behaves as if receiving external information" and "the brain is receiving external information" is the entire gap between neuroscience and metaphysics. The Imperial College data support the first statement. Hancock requires the second. The gap between them has not been bridged.

There is a further dimension to Hancock's argument that deserves mention because it represents a genuinely novel development in DMT research, even if Hancock's interpretation of it outruns what the evidence supports. In *Death by Astonishment*, Hancock and Gallimore describe a target-controlled intravenous infusion protocol, sometimes called **DMTx** (extended DMT), that was developed to maintain a stable brain DMT concentration above the breakthrough threshold for extended periods, potentially hours rather than minutes. The protocol was inspired by the observation that traditional ayahuasca ceremonies, which use **harmaline** and other **monoamine oxidase inhibitors** to slow DMT metabolism, extend the experience from minutes to hours but rarely achieve the peak intensity of vaporized or injected DMT. A controlled intravenous infusion, by maintaining brain DMT levels above the threshold concentration of approximately 60 nanograms per milliliter, could theoretically provide extended access to the full-intensity DMT state.

The protocol was presented at a private symposium attended by **Dennis McKenna**, Hancock, **David Luke**, Strassman, and Carhart-Harris. **Anton Bilton**, a British entrepreneur who had accrued over a hundred ayahuasca sessions and who funded the project, subsequently underwent DMT infusion sessions at Imperial College while wearing EEG electrodes. The sessions, which lasted longer than any previous controlled DMT exposure, provided neuroimaging data from the sustained DMT state for the first time. The results have not yet been fully published as of this writing, but the preliminary data reportedly confirmed and extended the findings of Timmermann's 2019 study.

The DMTx protocol is significant regardless of one's position on the ontological status of the entities. If the experience can be sustained for hours rather than minutes, the phenomenology can be studied with far greater precision, the neural correlates can be mapped with far greater resolution, and the question of what the brain is doing, whether building from internal resources or processing from an external source, becomes more tractable. The protocol brings the question closer to empirical testability, which is what distinguishes it from most of the speculation that surrounds DMT.


### The Therapeutic Reality

A **2024 Springer Nature systematic review** surveyed the accumulated clinical evidence on ayahuasca, DMT, psilocybin, and related psychedelics, confirming findings that were, by that point, well-established across multiple research programs. These compounds act primarily on **serotonin receptors** (specifically the **5-HT2A receptor**), producing altered activity in brain regions associated with visual processing, emotional regulation, self-referential thought, and introspection. They enhance **neuroplasticity**, the brain's ability to form new neural connections, an effect that persists well beyond the acute pharmacological window. And they have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in controlled clinical trials.

The clinical results are not ambiguous. **Psilocybin-assisted therapy** for treatment-resistant depression, tested at Johns Hopkins University and Imperial College London, has shown sustained improvement in a substantial percentage of patients after a single supervised session. **Ayahuasca-assisted therapy** has demonstrated efficacy for addiction and depression in trials conducted in both South America and Europe. **MDMA-assisted therapy** for post-traumatic stress disorder completed Phase 3 clinical trials in the United States, though regulatory approval remained contentious as of 2024. The neural mechanisms underlying these therapeutic effects (enhanced connectivity, increased neuroplasticity, disruption of rigid **default-mode-network** patterns associated with depression and rumination) are increasingly well-characterized by the same brain-imaging techniques that revealed DMT's effects on cortical wave dynamics.

The review also confirmed what mainstream neuroscience has consistently found: no peer-reviewed study has identified information retrieved under psychedelic states that could not have been derived from existing memory, cultural context, or pattern-recognition processes intrinsic to the brain. The subjective sense that the experience involves contact with an external intelligence is itself a known feature of strong serotonergic agonism. The same phenomenon, the felt sense of encountering an autonomous other, occurs in certain phases of REM sleep dreaming, in temporal lobe epilepsy, in psychosis, and in the hypnagogic state between waking and sleep. The felt sense of externality is neurologically generated. It is not evidence of actual externality.

This does not mean the experiences are meaningless. The therapeutic results are real, significant, and in some cases transformative. A person who emerges from a psilocybin session with their depression in remission does not need the entities to be ontologically real for the effect to be genuine. The therapeutic value of psychedelics does not depend on the metaphysics.


### The Shaman's Trade Secret

The relationship between the scientific findings and the shamanic traditions is more complex than either Hancock or his critics acknowledge. The indigenous cultures of the Amazon did not have EEG machines or fMRI scanners. They had millennia of direct, repeated, culturally structured experience with the DMT state, accumulated across hundreds of generations of practitioners who devoted their lives to navigating it. The shamanic traditions of the **Shipibo**, the **Ashuar**, the Piro, the **Matsigenka**, and dozens of other Amazonian peoples represent the largest body of experiential data on the DMT state in human history, dwarfing the few hundred clinical reports that Western science has so far accumulated.

The Western scientific response to this body of knowledge has been, characteristically, to ignore it. Shamanic reports are classified as folklore, religious belief, or cultural narrative. They are not treated as data. The implicit assumption is that only reports collected under controlled clinical conditions, with standardized questionnaires and neuroimaging equipment, qualify as evidence about the nature of the experience. This assumption is not unreasonable from an epistemological standpoint, but it does mean that the richest available dataset on the DMT experience is systematically excluded from the scientific conversation.

Hancock's project, whatever its flaws, has at least had the effect of forcing Western audiences to take the shamanic traditions seriously as observations rather than dismissing them as superstition. The Piro concept of kayiglu, the Shipibo tradition of **icaros** (healing songs sung during ayahuasca ceremonies that are said to be taught to the shaman by the plants themselves), the widespread Amazonian belief that ayahuasca provides diagnostic information about illness and social conflict: these are not theories about the DMT state. They are reports from people who have vastly more experience with it than any Western researcher.

Whether those reports describe genuine features of reality that Western science has not yet identified, or whether they describe genuine features of the brain's generative capacity that shamanic cultures interpreted through a different conceptual framework, is the question that the cortical connectivity model addresses but does not resolve.


### The Open Boundary

This chapter is the book's most speculative territory, and it is the right place to end. The book began with precision-manufactured stone vessels and ends with entities that may or may not exist in any space accessible to instruments. The trajectory is deliberate. The questions become less testable as you approach the boundary of what science can currently address, and the honest response to a less testable question is not to pretend you have an answer but to state clearly where the evidence runs out.

The scientific facts are these. DMT and ayahuasca produce profound alterations in cortical connectivity, visual processing, and subjective experience. Brain-imaging studies from three independent laboratories show activation patterns consistent with sensory processing even when no external stimuli are present. Computational models can reproduce the geometric features of the experience using known cortical architecture. Therapeutic applications of psychedelics show real efficacy for real psychiatric conditions through increasingly well-characterized neural mechanisms. No peer-reviewed study has demonstrated that information obtained under psychedelic states originates from an external source.

Hancock's claims about ayahuasca are personal, not evidentiary. He states them as personal. He has drunk ayahuasca over a hundred times and considers the experiences the most important of his life. He identifies himself explicitly as someone who believes the entities are real. These are personal convictions, openly stated. They are not evidence for his archaeological hypothesis. They are not presented as evidence for his archaeological hypothesis. When Hancock is being honest about the limits of his claims, which is most of the time, he is clear about the boundary between his experience and his evidence.

The deeper question, the one that the cortical connectivity model circles without resolving, is about the nature of consciousness itself. Why does the brain build these worlds? What are the entities? Why do they appear consistent across cultures, centuries, and pharmacological delivery methods? These are questions that neuroscience has begun to address with sophisticated tools and has not yet answered. They are questions that shamanic cultures in the Amazon, the Andes, Central America, and Africa addressed thousands of years ago, arriving at answers that Western science does not accept but also cannot fully replace with anything equally comprehensive.

The ayahuasca brew that Burroughs drank in a hut outside Pucallpa in 1953 is chemically identical to the brew that volunteers at Imperial College London drink while wearing EEG caps. The molecule has not changed. The question has not changed. The tools for investigating it have improved enormously, from Szara's lone self-experiment in Budapest to Timmermann's 64-channel EEG recordings in London. The answer remains incomplete.

The entities, whatever they are, do not appear to be in a hurry.
