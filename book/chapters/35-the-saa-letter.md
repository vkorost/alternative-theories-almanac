## Chapter 35: The SAA Letter – Institutional Gatekeeping and the Hancock-Dibble Debate
Richards was sitting in his home in the Pacific Northwest, scrolling through the aftermath of a debate that had just broken the internet, or at least the corner of the internet that cares about ancient history, when he decided to do something that almost nobody else on either side of the controversy had bothered to do. He went and read the actual letter.

The letter in question had been sent on November 30, 2022, by the Society for American Archaeology, the largest professional organization of archaeologists in North America with approximately 7,000 members, to **Ted Sarandos**, the co-CEO of Netflix. The occasion was the release of *Ancient Apocalypse*, a documentary series hosted by Hancock ([Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)) that argued for the existence of a lost Ice Age civilization responsible for seeding the world's earliest monumental architecture. The series had debuted on November 11, 2022, and immediately became one of Netflix's most-watched nonfiction programs. Within weeks, it was the platform's number-one show in several countries. The archaeological community, which had spent decades trying to ignore Hancock, found itself confronting the fact that more people had watched his documentary in its first month than had ever enrolled in an introductory archaeology course anywhere on Earth.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup>

The SAA's response was the letter. And the letter, like most institutional documents produced under pressure, said something more moderate than either Hancock's supporters or his critics claimed it said.

Richards read it line by line. Then he sat down and wrote an open letter of his own, addressed to the SAA's president and president-elect, that would outline what he believed the profession was doing wrong at a far more fundamental level than any single Netflix series could address.<sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[16](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup>

But **the SAA letter** came first.


### The Actual Words

The SAA letter to Netflix was five pages long, written in the formal, slightly stiff prose characteristic of professional organizations drafting documents by committee. It asked Netflix to reclassify *Ancient Apocalypse* as science fiction rather than documentary, and to develop an internal policy for managing content that, in the SAA's view, presented speculation as established fact. It cited more than a century of professional investigation of hundreds of Ice Age sites, noting that no archaeological evidence for an advanced global Ice Age civilization had emerged from any of them. It noted that Hancock's narrative had "a documented history of association with fringe ideas that strip credit from Indigenous peoples for their cultural heritage," a reference to the argument, advanced by some of Hancock's critics, that positing a lost civilization as the source of indigenous architectural and agricultural achievements functioned as a form of intellectual colonialism, regardless of Hancock's stated intentions.

The letter did not ask Netflix to remove the series. It did not ask Netflix to cancel Hancock's contract. It did not demand that Hancock be silenced, deplatformed, or prevented from publishing his views. It made no mention of legal action. It asked for content labeling and a review policy. The full text was, and remains, publicly available on the SAA's website, downloadable as a PDF.

This is not what happened to the letter in the public discourse. On the alternative-history side, the letter became proof that the archaeological establishment was trying to suppress Hancock's work, a coordinated institutional effort to silence a dissenting voice. Social media posts described the SAA as "trying to ban" Hancock, "censoring" his views, and "going after" an independent journalist. On the mainstream side, the letter became a reasonable professional response to misinformation, the equivalent of the American Medical Association writing to a network airing a series claiming vaccines cause autism. Both readings required ignoring portions of the text. The suppression narrative required ignoring that the letter asked for disclaimers, not removal. The reasonable-response narrative required ignoring that the letter's language about "fringe ideas" and indigenous cultural credit carried implications that went well beyond a request for a content label, implying a moral charge against Hancock that was not substantiated within the letter itself.

Richards, who read the letter with the same forensic attention he brought to ancient skulls and geological surveys, noticed what both sides had missed. The letter was a political document, not a scientific one. It was addressed to a corporate executive, not a scientific journal. Its purpose was to leverage institutional authority in a media-regulation context, to shape how a corporation categorized content for its subscribers. Whether this constituted censorship depended on your definition of the word, and people's definitions tended to correlate perfectly with their prior position on Hancock. But it was certainly not science. Science publishes counter-evidence and submits it to peer review. Institutions write letters to CEOs.

The distinction matters because the SAA's own bylaws, as Richards pointed out in his open letter, listed the education of the public as one of the organization's primary goals. If the public was turning to Hancock, to YouTube channels, to the entire ecosystem of alternative-history content rather than to professional archaeologists for information about the ancient past, the SAA letter was not an attempt to educate the public. It was an attempt to get a corporation to do the educating for them. Richards found this, in a word, lazy.


### The Electrician and the Professor

The debate that the letter precipitated, or more precisely the debate that the letter failed to prevent, arrived on October 17, 2023, when Flint Dibble, a classical archaeologist at **Cardiff University** specializing in the Mediterranean Bronze Age, sat down across from Graham Hancock in a studio in Austin, Texas, with **Joe Rogan** moderating. The episode ran for four hours and forty-seven minutes. It was viewed by tens of millions of people within its first week. It was, by any measure, the most-watched archaeological debate in history, and possibly the most-watched debate of any kind in which one participant held a PhD in a humanities discipline.

The format was unprecedented in the modern era. Academic archaeologists do not, as a rule, engage with alternative historians in public debate. The professional incentive structure actively discourages it. Engaging with a non-credentialed opponent on a popular platform risks elevating the opponent's claims to the status of "the other side of the debate," creating a false equivalence between a century of peer-reviewed research and a single author's interpretation. Academic careers are built on journal publications, conference papers, and grant applications, none of which reward a four-hour argument on a podcast. The colleague who gets tenure is the one who published in *Antiquity*, not the one who went on Joe Rogan. This is not an unreasonable set of incentives. It is also the set of incentives that, for decades, allowed mainstream archaeology to avoid confronting Hancock's claims in any public forum, ceding the popular-media battlefield entirely and then expressing surprise when the public preferred Hancock's version of the past.

Dibble broke the pattern. His stated motivation was science communication: correcting the record in a venue where millions of people were encountering archaeological claims, many of them false, for the first time. **Archaeology Southwest** and **Times Higher Education** both reviewed the debate and found that Dibble effectively presented the evidentiary case for mainstream archaeology, challenging specific factual claims that Hancock made about the age of archaeological sites, the absence of evidence for agriculture during the Ice Age, and the supposed suppression of inconvenient data by the archaeological establishment. Critics noted that Hancock repeatedly shifted claims when challenged rather than engaging directly with counter-evidence. From the mainstream perspective, the debate was a qualified success: a credentialed scientist had taken the case to the largest possible audience and, by most professional assessments, held his ground.

Richards saw it differently. And his perspective mattered, because by late 2023, Richards had become something genuinely novel in the ancient-history discourse: a voice that neither side could claim and neither side could dismiss.

**Jimmy Corsetti**, the host of the **Bright Insight** YouTube channel, one of the largest alternative-history channels on the platform, described Richards on air as "the most sane out of anybody" in the field: "You're a skeptic. You don't agree with everything that I say. You've done some debunk videos on me. But you also are so good-spirited about it, and you also identify and will say, hey, oh wait a second, he has a thought on this that's actually true. You're like this guy that's been kind of in the middle in like the most delightful way imaginable." Corsetti then observed the diagnostic test of Richards's independence: "All the archaeologists were warming up to you and loving you, and then as soon as you share a few things that contradicts things that, say, Flint Dibble had shared, now they're all wildly against you and talking about you all the time. So you're doing something right."

Richards, by late 2023, had become something that neither the academic nor the alternative community quite knew what to do with. He was an electrician by trade, living in the Pacific Northwest, who had started a YouTube channel called **DeDunking** (the name itself a provocation: not debunking, not endorsing, but something in between) that grew to nearly 130,000 subscribers in under a year. He appeared on the Joe Rogan Experience himself. He was invited to speak at the Cosmic Summit 2025 conference. He had earned a reputation as the most independently minded voice in the ancient-history space, a person who evaluated evidence without regard for which side it supported, which is a practice that makes you popular with people who want truth and unpopular with people who want allies.

Richards was not on Hancock's team. He had produced videos debunking specific Hancock claims. He was not on Dibble's team either. He had the disconcerting habit of changing his mind publicly when new evidence emerged, as he did with the Gunung Padang artifacts after Riano ([Chapter 12](12-the-date-that-moved.md)) dug up a supplementary information document that Richards and virtually everyone else had missed. Richards said on camera that he had egg on his face. This is not a thing that either professional academics or professional alternative historians typically say.

Richards's analysis of the Hancock-Dibble debate was devastating, and it was devastating specifically to Dibble, because Richards focused on the one thing that mattered most in a science-communication context: factual accuracy.


### The Ice Core and the Rice

The first issue Richards identified was Dibble's claim about **ice core data**. During the debate, Dibble had displayed a graph showing heavy metal concentrations, specifically lead, in ice cores from approximately 1100 BCE forward. The graph demonstrated convincingly that metallurgical activity leaves detectable traces in polar ice. Dibble then made a broader claim: that ice core evidence could rule out large-scale metallurgy during the Ice Age, more than 12,000 years ago.

Richards pointed out that the graph Dibble showed covered a period starting more than 10,000 years after the Ice Age ended. No published ice core study had tested for metallurgical signatures during the Ice Age itself. The absence of a study is not the same as evidence of absence. Dibble's presentation conflated the two, using a graph from one time period to make a claim about a different time period that the graph did not cover.

In his subsequent defense on social media, Dibble reframed his claim. He wrote that he had merely stated there was "no published evidence" for Ice Age metallurgy, a neutral statement that Richards would not have contested. But Richards had the receipts. On the podcast, heard by millions of listeners, Dibble's actual statement was more categorical: "We can be sure there was no large-scale metallurgy in the Ice Age." The word "sure" is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and the data Dibble cited does not support it. "How can we be sure if no testing's been done?" Richards asked. "We can make inferences, but Flint didn't talk about inferences in the debate. He made overarching, all-encompassing statements that are not completely accurate, not supported by the data."

The second issue was **plant domestication**. Dibble had argued on the show that the absence of domesticated plant remains from the Ice Age was definitive evidence against Ice Age agriculture. When Rogan asked whether domesticated plants could revert to wild forms, making ancient domestication invisible in the genetic record, Dibble responded that it would take "thousands of years." Richards had researched this question independently and knew the literature. A 2021 paper on feralization, published under the straightforward title "When a Crop Grows Back to the Wild," showed that the reversion process was far faster than Dibble claimed. Visible reversion to wild phenotype can occur within a hundred years. By a thousand years, the genetic signatures of prior domestication may be undetectable, particularly in species like rice, where multiple redundant quantitative trait loci control the domestication traits and where the genetic record shows what one paper called "remnants of the effects of selective sweeps in previously identified domestication genes." The genetic history of rice, Richards emphasized, is a "weedy mess" in which presumed wild rice populations turn out, on closer analysis, to be feral domesticated rice.

In his Twitter defense, Dibble omitted the "thousands of years" claim entirely, stating only that he had told Rogan he did not know the answer. Richards posted the clip. Both statements were on the record, sequential, in the same exchange: "thousands of years," followed by "well, I don't know." Dibble had given a confident, incorrect answer and then immediately hedged, and his subsequent retelling preserved only the hedge. Richards called this a "lie by omission." Whether the characterization is fair depends on one's interpretation of Dibble's intent, but the factual discrepancy between what was said on air and what was claimed afterward is not a matter of interpretation. It is on tape.

Richards also identified a subtler problem with Dibble's argumentation strategy. Whenever challenged on a specific factual claim, Dibble's defense consistently involved restating the claim in a weaker form, as though the weaker form was what he had originally said. The ice core claim shifted from "we can be sure" to "no published evidence." The feralization claim shifted from "thousands of years" to "I don't know." In each case, the original statement, the one heard by millions of listeners and embedded in their understanding of the debate, was more categorical than the evidence supported. The corrected version, offered only after challenge, was more accurate but had not been what was actually said. This pattern, Richards argued, was more damaging to institutional credibility than any single error, because it revealed a habit of overclaiming in real time and correcting only after the fact, which is precisely the accusation that scientists routinely level against alternative historians.

The dynamic was made worse by what Richards described as Dibble's treatment of the racism association. Dibble had, in multiple written pieces, linked Hancock's work to white supremacy and the erasure of indigenous agency. When pressed on this during the debate, with Rogan visibly uncomfortable, Dibble claimed the association was a one-time statement that had been "recycled." Richards compiled the evidence. The association appeared in articles Dibble had written, in the SAA letter he had co-signed, in his personal blog post after the debate, in his Twitter threads. It was not a one-time statement. It was a consistent element of Dibble's public engagement with Hancock. The claim that it was a single recycled quote was, in Richards's assessment, simply false, and the fact that Dibble made this claim on camera, in front of millions of people, while the written record contradicted it, was precisely the kind of thing that destroys public trust in expert authority.


### The Duality of Man

When Richards took the stage at the Cosmic Summit 2025, his first major public speaking engagement before a live audience of several hundred people, he opened not with the Hancock-Dibble debate but with a concept borrowed from philosophy: the **duality of man**. Humans can visualize perfection but cannot achieve it. They can imagine a world without hunger or suffering but recognize, at some level, that such a world is utopia, which literally means "no place." This tension between the ideal and the real, Richards argued, produces strange behavior in every human institution, but it produces especially strange behavior in science, because science promises a method for seeing reality clearly while being practiced by humans who are constitutionally incapable of perfect objectivity.

The history of earth science, Richards reminded his audience, is a history of pendulum swings driven by ideology as much as evidence. When geology first emerged as a discipline in the eighteenth century, it was tasked with proving the Bible: finding evidence of the Flood, the Tower of Babel, catastrophic divine interventions. Then **James Hutton** and **Charles Lyell** demonstrated that geological change was gradual, and almost overnight, the pendulum swung: catastrophism became heresy. No catastrophic explanations were accepted for any geological phenomenon, anywhere, for over a century. Scientists who proposed catastrophic events were dismissed as closet creationists. Richards pointed out that catastrophism was not rehabilitated in mainstream geology until 1980, when **Luis and Walter Alvarez** presented evidence that the Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction was caused by an asteroid impact. **Punctuated equilibrium**, the idea that gradual change is periodically interrupted by sudden catastrophic events, is now the standard framework. It took 150 years of institutional resistance to arrive at a position that, as Richards observed, "a kid could sus."

He cited **Max Planck**, the Nobel Prize-winning physicist, whose observation about scientific change has become known as **Planck's principle**: "Science does not progress one discovery at a time. It progresses one funeral at a time." The old guard has to die off. The young researchers who grew up with the new data, who were taught that catastrophic events are at least worth entertaining, are the ones who eventually change the consensus. Richards did not present this as a criticism of science. He presented it as a description of how science actually works, as opposed to the idealized version that science communicators present to the public.

The Clovis-first hypothesis was the paradigm fight Richards returned to most frequently, because it demonstrated the pattern in microcosm. The mechanism of that suppression, the institutional enforcement of the Clovis-first timeline and what it cost researchers like Dillehay and Adovasio, has been examined in detail in the context of the Clovis Wall. What Richards added here was the longer ante-Clovis thread that the mainstream story had dropped.

When **Jacques Cinq-Mars** published evidence of human occupation at **Bluefish Caves** in the **Yukon** in 1979, with lithic specimens dated to 24,000 years before present, the establishment's response was not curiosity but hostility. **Dr. Bill Farley**, an archaeologist at the YouTube channel **Archaeology Tube** who has no sympathy for Hancock's lost civilization claims, acknowledged this plainly in a video Richards cited: there was a "Clovis Mafia," a term coined by Farley's own PhD advisor, a group of senior archaeologists who actively suppressed pre-Clovis findings through the informal mechanisms of peer review, conference dynamics, and professional reputation. The resistance was not scientific skepticism. It was institutional enforcement of a preferred narrative.

The Clovis-first paradigm was eventually overturned. By the 2010s, pre-Clovis settlement was the standard view, with Bluefish Caves now joined by the **White Sands footprints** (approximately 23,000 years ago) and the far more controversial **Cerutti Mastodon site** in California, which some researchers argue shows evidence of human activity 130,000 years ago and which is now being investigated with the same rigor and initial skepticism that the earlier pre-Clovis sites once faced.

Farley treated the Clovis-first reversal as evidence that science is self-correcting, and in the long run it is. Richards treated it as evidence that the correction took thirty years longer than it should have, that real human beings paid real professional costs for being right too early, and that the people who enforced the wrong paradigm never apologized to the people whose careers they damaged. Both assessments are correct. They are not contradictory. Science does self-correct. It also sometimes takes a generation of funerals to do so.

Richards encountered the same pattern of institutional resistance in his own interactions with mainstream science communicators. In a detailed exchange with **Potholer54**, a popular science YouTuber and retired geologist, Richards presented the Bluefish Caves evidence and asked Potholer to correct his statement that the Clovis-first paradigm had been overturned cleanly by a single discovery. What followed was an extended back-and-forth in which Potholer repeatedly shifted his requirements for what would constitute evidence: first asking what artifacts were found (Richards cited lithic specimens), then asking where in the paper they were mentioned (Richards cited the abstract), then denying that lithic specimens were artifacts (they are, by standard archaeological definition), then demanding that the specific word "artifact" appear in the paper (it did not; the paper used the standard technical term "lithic specimens"), and finally telling Richards that his interpretation was "amateur" and that he was "not interested" in it. Richards polled professional archaeologists on Twitter, who unanimously confirmed that lithic specimens are artifacts. Potholer did not correct his video.

The exchange was trivial in its specific content but revealing in its dynamics. A science communicator, confronted with evidence that contradicted a claim he had made publicly, responded not by evaluating the evidence but by moving the goalposts until the evidence could not reach them. This is exactly the behavior that science communicators accuse alternative historians of, and Richards was not shy about pointing out the symmetry.


### The Uncomfortable Middle

The dynamic that Richards described, and that the Hancock-Dibble debate exemplified, is not unique to archaeology. It operates in every scientific field where established consensus meets disconfirming evidence. The pattern has been documented by historians and sociologists of science for decades: first, the new evidence is dismissed. Then the person presenting it is questioned. Then the person is attacked. Then, years or decades later, the evidence is quietly accepted, and everyone agrees they always knew it was right. Thomas Kuhn described this in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* in 1962. Planck described it more bluntly in the 1940s. Richards described it with the vocabulary of a person who watched it happen in real time on the internet, which gave it a different flavor but the same underlying structure.

What made the Hancock-Dibble debate unusual was not the pattern but the venue. Academic disputes normally play out in journals, conferences, and departmental hallways, where the audience is small, credentialed, and already committed to one side. This one played out in front of the largest podcast audience on Earth, where the audience was massive, uncredentialed, and forming its opinions in real time. The stakes were not tenure and publication; the stakes were public trust in science itself. And by this measure, Richards argued, the debate was a catastrophe for the mainstream, not because Dibble lost on points (he didn't, at least not clearly) but because the format exposed the gap between scientific authority and scientific accuracy. On a podcast viewed by millions, a single verifiable error does more damage to institutional credibility than a hundred accurate claims can repair.

Richards proposed a solution, or at least a starting point, in his open letter to the SAA leadership. The letter was measured and specific, written in the earnest, slightly overwrought style of a person who genuinely cares about both science and public trust and has watched both erode in real time. He argued that archaeologists attempting public outreach should accept two principles.

First, they must accept that they will sometimes have to leave some mystery intact when debunking a claim. When you hold up a mundane explanation next to an extravagant one in front of a public audience, and the mundane explanation accounts for ninety percent of the data but leaves ten percent unexplained, acknowledging the ten percent is not a concession to pseudoscience. It is honesty. And the public, Richards argued, can detect dishonesty with the precision of a mass spectrometer. The carbon dating of the Great Pyramid suggests it may have taken two to three hundred years to build, but Egyptology maintains a timeline of roughly twenty years per pyramid because the written records of pharaonic succession require it, with the discrepancy attributed to "old wood." Richards pointed out that this explanation is unsupported by independent evidence. It may be correct. It may not. Presenting it as settled fact is not science communication; it is advocacy.<sup>[17](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup>

Second, archaeologists who engage with the public must refrain from personal attacks, and specifically from the practice of associating alternative historians with white supremacy, racism, or colonialism. Richards was blunt on this point. When a credentialed scientist publicly accuses an interested amateur, or an author like Hancock, of "spreading racism" because they suggest pyramids had a mysterious origin, the scientist has not won an argument. The scientist has lost an audience. Permanently. Richards noted that Dibble had made exactly this association multiple times, in articles, in blog posts, in Twitter threads, and in the SAA letter that he co-signed. When the association was raised during the Joe Rogan debate, Rogan's body language made clear that he was not receptive, and Dibble visibly scrambled to walk the claim back, insisting it was "always the same quote recycled" and that he had "said it once." Richards posted the evidence that Dibble had, in fact, made the association in virtually every written piece about Hancock he had ever produced. The claim that it was a one-time statement was, Richards argued, simply false.<sup>[18](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[19](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[20](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[21](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[22](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup>

Richards went out of his way to insist that he did not want Dibble fired, that he did not want anyone fired, that the appropriate arena for this disagreement was public discourse, not employment tribunals. When his viewers began sending his videos to Cardiff University, he recorded an emergency video, unscripted and visibly frustrated, asking them to stop: "There's a huge difference between him misrepresenting facts to the public on Graham Hancock and him in a classroom talking to his students and making sure they walk out of there with a proper understanding of his field of specialty. So please, please, please, please, please, out of respect for me, out of respect for Flint, out of respect for yourself, you are better than this." He drew an analogy: if a McDonald's employee goes home and undercooks a hamburger, that does not mean the employee cannot cook a hamburger at work. The domains are separate. The fact that Richards had to explain this, repeatedly and at length, says something about the temperature of the discourse that the SAA letter had helped create.<sup>[23](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[24](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[25](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[26](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[27](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[28](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[29](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[30](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[31](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[32](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup><sup>[33](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup>


### The Science Communicator's Dilemma

The deeper problem that the debate exposed is structural, not personal. Dibble was not a bad scientist. He was a classical archaeologist, trained in the Mediterranean Bronze Age, who agreed to debate a professional author with thirty years of media experience in a format (long-form podcast with a sympathetic host) that favored narrative skill, confidence, and charisma over evidential precision and epistemic caution. The format rewarded bold claims and punished qualification. It rewarded storytelling and punished data citation. It rewarded the appearance of certainty and punished the admission of uncertainty, which are precisely the intellectual habits that make someone a good scientist and a terrible debater.<sup>[34](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup>

Richards articulated this structural problem in a video discussing the broader relationship between professional science and public discourse. He observed that the pseudo-archaeological community is full of passion and genuine research effort, but expecting its participants to translate their findings into formal scientific hypotheses is unrealistic. They would need to learn specialized vocabulary, submit to hostile peer review, endure institutional gatekeeping, and compile data in formats that most of them have neither the training nor the temperament to produce. The exceptions, like **Janet Stevens**, a hairdresser who figured out how Roman women actually styled their hair, cracked the historical mystery, and co-authored a peer-reviewed paper with a professional archaeologist, prove how rare it is for an outsider to successfully navigate the academic publication process.<sup>[35](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-35)</sup>

From the other direction, scientists who attempt to engage with the public frequently make the opposite mistake. They assume that their credentials are sufficient, that "trust the science" is a persuasive argument in an era when public trust in institutions has been declining for thirty years. They dismiss questions that sound like alternative history with reflexive hostility that is, as Richards put it, "clearly unscientific." And when they do engage, as Dibble did, they sometimes overstate their case in ways that undermine the credibility they are trying to defend. The problem is not that science is wrong. The problem is that individual scientists, operating in formats designed for entertainment rather than education, make claims that exceed their evidence, and the audience notices.


### What the Letter Did

The SAA letter to Netflix did not get *Ancient Apocalypse* removed. A second season was produced and released in 2024. Hancock continued to publish, speak, and appear on major podcasts. The letter, in practical terms, accomplished nothing that its authors presumably intended.

What it accomplished, unintentionally, was something else. It provided Hancock with a narrative of persecution that was more valuable than any amount of evidence. Every alternative-history YouTube channel, podcast, and forum cited the letter as proof that the archaeological establishment feared Hancock's ideas enough to try to suppress them. The letter transformed a mid-level controversy about a Netflix documentary into a culture-war event, complete with clear heroes (the independent thinker standing against the establishment) and clear villains (the institutional authorities wielding their credentials like weapons). The SAA, an organization that most members of the public had never heard of and would have been hard-pressed to identify by its acronym, became the face of scientific gatekeeping.

Dibble's decision to debate Hancock on Rogan was, in a sense, an attempt to correct this dynamic: to take the fight to the popular arena rather than hiding behind institutional letters. The attempt was courageous. It was also flawed in execution. And the flaws were amplified by the scale of the audience. On a podcast viewed by millions, a single inaccurate claim does more damage to institutional credibility than a hundred accurate claims can repair.

Richards ended up being the only participant in the entire saga who emerged with enhanced credibility on all sides. He did this by doing what neither the SAA nor Dibble nor Hancock consistently managed: evaluating specific claims against specific evidence, admitting when he was wrong, changing his position publicly when new data warranted it, and insisting that the goal of the exercise was understanding, not victory. He also did it by being funny, profane, and obviously sincere, qualities that the formal prose of institutional letters and the polished performances of professional authors cannot replicate.

The SAA letter is publicly available on the SAA website. It says what it says. The question is not what the letter said. The question is what the field does with the gap between its self-image and its public credibility, a gap that the letter made wider rather than narrower.

That question remains unanswered.
