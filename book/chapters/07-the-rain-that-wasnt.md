# Part II: The Ancient Sites


## Chapter 7: The Rain That Wasn't – The Sphinx, Water Erosion, and Alternative Dating
In 1990, a man who made his living teaching art history brought a geologist to Egypt to look at erosion patterns on a ditch. The man was **John Anthony West**, a New York autodidact who had spent two decades pursuing a single idea inherited from the French esotericist **R.A. Schwaller de Lubicz**: that the **Great Sphinx of Giza ([Chapter 6](06-the-wadi-al-jarf-papyri.md))** was older than Egyptology said it was, and that the proof was written in the stone itself. The geologist was **Robert Schoch**, an associate professor at **Boston University** with a specialty in stratigraphy. West needed a credentialed earth scientist to look at the walls of the **Sphinx enclosure**, the rectangular trench carved into the Giza plateau bedrock around the monument, and to state, on the record, what the rock was saying.

What Schoch saw when he examined those walls was, by his own account, unambiguous. The vertical weathering profiles, deep undulating channels running top to bottom through the limestone, were not the product of wind-driven sand abrasion. Sand erosion produces a different signature entirely: horizontally slotted channels with sharp edges, concentrated in softer strata. What the Sphinx enclosure displayed was something else. The channels were rounded, sinuous, and vertically oriented. They cut through both hard and soft rock layers without discriminating. Schoch identified them as the product of water, specifically precipitation runoff flowing over exposed limestone for an extended period.

The problem was the timeline. The Giza plateau has received almost no significant rainfall since approximately 3000 BCE, when the regional climate shifted decisively toward aridity at the end of the **Old Kingdom**. Egyptologists date the Sphinx to the reign of **Pharaoh Khafre**, around 2500 BCE. If the erosion was caused by rain, and rain had largely ceased by 3000 BCE, then the Sphinx enclosure had to have been exposed during the wetter period before that date, which meant the monument was substantially older than the Fourth Dynasty attribution allowed. This is the essence of the puzzle: **the rain that wasn't** there during the conventional construction window had nevertheless left its signature on the walls.

Schoch presented his findings at the annual meeting of the **Geological Society of America** in 1992. The response from attending geologists was broadly supportive. The response from Egyptologists was not. Mark Lehner, the foremost American Egyptologist working at Giza, rejected the redating. **Zahi Hawass**, then director of the Giza plateau and later Egypt's Minister of Antiquities, was blunter. The confrontation between Schoch and the Egyptological establishment became one of the defining episodes in the modern history of alternative archaeology, and for more than three decades it has generated more heat than resolution.

The reason is that the geology, contrary to what both sides claim, is genuinely inconclusive. And the most interesting recent work on the question comes not from Schoch's camp, not from the Egyptological mainstream, but from a series of geologists and independent researchers whose findings complicate every position simultaneously.

West himself was an unusual figure: a former playwright and Pynchon-era New York intellectual who had come to Egyptology through the esoteric tradition of Schwaller de Lubicz, whose ***Le Temple de l'Homme*** proposed that the Temple of Luxor was designed around the proportions of the human body and that Egyptian civilization encoded a sophisticated understanding of consciousness in its monuments. West translated and popularized Schwaller's ideas in his own book, ***Serpent in the Sky***, published in 1979. But the Sphinx argument was West's original contribution, and it was disarmingly simple. If the erosion on the Sphinx enclosure walls was caused by water, and the Sahara dried out thousands of years before the Fourth Dynasty, then the Sphinx enclosure was carved before the Sahara dried out. No lost civilization needed. No extraterrestrials. Just rain and rock and time.

What transformed this observation from an eccentric's hobby horse into a genuine scientific controversy was the 1993 NBC documentary ***The Mystery of the Sphinx***, narrated by Charlton Heston, which brought Schoch's geological analysis to a mass audience. Schoch appeared on camera in the Sphinx enclosure, pointing to the vertical channels and explaining the difference between wind erosion and water erosion in language that any homeowner with a crumbling foundation could understand. The documentary was watched by millions. It also made Schoch a target. His geological credentials were solid, his analysis was presented at a major professional conference, and his conclusion, that the Sphinx enclosure was older than the Fourth Dynasty, was never formally refuted by a peer-reviewed geological study. It was rejected by Egyptologists, which is not the same thing.

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)) cited the Sphinx erosion evidence extensively in ***Сенсационная история Земли*** and ***Древняя Мексика без кривых зеркал***. He drew the standard chain of inference: the rain erosion on the Sphinx and its enclosure demanded a date far earlier than the Fourth Dynasty, the Sphinx Temple was built from limestone quarried from the enclosure, therefore the Temple was also pre-dynastic, and since the Temple shared construction techniques with the **Granite Temple** and the mortuary temples of the Second and Third Pyramids, those structures were pre-dynastic as well. The entire Giza complex, in Sklyarov's framework, predated the pharaohs. He quoted Hancock's summary of West's work: «Эрозия Сфинкса и окружающей его траншеи, вырубленной в скале, является результатом вовсе не воздействия ветра, а сильных дождей, которые на протяжении тысяч лет поливали их задолго до того, как на свете появилось Древнее Царство» (The erosion of the Sphinx and the surrounding trench, cut from rock, is the result not of wind, but of heavy rains that poured on them for thousands of years long before the Old Kingdom came into existence).<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup>

Sklyarov also noted the awkward political dimension. At the 1992 GSA presentation, he wrote, «позицию Уэста поддержали почти 300 участников ежегодной конференции Геологического общества Америки» (West's position was supported by nearly 300 participants at the annual conference of the Geological Society of America). Geologists accepted the geological evidence. Egyptologists rejected the archaeological implications. The two disciplines spoke past each other then, and they continue to do so.


### The Walls That Remember

The first thing a geologist notices at the Sphinx enclosure is not the Sphinx. It is the walls.

The monument itself has been repaired so many times, by so many pharaohs and modern conservators, that its surface tells you almost nothing about original conditions. **Khafre** may have restored it. **Thutmose IV**, who erected the famous **Dream Stela** between the Sphinx's paws around 1400 BCE, certainly did. The Romans added repairs. The Egyptian Antiquities Service added more in the twentieth century. The Sphinx's body is a palimpsest of interventions. But the enclosure walls, the vertical faces of bedrock exposed when the trench was quarried around the figure, have been largely untouched. They preserve whatever happened to them.

What happened to them is the subject of the argument. Schoch saw rainfall erosion. His critics saw something else. Geologist **K. Lal Gauri** proposed in the 1980s and 1990s that the erosion could be explained by **salt weathering**, a process in which groundwater wicks upward through porous limestone by capillary action, dissolves soluble minerals, and then evaporates at the surface. As the water evaporates, salt crystals form within the rock's pore structure. Over time, the hydrostatic pressure of crystallizing salt fractures the stone from within, causing surface layers to flake and spall. The result is a rounded, undulating erosion profile that, to a casual observer, resembles water-flow erosion.

In 2014, geologist **Robert Schniker** published a paper elaborating on this mechanism. He argued that morning dew condensation and periodic wetting of the enclosure walls by saturated sand cover could produce the observed erosion patterns without requiring sustained rainfall on an exposed surface. The process would have operated over the entire 4,500-year period since the conventional Fourth Dynasty construction date.

Richards assessed both positions with characteristic bluntness. He noted that Schniker did not arrive at the question with a clean slate: Schniker himself admitted that his motivation was the conviction that "the Egyptologists probably know what they're talking about and that it isn't older." Richards observed that this was equivalent in scientific rigor to someone who believed the Sphinx was 12,000 years old traveling to Egypt and finding evidence for it. Both approaches went looking for a conclusion and found one. Neither constituted disinterested geology.

Richards also identified the core limitation of the salt-weathering model: it explains the undulating, wave-like surface erosion on the walls (what geologists call **exfoliation**), but it does not explain the deep vertical channels. Those channels look like water ran down the face of the rock with force and duration. Exfoliation from salt crystal growth produces broad, rounded surface loss, not narrow linear grooves. As Richards put it, neither Schoch's hypothesis nor Schniker's is complete. The geology seems to require more than one mechanism operating across more than one time period.

Richards also drew attention to a fact that both camps tend to underplay: the Sphinx itself has been recarved multiple times. The head is disproportionately small relative to the body, a feature that multiple researchers, mainstream and alternative alike, have attributed to re-carving from a larger original head (possibly a lion's head, given the body proportions). The **nemes** headdress has been modified; van Kerkwijk and others in the alternative community have documented evidence that crowns and a beard were added as separate pieces in later periods. If the Sphinx was recarved, then arguments about its "original" date based on the current surface become tangled with arguments about which surface is original. The enclosure walls, by contrast, cannot have been recarved without leaving obvious quarrying marks. This is why Richards, Carlson ([Chapter 4](04-the-cubit-coincidence.md)), and Reader all focus on the walls rather than the monument itself. The walls are the primary document. The Sphinx is the palimpsest.<sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup>


### The Fourth Dynasty Cut

Into this stalemate walked **Colin Reader**, a British engineering geologist with twenty-five years of field experience who had no prior investment in either camp. Reader published a series of papers beginning in 2001 that introduced an observation so straightforward it is remarkable nobody had made it before: the erosion patterns in the Sphinx enclosure are not uniformly distributed. They are concentrated in specific areas, and the distribution correlates with the enclosure's drainage history.<sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup>

Reader noticed that the northern wall of the enclosure contains a clear demarcation line. On one side of that line, the limestone is heavily weathered, with deep water-flow channels. On the other side, the stone is comparatively fresh. He identified the less-weathered section as an area that had been recut during the Fourth Dynasty, when Khufu (Khafre's father) quarried the plateau for pyramid construction. The more-weathered section, he argued, had been exposed for substantially longer.<sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup>

The critical piece of evidence was a quarry pit to the west of the Sphinx, created during Khufu's building program. In 1843, the Egyptologist **Karl Lepsius** was standing on the Giza plateau when a flash flood struck. The water was severe enough to sweep away iron crowbars. Lepsius described the runoff forming a temporary lake in the area west of the Sphinx, with no outlet, implying the water was captured by the quarry pit before it could reach the Sphinx enclosure. This is significant because the western side of the Sphinx enclosure displays the heaviest water-erosion channels, precisely the side facing the uphill slope where runoff would have flowed. Reader's argument was that before Khufu's quarry existed, rainwater from the plateau's western slope would have drained directly into the Sphinx enclosure. Once the quarry was dug, it intercepted the flow. The erosion on the western wall therefore predated Khufu, which means it predated Khafre as well.<sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup>

Reader's conclusion was more conservative than Schoch's. He did not propose a date of 10,500 BCE. He proposed that the Sphinx was a pre-dynastic site, likely associated with a **solar cult** that venerated the natural limestone outcrop for several centuries, perhaps a millennium, before the Fourth Dynasty incorporated it into their mortuary complex. He based this not on astronomical alignments or lost-civilization theories but on drainage mapping, quarry stratigraphy, and erosion distribution. The kind of evidence that does not make for dramatic documentaries but that engineering geologists find difficult to dismiss.

Reader also mapped the erosion distribution across the enclosure with a precision that previous investigators had not attempted. He found that the most intense water-channel erosion concentrated on the western and southwestern walls, exactly where plateau runoff would have flowed before Khufu's quarry intercepted it. The northern and eastern walls showed less channel erosion but more exfoliation, consistent with longer-term salt weathering under sand cover. The southern wall showed intermediate characteristics. This gradient was not random. It corresponded to the topography of the surrounding plateau and the hydrology of surface drainage, which meant the erosion pattern was being driven by geography, not by uniform exposure. The implication was that the different erosion types visible on the enclosure walls were not competing explanations but complementary processes operating in different areas and at different times.

Reader published his findings in 2001 and 2002, in papers that received attention from geologists but were largely ignored by both the mainstream Egyptological community and the alternative researchers who were, by that point, emotionally invested in Schoch's more dramatic 10,500 BCE date. Reader's work offered neither camp what it wanted: it was too radical for Egyptologists (pre-dynastic is still heretical) and too conservative for the alternative community (pre-dynastic is not pre-Ice Age). The result was that the most carefully reasoned geological analysis of the Sphinx enclosure fell into the gap between the two positions, cited by neither, contradicted by neither.


### The Primordial Mound

The mainstream position on the Sphinx is stated concisely: for a pre-dynastic date to be valid, there would need to be archaeological evidence of a civilization capable of quarrying and shaping hundred-ton limestone blocks in the Nile Valley prior to 7000 BCE, a period from which no such evidence exists. The limestone quarried from the Sphinx enclosure was used in the construction of nearby Old Kingdom buildings, demonstrating contemporaneous construction. The sociopolitical infrastructure required to carve the Sphinx is consistent with Fourth Dynasty Egypt, not with the hunter-gatherer societies of earlier millennia.

This is a strong argument. It is also, as Richards pointed out, slightly beside the point. The question is not whether hunter-gatherers carved the Sphinx in its current form 12,000 years ago. The question is whether the site was significant, modified, and perhaps partially excavated long before the Fourth Dynasty, and then recarved and incorporated into Khafre's complex.

Richards developed an interpretation that draws on Egyptian mythology rather than lost-civilization frameworks. The Sphinx sits on a natural **limestone outcrop** that is oriented due east, aligned to the rising sun on the spring equinox. During the **Green Sahara** period, roughly 11,000 to 5,000 years ago, when the region received substantially more rainfall, the water table would have been considerably higher. The area around the Sphinx outcrop would have been periodically flooded, with the rock itself protruding above the water line like an island.

In Egyptian cosmology, few concepts are more central than the **primordial mound**, the first piece of earth to emerge from the waters of chaos at the beginning of creation. This is the Benben stone, the original pyramidion. The concept appears in every major Egyptian creation myth. Richards proposed that the natural outcrop, a piece of bedrock facing the rising sun and emerging from seasonal floodwaters, was the original primordial mound in the proto-Egyptian imagination. If that is correct, then the site would have been venerated, and likely modified, for millennia before anyone carved a lion's body onto it.

This interpretation does not require lost civilizations, extraterrestrial architects, or even pre-dynastic construction capabilities. It requires only that people living near a remarkable natural formation during the wetter climate of the Green Sahara attached symbolic meaning to it, and that this meaning persisted long enough to be inherited by the dynastic Egyptians who later transformed the outcrop into the monument visible today. The Sphinx we see is the Fourth Dynasty's intervention. What preceded it may have been something as simple as a venerated rock with crude modifications, reshaped and expanded every few centuries by successive chieftains or priestly communities who wanted to associate themselves with what their ancestors had considered the place of creation.

The Egyptians had several creation myths, but one nearly ubiquitous feature was the inclusion of the primordial mound. The first piece of earth that poked through the waters at the beginning of creation. This idea was emulated in the major pyramids; virtually all of them have a mound of bedrock left in place at the center of the foundation, said to represent that original mound. The Benben stone, the original pyramidion that crowned the first pyramid or obelisk, was itself associated with the primordial mound. Richards suggested that the Ben stone may have literally been a piece chipped from the Sphinx outcrop: "maybe there was a little pointy bit that was aligned perfectly with the sun, and somebody later knocks that off." Whether that specific detail is correct is unknowable, but the broader point, that the power of the Giza site in Egyptian cosmology derived from its connection to sun, water, and creation, is consistent with the textual and mythological record.


### The Kufu Paradox

The inventory of evidence pointing to a pre-dynastic Sphinx is longer than the mainstream position typically acknowledges. The **Inventory Stela**, a limestone tablet found at Giza, describes Khufu repairing the Sphinx. If accurate, this means the Sphinx was already damaged a generation before Khafre, the pharaoh conventionally credited with building it, came to power. Mainstream Egyptologists dismiss the Inventory Stela as a Late Period fabrication, dating it to roughly 670 BCE, more than 1,800 years after the events it purports to describe. This dating is likely correct. But Richards and others have noted that even Late Period fabrications often draw on older traditions, and that the specific detail of Khufu repairing (not building) the Sphinx is a strange thing to invent if the Fourth Dynasty narrative was already canonical.

The French Egyptologist **Gaston Maspero**, who conducted the first systematic excavation of the Sphinx enclosure in the 1880s, read the Dream Stela differently from his colleagues. Where others saw Khafre credited with construction, Maspero saw him credited with excavation and restoration. He concluded that the Sphinx was "the most ancient edifice in all of Egypt." He was not a crank. He was the director of the Egyptian Museum in Cairo and the head of the Egyptian Antiquities Service. **E.J. Wallis Budge**, keeper of Egyptian and Assyrian antiquities at the British Museum, concurred, writing that the Sphinx "was in existence in the days of Khafre" and "was probably a very great deal older than his reign," dating it to at least the end of the **Archaic Period**, around 2686 BCE.

Even Petrie stated that it was "pointless to challenge the idea" that the Sphinx predated Khafre. The notion of a Fourth Dynasty origin for the monument only hardened into dogma in the twentieth century, when it became embedded in the standard chronological framework. The earlier generation of Egyptologists, working before that framework calcified, was considerably more open to the possibility that the Sphinx was older than the pyramids beside it.

The architectural evidence extends the argument. Sklyarov constructed a chain of inference that mainstream Egyptology has challenged but not fully broken. The Sphinx Temple, the structure immediately in front of the Sphinx's paws, was built from limestone blocks quarried from the Sphinx enclosure itself; all specialists, including mainstream Egyptologists, agree on this. If the enclosure dates to before the Fourth Dynasty, then the Sphinx Temple does as well. The Sphinx Temple was originally faced with granite veneer, in the same construction technique used in the Granite Temple next door, in the mortuary temple of the Second Pyramid, and in the mortuary temple of the Third Pyramid. Sklyarov argued that the identical construction techniques, the same combination of massive limestone core blocks with granite facing, indicate the same builders working in the same era. «Материал из этой траншеи пошел на строительство Храма Сфинкса, который расположен прямо перед лапами этого человеко-зверя. В этом сходятся все специалисты» (The material from this trench went to build the Sphinx Temple, which is located directly in front of the paws of this man-beast. All specialists agree on this).<sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-7)</sup>

The mainstream response is that the construction techniques are consistent across Fourth Dynasty structures because they were all built during the Fourth Dynasty. This is circular reasoning dressed as empirical observation: the structures are contemporaneous because they share techniques, and they share techniques because they are contemporaneous. Reader's drainage evidence introduces an independent variable. If the erosion on the enclosure walls predates Khufu's quarry, then the enclosure, and therefore the Sphinx Temple, predates the Fourth Dynasty regardless of what the construction techniques imply.

A 2024 paper in the journal ***Physical Review Fluids*** introduced a new variable into the debate. The authors conducted experiments on the fluid-mechanical erosion of clay mounds containing hard inclusions and found that wind-driven erosion of a featureless mound with a single hard nodule (representing the Sphinx's head, carved from a harder limestone stratum) could naturally produce a lion-like recumbent shape. The flow dynamics of prevailing wind around the hard inclusion created a protective "wind shadow" that preserved the body while the surrounding material eroded away. The implication was striking: the Sphinx's general form may not have been designed from scratch but sculpted by wind from a pre-existing natural formation, then refined by human hands.

This dovetails with Reader's drainage analysis and Richards's primordial-mound hypothesis. If the outcrop already resembled a reclining animal before anyone touched it, the decision to carve it into a specific figure becomes easier to explain. The Fourth Dynasty did not create the Sphinx from nothing. It inherited a site that had been accumulating symbolic meaning, and perhaps crude modifications, for a very long time.


### The Water Table Problem

A 2022 paper complicated the debate further by reconstructing the position of the **Khufu Branch** of the Nile during the Fourth Dynasty. Using core samples from the Giza area, the researchers determined that the river was substantially closer to the plateau in Khafre's time than it is today, and that in the pre-dynastic era, water levels were higher still. The implication, as presented by critics of the alternative position, was that the Sphinx enclosure might have been partially or fully submerged during seasonal floods for much of the pre-dynastic period. If so, the limestone would have been sitting in water rather than being rained upon, and water immersion produces different erosion patterns than rainfall runoff.

Richards examined this paper in detail and found methodological concerns. The model that showed higher Nile levels was derived from only two of five core samples taken in the area; the other three were excluded from the analysis, and the paper did not explain why, nor did it include their data in the appendix. Those three cores came from areas nearest to the Giza plateau, precisely the locations most relevant to the question of whether the Sphinx was underwater. Richards also noted that the areas adjacent to Giza from which the core samples were taken had been extensively modified by man-made canals and basins during the Fourth Dynasty, as documented by Lehner, making the use of those samples to reconstruct natural pre-dynastic water levels problematic.

The deeper problem is that anyone arguing for a submerged Sphinx must explain why the monument still exists. The Sphinx is carved from relatively soft Mokattam Formation limestone. A structure of this material sitting in a flowing river for millennia would have been worn down far more severely than what we observe, particularly at the vulnerable neck. The existence of the Sphinx in anything like its current form argues against permanent submersion during the pre-dynastic period.

Richards proposed a simpler explanation: if the site was venerated, its custodians would have built structures to protect it from flooding. Levees and dams are not high technology. They are among the oldest hydraulic interventions in human history. A mound of earth between the Nile's flood line and the enclosure would have sufficed. Millennia of subsequent quarrying and construction would have destroyed any traces of such a structure. The absence of evidence for a levee is not evidence that no levee existed; it is evidence that the Fourth Dynasty's building program obliterated whatever came before.


### What the Stone Says

**James Harrell** and other mainstream geologists argue that the erosion patterns are consistent with natural limestone weathering in a desert environment over 4,500 years. Salt weathering, periodic wetting by dew and groundwater, and the documented flash floods that still occasionally strike the plateau can, in this view, account for everything Schoch attributed to ancient rainfall. Hawass pointed out that Schoch "never demonstrates why the rainfall over the last 4,500 years would not be sufficient to round off the corners," noting that significant downpours have been recorded at Giza even in modern times.

This position is not unreasonable. It is also not as strong as its advocates present it. The salt-weathering model explains the broad surface erosion but not the deep vertical channels. The documented flash floods are episodic and brief, lasting hours rather than seasons; whether they could produce the observed channel depth over 4,500 years is a question that has been asserted but not demonstrated experimentally. And the entire mainstream argument rests on the assumption that the Sphinx enclosure has been continuously exposed since its creation. If the enclosure was periodically buried under sand, as it has been for most of recorded history, then the effective exposure time to weathering agents is considerably less than 4,500 years, making the observed erosion harder to explain within the conventional timeframe.

Schoch, for his part, has expanded his claims over the years. He now suggests the Sphinx may date to 10,500 BCE or earlier, a position that requires the existence of a civilization capable of megalithic construction at a time when the archaeological record shows none in the Nile Valley. He has connected the Sphinx to theories about **solar outbursts** ending the Ice Age, a framework that, whatever its merits in other contexts, adds speculative layers to what was originally a clean geological observation.

Carlson offered a more measured assessment. He noted that the Sphinx's erosion is probably the result of "a series of episodes" over a very long time, not a single climate period. He suggested that the enclosure walls, rather than the Sphinx itself, are the proper object of study, because the monument's surface has been altered by too many restoration campaigns to be diagnostic. The walls, by contrast, "are in their essentially pristine condition." Carlson considered the evidence for sheet flooding "indisputable" but was less committed to a specific date, noting that the erosion likely accumulated across multiple flooding episodes spanning tens of thousands of years.

This is where the debate stands. The salt-weathering model explains some of the erosion but not all of it. Schoch's rainfall hypothesis explains the vertical channels but requires a pre-dynastic date that lacks corroborating archaeological evidence. Reader's drainage analysis provides the most parsimonious explanation for a pre-Fourth Dynasty origin, but his proposed date (pre-dynastic, not Ice Age) is too modest for the alternative community and too radical for the mainstream. And the 2024 fluid-dynamics paper suggests that the Sphinx's form may owe as much to natural erosion as to human design, which, if correct, dissolves the question of "who carved it" into the less dramatic but more answerable question of "who finished it."

No one has conducted the definitive study. What is needed is a systematic analysis of salt crystal distribution within the enclosure walls, combined with controlled experiments comparing salt-weathering erosion rates to rainfall-erosion rates in the specific limestone of the Mokattam Formation. This would require core samples from the walls themselves, which the **Egyptian Supreme Council of Antiquities** has not authorized. Until that work is done, the Sphinx will continue to be the most thoroughly argued-about and least thoroughly tested geological question in archaeology.

There is one more piece of evidence that neither camp discusses often enough. The Sphinx faces due east. It faces the rising sun on the equinox. This is not an approximate alignment; it is precise. In a landscape where the monumental architecture of the Fourth Dynasty (the three great pyramids) is aligned to the cardinal directions with extraordinary accuracy, the Sphinx's eastward orientation stands out not as anomalous but as primary. If the site was a solar observation point before it was a royal monument, its orientation was the original datum, and the pyramids were subsequently aligned to a coordinate system that the site's custodians had already established.

Sklyarov, summarizing the work of Bauval ([see Chapter 27](27-the-inverted-map.md)) and **Adrian Gilbert**, noted that the astronomical alignments at Giza converge on a date of approximately 10,450 BCE, the beginning of the Age of **Leo**. The Sphinx has a lion's body. The zodiacal symbolism associates Leo with the constellation that greeted the rising sun on the spring equinox at that date. Sklyarov was honest enough to note the imprecision: the actual positions of the stars in Orion's Belt, which Bauval correlated with the three pyramids, do not exactly match the pyramid layout. «На самом деле реальное взаимное положение звезд Пояса Ориона все-таки существенно отличается от взаимного расположения пирамид Гизы» (In reality, the actual mutual positions of the stars of Orion's Belt still substantially differ from the mutual layout of the Giza pyramids). The correlation is approximate, not exact, which limits its evidentiary value.

But the approximate correlation, combined with the Sphinx's lion form and its precise equinoctial alignment, is the kind of data point that the mainstream has never fully explained within the Fourth Dynasty framework. If Khafre built the Sphinx in 2500 BCE, why did he give it a lion's body when the zodiacal age at that time was Taurus, not Leo? The mainstream response is that there is no evidence the Egyptians used the zodiac in the mid-third millennium BCE. This is likely correct. It is also a negative argument, and negative arguments have a way of being overturned by new evidence.

The enclosure walls are still there, still untouched, still preserving whatever story they have to tell. The story has been waiting for a reader since at least the time of Khufu. It is possible that it has been waiting longer than that.
