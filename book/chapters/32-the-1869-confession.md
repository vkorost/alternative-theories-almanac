## Chapter 32: The 1869 Confession – Giants in the Archaeological Record
On the morning of October 16, 1869, two laborers hired by a farmer named **William Newell** to dig a well on his property in **Cardiff, New York**, a small town south of Syracuse, struck something hard about three feet below the surface. They dug further. What emerged from the soil was a stone figure, roughly ten feet and four inches long, shaped like a naked human male, lying on its back with one hand resting across its abdomen. The surface had a yellow-brown color that some observers compared to old ivory. The legs were slightly bent at the knees. The feet were enormous. The face, broad and flat, wore what visitors would describe as either serene composure or fixed agony, depending on what they expected to see when they arrived.

Within hours, Newell had erected a tent over the figure and begun charging twenty-five cents admission. Within days, the price had risen to fifty cents. Within weeks, the **Cardiff Giant**, as the newspapers had named it, was drawing hundreds of visitors per day from across New York State. Special trains were chartered from Syracuse. Hotel rooms in Cardiff filled. Two theories competed for the public's attention: either the figure was a petrified man, a genuine ancient human of extraordinary stature turned to stone by geological processes, or it was a statue carved by some unknown prehistoric civilization, which would make it one of the most significant archaeological finds in American history. Both theories assumed the figure was real. Both were wrong.

The figure had been commissioned the previous year by **George Hull**, a tobacconist and avowed atheist from Binghamton, New York. Hull had gotten into an argument with a Methodist minister named **Turk** over the biblical passage in Genesis 6:4 that states, "There were giants in the earth in those days." The minister insisted that the passage was literal. Hull decided to prove how easily the literal-minded could be deceived. He purchased a five-ton block of gypsum from a quarry in **Fort Dodge, Iowa**, shipped it to a stonecutter in **Chicago** named **Edward Burghardt**, and commissioned a statue carved to resemble a gigantic petrified man. To add verisimilitude, Hull had the stonecutter use darning needles to punch tiny pores into the surface to simulate skin texture. He applied sulfuric acid to erode the figure's surface. He then shipped the finished product to his cousin Newell's farm, where it was buried in secret the previous November, a full year before its "discovery."

**Othniel Charles Marsh**, a paleontologist at Yale University and one of the most prominent scientists in America, visited the site, examined the figure for a few minutes, pointed to the clearly visible chisel marks, and declared it "a most decided humbug." His reasoning was straightforward: if the figure had been a genuinely petrified ancient human, geological processes would have eroded away tool marks over the millennia. The marks were fresh. The gypsum was the wrong type of stone for the local geology. The acid treatment was obvious upon close examination.

**The 1869 confession** came in December, roughly two months after the "discovery." Hull had spent a total of about $2,600 on the project (roughly $60,000 in 2026 dollars). By the time he confessed, he had already sold a three-quarter interest in the Giant to a syndicate of businessmen for $30,000. The syndicate continued exhibiting the figure even after the confession, because paying customers kept coming. **P.T. Barnum**, the circus impresario, offered to buy the Giant, was refused, and simply commissioned his own copy, which he exhibited in New York City. When the original syndicate sued Barnum for exhibiting a fake, the judge dismissed the case on the grounds that the plaintiffs could not claim damages for the counterfeiting of a counterfeit.

The Cardiff Giant now resides in the **Farmers' Museum** in **Cooperstown, New York**, where it is displayed without apology. Hull's confession did not end the public's fascination with the idea. It redirected it.

### The Recurring Template

The Cardiff Giant is the founding text of a genre that has proved more durable than the gypsum from which it was carved. Its structure, the accidental discovery, the ancient race of enormous humans, the institutional coverup, and the lone truth-teller persecuted by the establishment, has been replicated with minor variations for over 155 years. What changes between versions is the technology of the hoax and the name of the institution allegedly suppressing the truth. What does not change is the audience's willingness to believe, and the speed with which a confession or debunking is absorbed into the narrative as further evidence of suppression.

The genre did not begin with Hull. It inherited an older tradition. In the colonial and early republic period, American settlers frequently encountered the massive earthworks left by the mound-building cultures of the Mississippi and Ohio River valleys. The mounds at **Cahokia**, near present-day St. Louis, covered an area larger than medieval London. The mound at **Poverty Point**, Louisiana, dates to roughly 3,500 years ago. These structures were built by indigenous peoples over thousands of years, and the archaeological evidence for their construction is extensive and well-documented. But many early American settlers could not bring themselves to attribute such monumental works to the ancestors of the Native peoples they were in the process of dispossessing. Instead, they invented a "lost race" of mound builders, a civilization of superior beings (sometimes described as white, sometimes as Hebrew, sometimes as Greek) who had built the mounds and been wiped out or driven away by the ancestors of the current indigenous populations. This myth was politically convenient: if the mound builders were a separate, vanished race, then the land had already changed hands once, and the current dispossession was merely a repetition of history rather than a unique crime.

The "lost race" of mound builders was debunked as early as 1894 by **Cyrus Thomas** of the **Bureau of American Ethnology**, who demonstrated through systematic excavation that the mounds contained artifacts continuous with known indigenous cultural traditions. There was no lost race. The mound builders were the ancestors of the peoples still living in the region. But the myth persisted, and the giant-skeleton reports grew from its soil. If a lost race had built the mounds, perhaps that race had been physically imposing. If the skeletons found in the mounds were large, perhaps they were giants.

In the 1880s and 1890s, American newspapers published hundreds of reports of giant skeletons unearthed in mounds across the Midwest and South. The reports followed a template so consistent that it constitutes a genre: an ordinary citizen (a farmer, a well-digger, a railroad worker) uncovers enormous bones during routine excavation. The bones are described as belonging to a human of seven, eight, nine, or twelve feet in height. A local authority (a doctor, a minister, a county judge) examines the remains and pronounces them genuine. The bones are then either shipped to an institution for study (and never seen again) or crumble upon exposure to air. No photographs survive. No bones survive. The stories survive.

Aleksandr Sokolov of Antropogenez, the Russian science-communication project that has systematically debunked pseudoscientific claims since 2010, presented the Cardiff Giant as the prototype of this genre at a Scientists Against Myths conference. The Antropogenez presentation situated the Giant within a broader taxonomy of archaeological fakes: forged medieval mummies (the Vatican's collection includes child-sized mummies that turned out to contain medieval adult bones wrapped in genuinely ancient bandages, with gold leaf traceable to nineteenth-century Scottish tin manufacturing), the **Piltdown Man** forgery, and the persistent genre of giant-skeleton reports. Sokolov noted that Hull was a skeptic, not a believer. He created the Giant specifically to demonstrate that religious literalists would accept any physical object that confirmed their reading of Genesis. The experiment worked far better than Hull anticipated. The believers did not stop believing after the confession. They simply concluded that the confession was part of the coverup.

### The Smithsonian Story

The modern version of the giant-skeleton narrative centers on the Smithsonian Institution. In this version, the Smithsonian has systematically collected, confiscated, and destroyed giant skeletons over the past century and a half in order to protect the mainstream historical narrative. The story has dozens of variants, but the core structure is constant: someone found giant bones; the Smithsonian took them; the bones were never seen again; the Smithsonian denies everything.

The source of the modern narrative is a 2014 article published on **World News Daily Report**, a satirical website whose disclaimer states explicitly that its content is fictional. The article claimed that a Supreme Court ruling had forced the Smithsonian to release classified documents proving the institution had destroyed thousands of giant skeletons in the early twentieth century. The article was shared millions of times on social media. It was picked up by conspiracy-theory aggregation sites that stripped the satirical disclaimer. Within a year, the "Smithsonian giant coverup" had become a permanent fixture of the alternative-history internet, cited as established fact by people who had never visited the source and would not have recognized it as satire if they had.

**PolitiFact** investigated the claims in 2023 and found no documented, peer-reviewed discovery of giant human remains in the Smithsonian's collections or anywhere else. **Snopes** conducted a parallel investigation with identical results. The Smithsonian's own spokespeople have addressed the claims directly. The Institution's archives, which are publicly searchable, contain no records of confiscated or destroyed giant skeletons.

What the Smithsonian's archives do contain is the work of **Ales Hrdlicka** (Aleš Hrdlička), the Institution's first curator of physical anthropology, who served from 1903 to 1942. Hrdlicka was a Czech-born scientist who specialized in human osteology and who spent decades systematically debunking giant-skeleton reports. His method was simple: when a report surfaced, he would request the bones for examination. When the bones arrived, they invariably turned out to be either misidentified animal remains (mammoth bones, in particular, were frequently mistaken for giant human bones by non-specialists), bones of individuals at the upper end of normal human size variation (modern humans occasionally exceed seven feet in height, as the **NBA** demonstrates annually), or, in many cases, the bones simply never arrived because they had never existed. Hrdlicka published his findings as early as 1934, decades before the internet gave the giant-skeleton genre its current distribution network.

Richards addressed the giant-skeleton narrative in the context of his broader investigation of **mound sites** in the American Midwest. Richards grew up near **St. Louis, Missouri**, within driving distance of **Cahokia Mounds**, the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico. As a child, he visited Cahokia on school field trips. As an adult, he returned to it as part of his research into the destruction of indigenous earthworks during westward expansion.

Richards confirmed the Smithsonian's own data: roughly ninety percent of indigenous mound sites in the eastern United States were destroyed by plowing, construction, and deliberate demolition during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. This is a genuine archaeological catastrophe. The city of St. Louis itself was built on top of dozens of mounds that were leveled for commercial development in the 1800s. **Mound City**, Ohio, was partially destroyed by the construction of a military camp during the Civil War. Across the Mississippi and Ohio valleys, farmers plowed mounds flat because they obstructed field operations. Road builders cut through them. Railroad companies graded them. The loss is not hypothetical; it is documented site by site in the archaeological literature, and Hancock ([Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)), in the sixth episode of *Ancient Apocalypse*, gave it appropriate emphasis.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[16](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[17](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[18](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[19](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup>

**Milo Rossi**, the archaeologist and YouTuber known as **Miniminuteman**, covered this same territory in his response series to Hancock's Netflix show. Rossi acknowledged Hancock's point about the destruction of mound sites while pushing back on the interpretive framework. Richards, reviewing Rossi's coverage, noted something unusual: Rossi was "genuine" in a way that most participants in the Hancock debate were not. "He's as snide and snarky as I am," Richards said of Rossi, "but looks like what I'd look like if I could actually grow a full beard and didn't know what an electric razor was." The exchange between the two, a mainstream archaeologist and an alternative-history researcher who both cared about the evidence and both found the tribalism of the debate exhausting, was one of the few genuinely productive interactions the Hancock controversy generated.<sup>[20](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[21](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[22](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup><sup>[23](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-32)</sup>

But Richards also warned that anyone who begins researching mound sites will quickly encounter the giant-skeleton community. "Fair warning," he told his audience, "you go start digging your toes into this and the pile of woo gets big, deep, thick, and quick, and it's all about giants."

The woo is, in Richards' assessment, a parasite on a real problem. The destruction of mound sites is documented. The cultural loss is genuine. The indigenous history of North America before European contact is dramatically understudied relative to its complexity and scale. Poverty Point, the mound complex in Louisiana that dates to roughly 1700 BCE, was built by a hunter-gatherer society, which challenges the standard assumption that monumental architecture requires agriculture. The site's concentric earthen ridges, visible from the air, enclose an area of nearly a square mile. It was not recognized as a human construction until aerial photography became available in the twentieth century. How many similar sites were destroyed before they could be recognized is a question that cannot now be answered.

Injecting giant skeletons into this narrative diverts attention from the real story and discredits anyone who tries to tell it. Every hour that a researcher spends debunking fabricated photographs of giant bones is an hour not spent on the genuine and remarkable archaeology of pre-Columbian North America. The giant-skeleton genre is not merely wrong; it is destructive, because it colonizes the public's attention and leaves the real mysteries, which are considerably more interesting than twelve-foot skeletons, in the dark.

### The Nephilim Question

The biblical basis for the giant-skeleton belief is Genesis 6:1-4, which states: "There were giants in the earth in those days; and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bare children to them, the same became mighty men which were of old, men of renown." The **King James Version** translates the original Hebrew word as "giants." Other translations render it as **Nephilim**, a word whose etymology has been the subject of considerable scholarly debate.

Hancock, in *Magicians of the Gods*, devoted substantial space to the Nephilim question, situating it within the broader **Book of Enoch** tradition and the story of the **Watchers**, a group of angelic beings who descended to **Mount Hermon** in Lebanon, taught humans forbidden knowledge, mated with human women, and produced hybrid offspring. Hancock's treatment was careful to distinguish between the Nephilim (whom the Book of Enoch describes as the offspring of Watcher-human mating) and the Watchers themselves (who are described as having brought technology and science to early humans). He noted that the Watchers must have been physically compatible with human women to the degree that they could produce fertile offspring, which strongly implies that they were either human or very closely related genetically.

The late **Zecharia Sitchin**, whose ***Earth Chronicles*** series popularized the idea that the Nephilim were extraterrestrial visitors, built his entire framework on a translation of "Nephilim" as "those who were cast down," linking the word to the Sumerian cuneiform texts that Sitchin claimed described alien visitors mining gold on Earth. **Michael S. Heiser**, a genuine biblical scholar and ancient Semitic languages expert, demonstrated conclusively that Sitchin's translation was linguistically impossible. The Hebrew word *Nephilim*, if derived from the root *naphal* (to fall), should be spelled differently from its actual biblical form. The Aramaic cognate *naphil(a)* simply means "giant," which is why the ancient Greek **Septuagint** translation rendered it as *gigantes*. Sitchin, Heiser demonstrated, could not distinguish Aramaic from Hebrew. His translation was not a scholarly error; it was a fabrication deployed to make the Nephilim "sound like ancient astronauts."

Hancock, to his credit, distanced himself from Sitchin's more extreme claims while noting that the underlying Mesopotamian texts do contain material of genuine interest. He also addressed the passage in Numbers 13, set thousands of years after the Flood, in which Israelite scouts report that "all the people we saw there are of great size. We saw the Nephilim there." The context is unambiguous: the word means people of large stature. Whether "large stature" means a distinct race of giants or simply refers to individuals at the upper end of normal human variation is the question that the archaeological evidence must answer.

The archaeological evidence answers clearly. No osteological study, in any peer-reviewed journal, in any country, has identified a human skeletal population outside the range of normal *Homo sapiens* size variation that could be attributed to a distinct giant species or subspecies. Individual humans of exceptional height exist and always have. **Robert Wadlow** of Alton, Illinois, reached eight feet eleven inches before his death at age twenty-two in 1940, a consequence of pituitary **gigantism**. The condition is well understood, medically documented, and occurs at a rate of roughly three to four cases per million people. It does not require a non-human lineage. It requires an overactive pituitary gland.

The confusion between "people of exceptional height" and "a race of giants" is at the heart of the genre. Ancient peoples encountered individuals with gigantism or acromegaly and recorded them in terms consistent with their cultural frameworks: as heroes, as semi-divine beings, as the offspring of gods and humans. The Nephilim tradition, the Greek Titans, the Norse Frost Giants, the Hindu Rakshasas: these are mythological renderings of a real but rare biological phenomenon, filtered through cultures that did not have endocrinology but did have narrative. When Hancock writes that the Watchers' offspring "are not sickly as one might expect from an even slightly mismatched genetic makeup" and that they "thrive so vigorously" that they are described as giants, he is describing a population at the upper end of normal human stature, possibly with some selective advantage in a pre-agricultural environment where body size correlated with social dominance. The "three thousand ells" attributed to the Nephilim in some Enoch manuscripts (roughly 4,500 feet, or nearly a mile in height) is, as Hancock himself acknowledges, "patently absurd" and clearly the product of rhetorical exaggeration by an "angry old shaman" seeking to discredit the Watchers.

The Watchers tradition is genuinely interesting for what it reveals about cultural memory. A group of outsiders arrives, possessing advanced knowledge, interacts with the local population, produces offspring, and is remembered as semi-divine. This is a pattern that recurs in cultures around the world, from the Apkallu of Mesopotamia to the Viracocha of the Andes. Whether these traditions preserve memories of real contact between different human populations (perhaps maritime travelers encountering inland communities) or represent independent inventions of the "culture hero" archetype is a legitimate question. The question does not require, and is not illuminated by, the claim that the culture heroes were twelve feet tall.

Mainstream biblical scholarship treats the Nephilim passage as mythological, as a fragment of a pre-Israelite tradition that was incorporated into Genesis without full integration into the surrounding narrative. No peer-reviewed study in physical anthropology has attributed any skeletal collection to a Nephilim population. The word means "giants" in the oldest available translations. The passage means that some people in the ancient Levant were notably tall. This is not a mystery that requires a supernatural explanation. It is a demographic observation.

### The Easter Island Digression

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)) touched on the giant question tangentially in his study of Easter Island. The moai, the enormous stone statues that line the coastline, stand up to thirty-three feet tall and weigh up to eighty-two tons. They are, obviously, depictions of figures of enormous size. But they are statues, not evidence of biological gigantism. The indigenous Rapanui tradition holds that the moai "walked" to their platforms, a claim that a 2025 study on moai center-of-gravity engineering confirmed is mechanically plausible: the statues were designed to be rocked forward on a curved base, allowing teams of workers to "walk" them upright along prepared roads. The fact that a culture built enormous statues tells you about its engineering capacity, its social organization, and its aesthetic preferences. It tells you nothing about the physical stature of the builders.

Sklyarov, characteristically, went further. In *Мифы об острове Пасхи* (Myths about Easter Island), he noted that the older geological layers surrounding partially buried moai suggested that some statues might have been covered over much longer periods than conventional dating allows. Schoch ([Chapter 7](07-the-rain-that-wasnt.md)) made a similar observation. Whether these observations indicate an older date for the moai or simply reflect the rate of soil accumulation in volcanic terrain is a question that has not been resolved by systematic investigation. The observation exists. The investigation does not.

The connection to the giant-skeleton genre is through analogy rather than evidence: large statues suggest large beings; large beings suggest giants; giants suggest a lost race. Each step in this chain requires abandoning the previous step's evidentiary basis and replacing it with implication. The moai are real. Their impressive size is real. The leap from "the statue is big" to "the builders were big" to "the builders were a different species" is a series of increasingly unsupported inferences, each masquerading as a logical consequence of the one before it.

### The Olmec Heads

The alternative-history community's interest in giants is not limited to skeletons. Luke Caverns, a cultural anthropologist who graduated from South Texas University and specializes in Mesoamerican history, presented at the Cosmic Summit on the **Olmec colossal heads** of **San Lorenzo, Mexico**. These basalt heads, seventeen known specimens ranging from six to fifty-two tons, depict human faces with broad noses and full lips. They have been interpreted by some researchers as representations of African visitors to pre-Columbian America, a hypothesis that mainstream archaeology has rejected on the basis of DNA evidence, archaeological stratigraphy, and the artistic conventions of Olmec sculpture.

Caverns, who visited the sites in person and interviewed archaeologists on location, reported something that diverged from the published accounts. The official record states that the iconic San Lorenzo head was found approximately six feet below the surface. An archaeologist in his eighties, still walking the site, told Caverns through a translator that the actual excavation depth was sixty feet. Other archaeologists at the site corroborated this number. If accurate, the depth has implications for the age of the deposit and the sequence of cultural layers that buried it. Caverns was careful to present this as oral testimony from primary witnesses, not as published data. The distinction matters.

The Olmec heads are not evidence of giants. They are evidence of a sophisticated civilization that could quarry, transport, and carve basalt blocks exceeding fifty tons, a feat of engineering that mainstream archaeology attributes to the Olmec culture (circa 1500-400 BCE) and that alternative researchers sometimes attribute to an earlier, more advanced society. The heads' large size reflects their monumental function, not the stature of the people they depict. But the ease with which the giant-skeleton community has absorbed the Olmec heads into its narrative illustrates the genre's capacity to assimilate any large object as confirmation of its central thesis.

### The Photoshop Era

The digital age added a new layer to the giant-skeleton genre: fabricated images. In 2002, the graphic design website **Worth1000** (later absorbed into **DesignCrowd**) ran a Photoshop contest in which users were challenged to insert archaeological anomalies into real photographs. Several entries depicted giant skeletons being excavated by teams of archaeologists at what appeared to be genuine dig sites. The images were technically accomplished, compositionally plausible, and completely fictional. Within months, the contest entries had been separated from their context and were circulating on the internet as evidence of real giant-skeleton discoveries. They continue to circulate today, decades later, reproduced on websites and social media accounts that present them as authenticated photographs.

The Photoshop images represent a genuine evolution of the genre. Hull needed $2,600 and a year of planning to create a single physical artifact. The Worth1000 contestants needed a few hours with image-editing software. The speed of production decreased. The speed of dissemination increased by orders of magnitude. And the fundamental structure remained the same: a fabricated object, presented without its original context, interpreted as evidence by an audience primed to believe.

PolitiFact and Snopes have traced virtually all photographic "evidence" for giant skeletons currently circulating online to the Worth1000 contest or to similar image-manipulation sources. No photograph of a genuine giant skeleton has been authenticated by any peer-reviewed publication, any museum, or any accredited archaeological institution. The images are known fabrications. They are treated as evidence.

### The Satirical Afterlife

The Cardiff Giant was created by a skeptic to mock believers. World News Daily Report was created by satirists to mock the credulous. The Worth1000 Photoshop contest was created by designers to showcase their skills. In all three cases, the targets of the mockery, or the unintended audience, adopted the fiction as evidence. Hull's confession did not end belief in the Cardiff Giant; it became part of the lore. The World News Daily Report disclaimer did not prevent the article from being cited as fact; it was stripped away in transit. The Worth1000 watermarks were cropped out of the images.

The pattern suggests something about the relationship between evidence and belief that this book has encountered in other chapters. When the belief precedes the evidence, no amount of debunking can dislodge it, because the debunking is incorporated into the narrative as further proof of suppression. Hull confessed: the confession is a coverup. The satirical website has a disclaimer: the disclaimer is disinformation. The Smithsonian denies destroying bones: that is exactly what you would expect the Smithsonian to say. The structure is airtight because it is unfalsifiable. Any evidence against the thesis becomes evidence for the thesis.

This is the opposite of how knowledge works. Knowledge grows by identifying what the evidence rules out, narrowing the space of possibilities until what remains is either confirmed or genuinely unknown. The giant-skeleton narrative operates by the reverse principle: every piece of disconfirming evidence expands the conspiracy, widening the space of possibilities until anything is compatible with the thesis.

The Olmec heads are real. The mound sites are real. The destruction of indigenous cultural heritage is real. The Nephilim passage in Genesis is a real text that real scholars have studied for centuries. Pituitary gigantism is a real medical condition that produces individuals of exceptional height. None of these facts require, imply, or support the existence of a distinct race of giant humans. The archaeological record contains no giant skeletons. The genetic record contains no giant lineage. The institutional record contains no evidence of suppression.

What the record does contain is a 155-year-old genre that began as a joke and became a religion. Hull spent $2,600 to prove that people would believe in giants if you gave them a physical object to look at. He was correct. The World News Daily Report spent nothing and proved that people would believe in a Smithsonian coverup if you gave them a headline to share. That too was correct. The Worth1000 contestants proved that people would believe in giant skeletons if you gave them a photograph. Correct again.

The pattern does not tell you that giants never existed. It tells you that the evidence presented for their existence has, in every verified case, been fabricated, misidentified, or stripped of its original satirical context. It tells you that the desire to believe in giants predates the evidence by millennia and persists in the absence of evidence with a tenacity that evidence cannot match. It tells you that the institutional suppression narrative, which appears to explain why no giant skeletons have been verified, actually functions as a substitute for the evidence that would be required to support the claim in the first place.

Hull confessed in December 1869. The Cardiff Giant continued to draw paying customers for years afterward. It draws visitors to Cooperstown still. The confession changed nothing then. It has changed nothing since.

That is the most important fact about this chapter, and it has nothing to do with the bones.
