## Chapter 26: The Seven Sages – Ancient Myths as Encoded Records
On a humid April morning in 1849, the young **Austen Henry Layard** stood in a trench cut into the mound of **Kuyunjik**, across the Tigris from the modern city of Mosul in northern Iraq, and watched his Assyrian workmen lift a series of clay tablets from the rubble of what had once been the library of Ashurbanipal, the last great king of the **Neo-Assyrian Empire**. Layard was thirty-two, possessed of more ambition than formal training, and working on a budget so thin that he paid his laborers from his own pocket when British Museum funds ran short. He had been sent to dig up Assyrian palaces, and he was succeeding spectacularly: rooms of carved alabaster reliefs, colossal winged bulls with human heads weighing ten tons apiece, and, buried in the collapsed floors, thousands upon thousands of clay tablets inscribed with the wedge-shaped script that would come to be called **cuneiform**.

Layard could not read them. Almost nobody in Europe could. The decipherment of cuneiform was still a work in progress, and the tablets were more valuable as artifacts than as texts. They were packed into crates, shipped by raft down the Tigris, transferred to steamers at Basra, and deposited in the storerooms of the British Museum, where they sat, largely unstudied, for two decades. The Museum had acquired one of the greatest libraries of the ancient world and had no way to read it.

The tablets Layard packed into those crates were still largely unstudied when, in the early 1870s, George Smith, a self-educated engraver's apprentice, found Tablet XI of the *Epic of Gilgamesh* among the fragments and recognized the flood story: a man warned by a god, a boat, "the seed of all living things," a deluge. The man's name was Utnapishtim. The story predated Genesis by at least a millennium. What that discovery cost Smith personally is told elsewhere; he died at thirty-six in Aleppo, still in the field. What it opened was the question of what else was buried in the same library.

And what was buried there pushed into territory neither Smith nor his Victorian contemporaries were equipped to address: lists of kings who reigned before the flood for durations that made the 969 years of **Methuselah** look modest. Teachers from the sea who brought civilization to humankind. Seven sages who appeared in the deep past and whose instructions made the present world possible.

The tablets did not ask to be believed. They asked to be understood.


### The List and Its Numbers

The Sumerian King List exists in multiple copies, the most complete being the **Weld-Blundell Prism**, a four-sided clay prism inscribed around 1800 BCE, now housed at the **Ashmolean Museum** in Oxford. The prism was purchased in 1922 from an antiquities dealer by **Herbert Weld-Blundell**, an English collector whose acquisitions helped build one of the finest Near Eastern collections outside the British Museum. The object is small enough to hold in two hands and heavy enough to feel significant, its four faces covered in tiny cuneiform signs that were legible, when fresh, to anyone literate in Sumerian. Its text begins with a statement of breathtaking directness: "After the kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridu."

Eridu, now the archaeological site of **Tell Abu Shahrain** in southern Iraq, is considered by many scholars to be the oldest city in the world, with occupation layers dating back to approximately 5400 BCE. It sits at the edge of the marshlands where the Tigris and Euphrates merge, near the head of the Persian Gulf. In Sumerian tradition, Eridu was the first city, the primordial settlement, the place where the gods established the institution of kingship and where the god Enki maintained his freshwater temple, the **Abzu**, the subterranean ocean of sweet water beneath the earth. The city's primacy in the King List is not incidental; it is theological. Kingship begins at the place where divinity and human civilization first intersect.

Eight kings ruled before the flood. Their reigns, recorded in the Sumerian **sexagesimal** (base-60) numbering system, total 241,200 years in one version and 432,000 years in another, depending on which copy of the list is consulted. The first king, **Alulim** of Eridu, reigned 28,800 years. **En-men-lu-ana** reigned 43,200 years. **Dumuzid**, the shepherd, who also appears as a character in the Gilgamesh epic and in numerous Sumerian love poems as the consort of the goddess **Inanna**, reigned 36,000 years. These numbers are not debated as transcription errors. They appear consistently across multiple copies of the list, inscribed by different scribes at different times in different cities. Whatever they mean, they were meant.

Then comes the line that divides the list in two: "The flood swept over."

After the flood, kingship descends again, this time to the city of **Kish**. The postdiluvian reigns are still fantastically long at first, the first king of Kish ruling for 1,200 years, but they shorten progressively. By the time the list reaches the historically verifiable **First Dynasty of Ur** (approximately 2500 BCE), the reigns have contracted to decades, and the kings can be cross-referenced against other inscriptions, seal impressions, and archaeological contexts. **Enmebaragesi**, who appears in the list as a king of Kish, is attested by an inscribed alabaster vase fragment found at **Nippur**. The transition from mythology to history occurs within the document itself, with no announcement, no change of format, and no acknowledgment that anything unusual has happened.

Mainstream **Assyriology** treats the list as a political and propagandistic document. The antediluvian reigns are mythological in character: only seven kings from the entire list are independently attested in the archaeological record. Scholars at the **Oriental Institute** of the University of Chicago have noted that leading Assyriologists have advocated using the text "with caution, if at all" for historical reconstruction. The fantastically long reigns are widely understood as a literary convention conferring divine or semi-divine status on the earliest rulers, not as records of actual lifespans. The total of 432,000 years is itself significant in the sexagesimal system, being 120 times 3,600 (a **shar**, the largest standard unit in Sumerian mathematics), and may represent a calendrical or astronomical cycle rather than a human chronology.

The list served a clear political function. By establishing an unbroken chain of kingship from the gods through the flood to the present dynasty, it legitimized whichever ruler commissioned the latest copy. The flood, in this reading, is a narrative punctuation mark: it separates the era of divine governance from the era of human rule, and it explains why the world before the flood was different from the world after. The device is familiar; the Hebrew Bible uses the same structure, placing the Patriarchs with their centuries-long lifespans before the Flood and the historical kings with their decades-long reigns after it.

This is the standard interpretation, and it is well supported by internal evidence and by comparison with other ancient Near Eastern propaganda texts. It is also, as several researchers have pointed out, an interpretation that preemptively forecloses the question of whether the numbers encode something real.


### The Mill Between the Stars

The number 432,000 has appeared once already in this book, in the context of the precessional hypothesis: the argument, advanced by **Santillana** and **von Dechend** in *Hamlet's Mill* and amplified by **Hancock** ([Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)), that recurring numbers across unrelated mythologies encode measurements of the Earth's axial wobble. That argument, with its strengths and its substantial weaknesses, is examined at full length in the chapter on the precession cycle. What matters here is not the precessional hypothesis in general but the specific intersection of that hypothesis with the Sumerian King List.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup>

The number 432,000, the total of the antediluvian reigns in one version of the list, is also the total number of warriors who march from the 540 doors of Valhalla to fight at **Ragnarok** in Norse mythology (540 times 800). It is also the duration in years of the **Kali Yuga** in Hindu cosmology. It is also the total number of syllables in the Rig Veda. The mainstream response is that these correspondences reflect the mathematical properties of base-60 systems: 432,000 equals 120 times 3,600, which is 120 shars, a clean number in Sumerian arithmetic. The Babylonian transmission of base-60 mathematics to India during the first millennium BCE, a documented historical process via the **Achaemenid** and **Hellenistic** empires, could account for the number's appearance in Hindu cosmology. The Norse parallel is harder to explain by diffusion but may represent independent convergence on a number that is mathematically elegant in any counting system with factors of 12 and 60.

The criticism is valid. It does not, however, address what happens when the number is placed in the specific context of the Apkallu tradition. Here, 432,000 is not floating in mythological space. It is attached to a specific institution: antediluvian kingship. The seven Apkallu served these antediluvian kings during these reigns. If the reigns encode precessional time, then the Apkallu are being placed not merely "before the flood" but at a specific astronomical epoch. The priests of **Marduk's** temple in Babylon maintained astronomical records with an obsessiveness that bordered on the devotional; they tracked the movements of Venus, Jupiter, and the Moon across centuries, and their observations, preserved on tablets from the first millennium BCE, form the earliest systematic astronomical data set in human history. Whether this observational tradition extended backward to the period when the King List was compiled, and whether the compilers had access to genuine astronomical knowledge that they encoded in the language of kingship, is a question that the evidence does not permit an answer to. Both explanations, intentional encoding and mathematical coincidence, account for the data. Neither can be excluded.

The question Santillana never answered, because nobody can, is whether the encoding was intentional or retroactive. The number is there. The mythology is there. The astronomical tradition is there. Whether the three are connected by design or by the human habit of finding patterns in large numbers is the kind of question that scholarship names and then moves past, because there is no experiment that could resolve it.


### The Teachers from the Sea

The Sumerian King List is not the only antediluvian tradition in Mesopotamian literature. A parallel tradition describes a group of beings called the **Apkallu**, typically numbered at seven, who emerged from the sea before the flood to bring the arts of civilization to humankind. The first and most famous was Oannes, described by the Babylonian priest Berossus in the third century BCE as a creature with the body of a fish and the head of a man (or a man's head beneath a fish's head), who emerged from the Persian Gulf each morning, taught humanity writing, agriculture, mathematics, law, and temple-building, and returned to the sea each night. Berossus, writing in Greek for a Seleucid audience, noted that "nothing material has been added by way of improvement" to Oannes's teachings, a claim that amounts to saying civilization was delivered complete at the beginning, not developed incrementally over time.

Berossus was summarizing much older Mesopotamian traditions. The Apkallu appear in earlier Sumerian and Akkadian texts as advisors to the antediluvian kings, sages who possessed the **me**, the divine decrees that governed every aspect of civilized life, from kingship and priesthood to beer-brewing and sexual intercourse. The me were not abstract principles. The Sumerian texts list them with a specificity that reads like the inventory of a civilization: kingship, the art of the scribe, the art of the coppersmith, the art of the leather-worker, the art of the fuller, the art of the builder, the art of the basket-weaver. There are over a hundred of them. Each was a portable unit of knowledge, a discrete cultural technology that could be acquired, transported, and, critically, lost. When the goddess Inanna stole the me from Enki in a famous Sumerian poem (she got him drunk, which may be the oldest drinking story in the world), she loaded them onto her boat and sailed away with them, and Enki spent the rest of the narrative trying to get them back. The me were property. They could be stolen because they were things, not ideas.

The Apkallu's role was not to rule but to instruct. Each of the seven was associated with a specific king and a specific city. The first, Adapa (later identified with Oannes), served the first king of Eridu. The seventh, **Utu-abzu**, served the last antediluvian king of Shuruppak. The list is not casual; it preserves a one-to-one correspondence between sage and ruler that mirrors the one-to-one correspondence between king and city in the King List itself. The parallel structure suggests that the two lists were conceived as a single integrated document: one column records who ruled, the other records who taught the ruler to rule.

After the flood, the Apkallu did not return. A later tradition holds that seven post-flood sages, the **Ummanu**, continued the work, but they were human, not divine, and their knowledge was understood to be a diminished inheritance, a partial transmission of something that had once been whole. The asymmetry is pointed. The pre-flood teachers were divine. The post-flood teachers were human. The knowledge available after the catastrophe was a fraction of what had existed before. This is not a narrative of progress. It is a narrative of loss, told by a civilization that believed it was living in the aftermath of something it could not recover.

The Apkallu are not merely textual figures. They survive in stone. The palace reliefs of **Ashurnasirpal II** at **Nimrud**, carved in the ninth century BCE and now divided among the British Museum, the **Metropolitan Museum of Art**, and the **Iraq Museum** in Baghdad, depict three distinct forms of Apkallu: the fish-cloaked type (a human figure wearing a fish skin, the head of the fish forming a hood over the human head, the body and tail draping down the back), the bird-headed type (a winged figure with the head of an eagle and the body of a man), and the fully human type (a bearded man in elaborate robes, often carrying a bucket and a cone-shaped object interpreted as a purification device). All three types appear flanking the **Tree of Life**, a recurring motif in Assyrian palace art. The reliefs were not decorative; they were apotropaic, intended to protect the king and his household from malevolent supernatural forces. The Apkallu were guardians. Their images were buried beneath doorways, embedded in foundation deposits, positioned at thresholds. They stood, in stone and in clay, at every point where the palace was vulnerable to intrusion from the spiritual world. The level of artistic investment in these figures, the precision of the carving, the standardization of the three types across different rooms and different palaces, indicates a theological tradition of considerable institutional depth, not a casual folklore motif but a sustained belief system with its own iconographic grammar.

Hancock, in *Magicians of the Gods* (2015), identified the Apkallu with a lost civilization's survivors who, after a catastrophic event at the end of the ice age, dispersed across the globe and seeded the emergence of agriculture, astronomy, and monumental architecture in previously hunter-gatherer societies. The Apkallu, in Hancock's reading, were not fish-men or divine beings but human teachers whose maritime origins (they "came from the sea") reflected their status as navigators from a drowned homeland. **The Seven Sages** became, in this framework, the central organizing metaphor: a small number of civilized survivors who preserved and transmitted the knowledge of a prior world.

Hancock pointed to parallel figures across the globe. Viracocha in Andean mythology also came from the water, taught civilization, and departed, promising to return. **Quetzalcoatl** in Mesoamerica, the feathered serpent associated with knowledge, agriculture, and the arts, was described as a bearded, light-skinned figure who arrived from the east and departed by sea. The **Followers of Horus** (the Shemsu Hor) in Egyptian tradition were semi-divine beings who governed Egypt during the mythological period known as **Zep Tepi** ("the First Time"), thousands of years before the historical pharaohs. The **Turin Royal Canon**, a hieratic papyrus dating to the reign of Ramesses II (approximately 1250 BCE), records the reigns of these predynastic divine rulers in a format strikingly similar to the Sumerian King List, with fantastically long reigns for the earliest figures and progressively shorter ones as the list approaches historical time.

The structural similarity is consistent across these traditions: beings from elsewhere who arrive after a catastrophe and teach the arts of civilization. The traditions appear in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Andes, Mesoamerica, and the Pacific. Hancock's argument was that the structural consistency pointed to a common historical source. The mainstream response was that the structural consistency pointed to common cognitive and cultural processes: cultures naturally attribute their origins to divine or semi-divine founders, and the "arrival from the sea" motif reflects the near-universal importance of waterways for early civilizations rather than a literal historical memory of maritime refugees.

The mainstream objection is **Euhemerism**, the interpretive approach that treats myths as distorted records of real historical events. Once fashionable during the Enlightenment, Euhemerism has been largely rejected by contemporary scholarship in mythology, anthropology, and classical studies. Modern mythologists point out that euhemeristic readings impose anachronistic assumptions, ignore the symbolic and cosmological functions of myth, and have been associated historically with colonialist dismissals of non-Western religious traditions (the assumption that indigenous peoples could not have developed civilization independently and must have received it from visiting outsiders). The scholarly consensus holds that myths are primarily cultural, cosmological, and ritual texts rather than garbled chronicles.

This objection is strongest when applied to specific identifications: Oannes was a real person from Atlantis who arrived in Sumer by boat. It is less decisive when applied to the structural pattern itself. The question is not whether the Apkallu were real fish-men. The question is why the structure of the story, teachers from the sea who bring knowledge after a catastrophe, recurs in civilizations separated by oceans and millennia. Diffusion from Mesopotamia through trade routes explains some instances. Independent invention, the tendency of cultures to attribute their achievements to divine origins, explains others. Neither fully accounts for the specificity of the pattern: aquatic origin, post-catastrophe timing, teaching function, eventual departure or disappearance.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup>

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)), characteristically, was less interested in whether the Apkallu were human or divine than in what they brought and what human cultures did with the memory. In *Предметы богов и их копии* (Objects of the Gods and Their Copies), he applied the cargo cult analogy, the mechanism by which a population technologically overwhelmed by contact with a superior civilization converts observation into religious narrative and remembrance into ritual. The argument at full length belongs to his framework of the civilization of the ancient gods, examined earlier in this book. Its application here is structural: Sklyarov proposed that the Apkallu tradition operated by exactly this mechanism, that Oannes and his six companions were remembered not as they were but as what they could do, and that the fish-body iconography was a costume built from incomprehension.

His point was not that the Sumerians worshipped aliens the way Melanesian islanders worshipped American supply drops. His point was that the cognitive mechanism, the transformation of incomprehensible technology into religious narrative, is a documented human universal. Whether this mechanism applies to the Apkallu tradition is a question that the available evidence does not answer. The analogy is structurally illuminating. It is not proof.


### The Flood That Divides

The flood itself, the event that the Sumerian King List uses as its structural divide, is the element of the tradition that has generated the most sustained scholarly attention, precisely because it corresponds, at least in broad outline, to events that are geologically documented.

Sklyarov, in his extended essay ***Миф о Потопе: расчёты и реальность*** (The Myth of the Flood: Calculations and Reality), approached the global flood traditions not as theology but as a data set. He collected flood narratives from Mesopotamian, Hindu, Greek, Norse, Mesoamerican, South American, Oceanic, and East Asian traditions, and noted their structural similarities: a warning from a divine being, a single family or group that survives, a vessel of some kind, the destruction of the rest of humanity, a mountain or high ground where the survivors take refuge. «Почти во всех культурах мира существуют поразительно похожие друг на друга легенды о Вселенском потопе, их более пятисот» (In almost all cultures of the world there exist strikingly similar legends about a universal flood, more than five hundred of them), he noted.

The mainstream position on global flood myths, shared by the vast majority of scholars in anthropology, comparative religion, and geology, is that they reflect local hydrological events: stories about real floods that occurred in river valleys, coastal zones, or lacustrine basins and were remembered, amplified, and universalized in the retelling. The Mesopotamian flood narrative is the best-documented case. Archaeological evidence of massive flooding in the Tigris-Euphrates valley has been found at multiple sites, including **Ur** and Shuruppak, dated to approximately 2900 BCE. The earliest written flood narrative, the **Eridu Genesis**, predates the biblical account by at least a millennium and shares its basic structure. The diffusion of this story from Mesopotamia to the Levant, and thence into the Hebrew Bible, is a documented process of cultural transmission that can be traced through intermediate texts.

The difficulty arises when the field of comparison is expanded beyond the ancient Near East. The **Araucanian** people of pre-Columbian Chile preserved a flood tradition in which survivors fled to a mountain called Teg-Teg, "the thundering" or "the sparkling," which had three peaks and could float on water. The Inca tradition preserved by **Cristobal de Molina** describes a shepherd warned by his llamas, who studied the stars and predicted the flood, and who retreated to a mountain called **Ancasmarca**, which grew taller as the waters rose. The Yamana of Tierra del Fuego attributed the flood to a vengeful Moon-woman. The **Tz'utujil Maya** describe a flood sent by the gods to destroy an imperfect creation of humans made from wood. These traditions share structural elements with the Mesopotamian original, but many of them come from populations with no documented contact with the ancient Near East.

The standard explanation is convergence: floods are universal natural events, and any river-valley or coastal civilization will eventually experience one dramatic enough to generate a cultural memory. This is reasonable. It does not explain why the narrative structure (divine warning, single survivor, vessel, mountain, repopulation) is so consistent across unrelated traditions, unless the structure itself is a kind of cognitive universal, the shape that flood stories naturally assume in human memory. This is possible. It is also unfalsifiable, which gives it the same epistemological status as the traditions it seeks to explain.

The Mesopotamian flood account itself has an archaeology that predates even the Eridu Genesis. **Sir Leonard Woolley**, excavating at Ur in the winter of 1928-1929, drove a test shaft through the royal cemetery levels and into virgin soil, or what he expected to be virgin soil. Instead, his workers hit a thick stratum of clean, water-laid clay, nearly three meters deep, with no artifacts, no pottery, no human trace of any kind. Below the clay, occupation resumed: flint tools, painted pottery, the remains of a culture that had existed before the inundation. Woolley's wife, **Katharine**, reportedly said, "Well, of course it's the Flood." Woolley, who had a keener sense of what the press would do with such a claim, initially resisted the identification, but eventually published it as physical evidence of the biblical flood, a declaration that made international headlines. Subsequent investigation revealed that the clay layer was local, not uniform across the Mesopotamian plain: other excavations at nearby sites found flood deposits at different stratigraphic levels, indicating multiple separate inundation events rather than a single universal deluge. The Mesopotamian alluvium was prone to catastrophic flooding whenever the Tigris and Euphrates broke their banks simultaneously, and such events, occurring once every few centuries, would have been devastating to communities living on the floodplain. The memory of the worst such event, or the composite memory of several, likely became the Sumerian flood narrative.

But the composite-memory hypothesis works best when applied to the Near East, where the alluvial plain is demonstrably flood-prone and where the textual transmission from Sumerian to Akkadian to Hebrew can be documented. It works less well for traditions like the Inca shepherd who was warned by his llamas, or the Yamana Moon-woman story at the tip of South America, or the Aboriginal Australian traditions about the sea swallowing the land, which researchers at the University of the **Sunshine Coast** have correlated with specific post-glacial sea-level rise events at particular locations along the Australian coastline, some dating back more than 7,000 years. If these Australian traditions genuinely encode memories of specific geographical changes, then oral transmission across seven millennia is not merely hypothetically possible; it has a documented example. The question is whether such preservation is the exception or, in some attenuated form, the rule.

What the geological record confirms is that the end of the last ice age produced flooding events of a scale no local river flood can match. The collapse of the Laurentide Ice Sheet sent catastrophic meltwater pulses into the Atlantic, the Gulf of Mexico, and the Arctic. Sea levels rose over 120 meters. Coastal landscapes that had been inhabited for millennia were submerged. Whether these events were the "flood" that the Sumerian King List records, or whether the list's flood is a literary device imposed on the geological record by modern interpreters eager for correspondence, is a question the evidence does not resolve.


### What the Prism Records

The Sumerian King List records something. The question is what.

If the antediluvian reigns are political mythology, as mainstream Assyriology contends, then the list is a legitimation document, and the fantastically long reigns are a literary device conferring divine sanction on the institution of kingship. This interpretation is well-supported by the list's political function and by the progressive shortening of reigns as the document approaches verifiable history.

If the reigns encode astronomical cycles, as Santillana, von Dechend, and their successors contend, then the list is a repository of precessional knowledge disguised as chronicle. The number 432,000 becomes a key to a mathematical system rather than a statement about longevity. This interpretation is intriguing but unverifiable: any set of large round numbers in a base-60 system will produce apparent precessional harmonics, and distinguishing intentional encoding from numerical coincidence requires independent corroboration that does not exist.

If the flood in the list corresponds to a real catastrophic event at the end of the last ice age, as Hancock and others argue, then the list's antediluvian section may preserve, in deeply garbled form, a memory of civilizations that preceded the historical Sumerians. The Apkallu tradition, on this reading, records the transmission of surviving knowledge from one era to the next. This interpretation requires accepting that oral traditions can preserve meaningful information across ten thousand or more years of retelling. Some anthropologists find this plausible: **Aboriginal Australian** oral traditions about sea-level rise have been tentatively dated to events over 7,000 years ago by correlating the narratives with known geological changes at specific coastal sites. Others consider the proposition implausible for anything beyond general narrative templates, arguing that ten millennia of retelling would degrade any specific factual content beyond recognition.<sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup>

The prism itself, inscribed on clay in a museum in Oxford, offers no guidance on which interpretation is correct. It simply records the names of kings, the lengths of their reigns, and the cities where kingship resided, and then it notes, without elaboration, that the flood swept over, and kingship descended from heaven again, and the world resumed.<sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup>

Seven kings are independently attested. The antediluvian reigns are political mythology, or astronomical code, or the longest game of telephone in human history. The flood is a literary device, or a geological event, or both simultaneously, one layered upon the other so completely that separating the two is no longer possible.<sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup>

The seven sages came from the sea. They taught everything. They left nothing in writing that has survived, which is to say they left nothing except the memory that they had once existed. That memory was committed to clay by scribes who believed it, carved in stone by artists who took it seriously enough to standardize three distinct iconographic types, and preserved across twenty-five centuries of Mesopotamian civilization without being dismissed as metaphor. The scholars who study these texts treat them as mythology. The Sumerians who composed them treated them as history. Neither position has been falsified.<sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup>

The prism does not resolve the question. It merely insists, with the quiet stubbornness of fired clay, that the question was worth recording.<sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup><sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-26)</sup>
