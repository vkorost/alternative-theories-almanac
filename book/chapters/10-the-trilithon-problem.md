## Chapter 10: The Trilithon Problem – Baalbek and the Unexplained Megaliths
The quarry is eight hundred meters south of the temple, which is close enough to see from but far enough to wonder about. Inside it, still attached to the bedrock at one end, lies a block of **Jurassic limestone** that weighs approximately 1,650 tons. Next to it, partially buried until German and Lebanese archaeologists excavated the site between 2014 and 2016, rests a second block of comparable dimensions. A third, the one tourists have been photographing since the Ottoman period, sits at a slight angle above the other two, its surface weathered by two millennia of Lebanese winters. The locals call it **Hajar el-Hibla**, the Stone of the Pregnant Woman, a name whose origin no one has convincingly explained, though the theories range from the poetic to the anatomical.

None of these stones ever left the quarry. They were cut, shaped on five of six sides, and then abandoned. The largest of them, discovered only in the 2014 excavations led by the German Archaeological Institute under archaeologist **Margarete van Ess**, measures roughly 19.6 meters long, 6 meters wide, and 5.5 meters tall. Its estimated weight of 1,650 tons makes it the single heaviest quarried block in the known archaeological record. The starting mass of the **Saturn V** rocket at the launch of **Apollo 16** was 2,965 tons, as Antropogenez helpfully noted; the comparison is arresting and also, in any useful sense, meaningless, because lifting something with controlled combustion and sliding something across sand are problems from different centuries of engineering. Still, the figure conveys scale. This is a stone you could lose a three-story building behind.

When the excavation team cleared the soil beneath the Stone of the Pregnant Woman, they found not only the new megalith but also fifteen small caves containing burials from the **Mamluk period**, and, tucked into one of the caves, the remains of a forge: a kiln, charcoal deposits, and iron slag. Somebody had been making tools down here, in the shadow of the largest cut stones on Earth, using technologies that would not have troubled a village blacksmith. The forge raises no archaeological questions. It simply sits there, modestly, a reminder that the mundane and the colossal can share the same address.

The quarry stones are not the puzzle. They are abandoned projects, impressive mainly for their ambition. The puzzle is five hundred meters to the north, already installed in a wall, seven meters above ground level, fitted together with a precision that leaves no gap visible to the naked eye. Three blocks, each approximately 19 meters long, 4.3 meters high, and 3.6 meters wide, each weighing in the range of 800 tons. They form a single course of masonry on the western side of the great platform at Baalbek, the ancient temple complex in Lebanon's **Bekaa Valley**. They are called the **Trilithon**, and they have been famous since antiquity.

The Romans built one of the most spectacular temple complexes in their empire on top of and around that platform. That much is not in dispute. The question is whether the Romans also built the platform itself, or whether they found it already there and, with characteristic pragmatism, decided to put it to use.


### The City of the Sun

Baalbek sits in the Bekaa Valley, a fertile corridor running between the **Lebanon** and **Anti-Lebanon** mountain ranges, roughly ninety kilometers northeast of Beirut. The name is Semitic: **Baal**, the storm god of the Canaanites and Phoenicians, and **Bek** (or Beka), the valley. Baal of the Bekaa. The site was sacred long before the Romans arrived. Archaeological excavations beneath the Temple of Jupiter have revealed layers of continuous habitation stretching back to the **Pre-Pottery Neolithic** period, roughly contemporaneous with Gobekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey. Someone chose this particular spot, on a flat section of the valley floor between two mountain ranges, and kept choosing it for the better part of ten thousand years.

The **Phoenicians** maintained a temple to **Baal-Hadad** on the site, worshipping a triad of local deities: Baal-Hadad himself, the goddess **Atargatis** (a formidable figure in her own right, part fertility goddess, part fish deity, worshipped from Syria to the Euphrates), and a younger god sometimes identified with **Hermes** or **Bacchus** in later Greek and Roman interpretations. The triad is documented in the work of the Lebanese historian **Youssef Hajjar**, whose study of the Baalbek cult remains the standard reference. When **Alexander the Great** conquered the region in the fourth century BCE, the site acquired the Greek name **Heliopolis**, City of the Sun. It retained this name through the **Seleucid** period and into Roman rule. The Romans attempted to rename it **Colonia Julia Augusta Felix**, but the name did not take, and the older Semitic designation persisted through the Arab period and into the present.

The Romans, displaying their usual talent for religious syncretism, identified the local triad with **Jupiter**, **Venus**, and **Mercury** (or Bacchus), and in the first century CE began the construction project that would make Baalbek one of the grandest religious complexes in the ancient world. The scale of what they built is not subtle. The Temple of Jupiter, the largest of the three main structures, originally stood on fifty-four columns, each nearly twenty meters tall, composed of three enormous drums stacked atop monolithic stone plinths 2.7 meters high. Six of these columns remain standing today, and even in their diminished state they dominate the landscape for kilometers. Hancock ([see Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)), who wrote about the fifty-four columns in *Magicians of the Gods*, noted that fifty-four is a number in the **precessional sequence**: derived from seventy-two (the number of years for one degree of precessional motion), halved to thirty-six, added back to seventy-two for one hundred and eight, then halved again. Whether the Romans chose that number for precessional reasons or for structural ones is unknowable, but the coincidence is on the record.

The **Temple of Bacchus**, smaller but far better preserved, is one of the finest surviving Roman temples anywhere, its interior colonnade and ornamental ceiling still largely intact despite earthquakes, religious upheaval, and centuries of reuse as a fortress. A smaller circular **Temple of Venus** rounds out the complex. The construction spanned most of the first and second centuries CE, from the reign of **Augustus** through **Nero** and possibly into the **Antonine** period.

Aleksandr Sokolov of the Russian science channel Antropogenez (Антропогенез), who visited Baalbek with a team in 2018, emphasized the depth of the scholarly record. The site had been continuously excavated and documented since 1898, when a German archaeological mission began work following a visit by **Kaiser Wilhelm II**. Two years of clearing rubble and sand were required before the temples could even be properly photographed. The French continued the work after World War I, and the **Lebanese Department of Antiquities** maintained excavations through the 1960s and 1970s. A ten-year German-Lebanese program launched in 2001 produced a stream of published results, including detailed reports on pottery, stratigraphy, and seismic vulnerability. Typing "Baalbek archaeology" into **Google Scholar** returns over 6,100 sources, as Sokolov noted, including a book from 1898 and articles fresh from the 2010s. The claim, popular in alternative-history circles and deployed most aggressively by the Russian conspiracy channel **Kramola**, that mainstream archaeologists have ignored or are forbidden from studying Baalbek is factually wrong. Sokolov was blunt: nearly everything in Kramola's own video about Baalbek was a "retelling of information we know thanks to the labor of historians and archaeologists."

What has not been settled, despite all that investigation, is the relationship between the Roman temples and the enormous stones beneath them.


### The Drawing on the Stone

In the mid-1960s, **Emir Maurice Chehab**, Director General of Lebanon's Department of Antiquities, ordered the top of the Trilithon cleared of debris. The work required removing centuries of accumulated rubble, Arab-era fortification walls, and the general detritus of a site that had been continuously inhabited since the collapse of Roman authority. What the workers found on the surface of the southernmost block was unexpected. **Professor Haroutune Kalayan**, the engineer in charge of Baalbek's restoration, described the discovery: the stone bore a full-scale orthographic drawing of the pediment of the Temple of Jupiter. The drawing extended partly under Roman construction and partly under an early Arab fortification wall.

The implication was significant and specific. If Roman architects had used the top surface of the Trilithon stone as a drafting table to plan the dimensions of the temple's pediment, the stone had to have been in place before the temple was built. Not approximately in place; exactly, stably, permanently in place, level enough to serve as a surface for precise architectural drawing. The drawing dated to the beginning of the second half of the first century CE, roughly the reign of Nero. The Trilithon, Kalayan concluded, was already in place «to serve as a trestle board for the dimensioning and ordering of the pediment blocks.» After the pediment was constructed, Roman builders laid further construction on top of the drawing, covering it permanently. This sequence is confirmed by the stratigraphy: the drawing is under the Roman work, not on top of it.

This is the discovery that gives **the Trilithon Problem** its shape. The drawing tells us that the stones were there when the Romans started building the temple's upper structure. It does not tell us who put them there, or when. It tells us only that the Trilithon predates the Temple of Jupiter's pediment. The distance between "predates the Temple of Jupiter" and "predates the Romans" might be a few decades or a few millennia. The drawing alone cannot resolve the question.

**Daniel Lohmann**, a German architect and archaeologist who spent years excavating the site and who corresponded extensively with Hancock in 2015, challenged Kalayan's interpretation. Lohmann was, by Hancock's own account, «extremely thorough and really quite brilliant,» and the correspondence between them was both extensive and civil, a rare achievement in a field where the mainstream and the alternative rarely communicate without hostility. In papers presented in 2009 and 2010, Lohmann argued that the Trilithon and the temple were built synchronously, both as part of a single Roman construction campaign. His detailed analysis identified two distinct building phases: an earlier platform (which he called "Podium 1") that he attributed to **Herod the Great**, the Roman client king who also rebuilt the Temple in Jerusalem; and a later, much larger U-shaped megalithic wall ("Podium 2") that the Julio-Claudian emperors constructed around Podium 1. The Trilithon, in Lohmann's schema, was part of Podium 2: a cosmetic shell intended to «hide the older, inconveniently shaped temple terrace behind a podium in fashionable Roman manner.»

Lohmann's case rested on stylistic parallels between Baalbek's masonry and Herod's documented building projects, particularly the **Temple Mount** platform (the **Haram esh-Sharif**) in Jerusalem. The proportions, the use of alternating rows of headers and stretchers, the drafted-margin stonework: all bore what Lohmann considered unmistakable Herodian signatures. And in the foundation beneath the Trilithon, Kalayan himself had noted a critical detail: a discarded column drum, similar in dimensions to the columns of the Temple of Jupiter, had been used as a foundation block. If a temple column was already available to be repurposed as fill material before the Trilithon was laid, the Trilithon could not predate the temple by very much.

Hancock, who visited Baalbek in 2014, accepted the column drum as evidence that the Trilithon was roughly contemporary with the first century CE construction. But he pressed a question that Lohmann's analysis left open: if Podium 1 was Herodian, was there a "Podium 0" beneath it, something older still, around which the entire sequence of later construction had organized itself? Lohmann conceded that the tell was «continuously inhabited since the pre-pottery Neolithic period,» the age of Gobekli Tepe, but did not consider the possibility of a pre-Herodian megalithic structure. Hancock found this omission telling: the same reluctance to look beneath the documented layers, he argued, characterized the mainstream treatment of the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, where the enormous blocks exposed by the **Hasmonean Tunnel** have never been satisfactorily attributed to Herod or to anyone else.


### The Quarry and the Question of Scale

The three quarry stones were never installed. This is, in itself, informative. If the Romans carved them, as Lohmann and the mainstream consensus hold, then something went wrong between quarrying and transport. If a pre-Roman civilization carved them, then the Romans inherited a site with three unfinished blocks and no instructions.

Richards investigated the Trilithon question at length in a dedicated video essay, noting that the architecture of the wall beneath the Trilithon does not match standard Roman construction patterns. The course of masonry below the three massive blocks consists of large but considerably smaller stones, arranged in an unusual sequence: smaller blocks at the base, then the enormous Trilithon blocks, then smaller blocks again above. Standard Roman and ashlar masonry places the largest stones at the foundation, then works upward with progressively smaller courses, sandwiching the smaller interior courses between the two large exterior ones for earthquake resistance. The Baalbek arrangement reverses this logic. Richards called it «really weird and the kind of thing I dare say you almost never see anywhere else on the planet.»<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup>

Some archaeologists have interpreted marks on the smaller blocks beneath the Trilithon as rope-fixing points, evidence of Roman involvement. Richards acknowledged this but considered it inconclusive: rope marks on stone can be produced by any civilization that uses rope, which is to say, every civilization since the Neolithic. The stones beneath the Trilithon are offset from each other, a design feature used to reduce seismic vulnerability in ashlar masonry. But they are offset irregularly, not in the standardized pattern typical of Roman construction. The result, Richards observed, «looks professional at a casual glance but on closer inspection kind of just seems like they maybe just went with whatever blocks they had next in line.» This is not the hallmark of Roman engineering, which prized regularity to the point of obsession.

Richards also dismantled the comparison frequently drawn between the Trilithon and the **Thunder Stone**, the 1,500-ton granite boulder moved from the shores of the **Gulf of Finland** to **St. Petersburg** in 1770 to serve as the pedestal for the equestrian statue of **Peter the Great**. The Thunder Stone project is often cited by debunkers as proof that pre-industrial societies could move megaliths of comparable weight. Richards argued that it proves the opposite. Moving the Thunder Stone required brass ball bearings of a specific alloy (iron ones were crushed flat by the weight), screw jacks, specialized wooden enclosures, capstans, and the combined effort of hundreds of workers over months. The first spheres were iron, and they failed; eventually brass spheres of a specific composition were manufactured that could sustain the load. The metallurgy and mechanical engineering available to eighteenth-century Russia were substantially different from anything documented in the Roman toolkit. «What Dr. Miano is actually showing,» Richards argued, «is the difference in technology, not the similarity.» Comparing the Thunder Stone project to Baalbek is «like comparing the marksmanship of an M16 with a longbow. These are not the same tool. Just because they're designed to do the same thing does not mean they are remotely the same.»

The Phoenicians, sometimes proposed as alternative builders, fared even worse under Richards's analysis. Phoenician masonry used large amounts of wood, deteriorated far more rapidly than Roman or Greek stonework, and bore no resemblance to the Trilithon wall. Richards found them an even less viable candidate than the Romans.

What remains is a site so thoroughly disturbed by four centuries of pre-scientific tourism and excavation that its stratigraphy is, in Richards's assessment, «completely annihilated.» When the Germans arrived in 1898, they essentially dug holes; nineteenth-century archaeology lacked the methods that modern excavation takes for granted. Visitors since the Ottoman period had been carrying away fragments as souvenirs. By the time modern tools arrived, the contextual evidence that could have settled the dating question was largely gone. A paper on the deep sounding in the Great Courtyard admitted as much: «since the quantity of items in the total assemblage is not very high, and chipstone industries for the post-Neolithic periods are not very well known, chronological developments and toolkit comparisons are not possible at the present stage of research.»

This cuts both ways, as Richards pointed out. When **Brien Foerster** claims that a buildup of dirt on one side of the wall proves the site is older than the Romans, the dirt could have been deposited by anyone in the last four hundred years of uncontrolled digging. When **Dr. Miano** points to a partial column drum found inside the enclosure as evidence of Roman construction, somebody could have thrown that drum in as backfill when digging somewhere else. The stratigraphy is gone. Normal archaeology is at a permanent disadvantage here.


### The Roman Argument

The mainstream case for Roman authorship of the Trilithon rests on several pillars, and they are not trivial.

First, the engineering. **Jean-Pierre Adam**, a French architect and specialist in ancient construction techniques whose work is cited by both Antropogenez and the academic literature, calculated that the Trilithon stones could be moved by organized labor using Roman-era technology. The quarry sits at a slightly higher elevation than the temple platform, meaning no vertical lifting was required for most of the transport distance. Wooden sledges, rollers, earthen ramps, and the labor of roughly a thousand workers could, in principle, slide an 800-ton block down a gentle incline on a well-prepared Roman road surface. Adam estimated that approximately six powerful capstans, each operated by about thirty workers, would suffice for the final positioning. The last phase, rotating the blocks into position against the existing wall, could not use rollers and would have required a friction-reducing medium, perhaps a clay slurry, and a workforce of over a thousand. The physics are not impossible. They are merely at the outer edge of what anyone has documented Romans doing, at a scale that exceeds every other known Roman engineering project by a factor of two.

Second, the epigraphy. The construction-era graffiti on the Trilithon stones, including the pediment drawing discovered by Kalayan, dates to approximately 60 CE. Sokolov noted that on one of the blocks an inscription references the emperor Nero. Roman construction marks, including what some archaeologists interpret as rope-fixing points, appear on the smaller blocks beneath the Trilithon course. The site's formal designation as a Roman colony under Augustus (circa 16 BCE) provides a plausible starting date for the construction campaign. A fragment of a column drum from the Temple of Jupiter was found in the foundation beneath the Trilithon.

Third, the context. The Romans built big. The column drums of the Temple of Jupiter, some weighing over 100 tons each, were raised to a height of twenty meters. Fallen pediment blocks weighing up to 360 tons lie scattered between the Temple of Jupiter and the Temple of Bacchus. Sokolov walked among them and measured them with his hands. The Trilithon blocks are larger than anything else the Romans are documented as having installed, but the engineering tradition from which they would have emerged was clearly comfortable with multi-hundred-ton stones.

Margarete van Ess, the lead archaeologist, argued that using fewer, larger stones was actually more economical than using many smaller ones: fewer transport operations, fewer joints, less fitting. Richards found this argument mathematically plausible and practically absurd. Hauling rubble is inefficient, he conceded, but so is moving stones so large that humanity is still debating how it was done two thousand years later. A «happy medium,» standardized blocks that allow reusable tooling (ramps, rigging, capstans, pulleys, rails, rollers), is what every other construction tradition in history converged on. If the stones are basically identical, «the same tools can be used to transport the stones to the construction site.» By comparison, «trying to grab the biggest stones you can and move them to the site is horribly inefficient.»


### The Sklyarov Dimension

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)) visited Baalbek multiple times with his research team from the **Laboratory of Alternative History** (Лаборатория Альтернативной Истории, or **LAH**). His 2016 book ***Яхве против Баала: хроника переворота*** (Yahweh Against Baal: A Chronicle of the Coup) placed the Baalbek complex at the center of a sweeping argument about divine civilization and territorial control in the ancient Near East. The book's genesis was characteristically spontaneous. During the fourth **International Scientific-Practical Seminar** on traces of technogenic civilizations, held in Israel in April 2016, Sklyarov and two colleagues, **Alexey Pavlov** and **Igor Levashov**, began an impromptu brainstorming session over dinner. Within twenty minutes, they had constructed the skeleton of the entire book: the Israelite conquest of Canaan was, at its core, a story about one faction of gods directing a human population to seize control of ancient megalithic structures built by a technologically advanced civilization.

Sklyarov's framework identified three sites, Baalbek, the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, and the enclosure at the **Cave of Machpelah** in Hebron, as products of a single pre-human building tradition. The team's visit to Machpelah, possible only because they had scheduled the seminar between the seemingly infinite parade of holidays in Israel, confirmed their preliminary assessment from photographs: the walls around the cave «имеют самое прямое отношение к сфере наших поисков и исследований» (are directly relevant to the scope of our searches and research). The massive blocks, the tight joints, the surface treatment of the stone: all matched what they had documented at Baalbek and at the Temple Mount.

The theological framework is speculative, a conclusion that leaps past the available evidence by a considerable margin. Sklyarov proposed that **Yahweh** was a specific member of a technologically advanced civilization who used the Israelites as pawns in a power struggle against Baal, the god whose territory included the Bekaa Valley. The biblical Abraham, in Sklyarov's reading, paid four hundred shekels of silver for the plot at Machpelah not because it contained a cave but because it contained the walls of the gods, and the price reflected the value of divine infrastructure rather than real estate.

Strip the theology away and the observational core remains. Sklyarov's team documented the masonry at all three sites with obsessive attention to surface detail. They photographed joints, measured tolerances, and catalogued what they considered diagnostic differences between the megalithic lower courses and the demonstrably later upper construction. The mainstream attributes all three sites to Herod, who was certainly capable of grand construction. Sklyarov attributed them to an unknown earlier civilization. The physical evidence on the ground, absent the destroyed stratigraphy at Baalbek and the political impossibility of excavating beneath the Temple Mount, cannot currently distinguish between these two possibilities with the certainty that either side claims.


### Three Stones in the Wall

Richards proposed a hypothesis that threads between the mainstream and alternative positions with a pragmatism that neither side entirely welcomes. When the Romans arrived at Baalbek, he suggested, the Trilithon and its associated section of wall were already in place. The sheer scale of the existing masonry forced the Romans into a dilemma. They could not afford to be outdone by whoever had built it, because Roman authority depended in part on the demonstration of Roman engineering superiority. So they built the largest temples they had ever constructed, specifically to match the scale of what they found. They also began quarrying three new blocks of comparable dimensions, intending to complete the wall.

And then they failed.

The quarrying succeeded. The three blocks were cut. But the transport and installation defeated them. The metallurgy available in the first century CE could not produce bearings or fixtures capable of sustaining the concentrated loads. The wooden mechanisms that worked for hundred-ton blocks did not scale. The stones remained in the quarry. The Romans, unwilling to admit the failure to a local population they were trying to impress, simply left the blocks where they lay and allowed the impression to form that they would be installed eventually. One of the three quarry stones was partially cut up during a later construction phase, long after the original motivation had faded.

This accounts for several facts that the purely Roman hypothesis does not easily explain: why three enormous blocks sit finished in the quarry without ever having been installed (if the Romans moved the first three, why stop?); why the wall's masonry pattern does not match standard Roman construction; why the dimensions of the quarry stones match the Roman foot better than the Trilithon stones do (suggesting the quarry stones are Roman copies, not the originals); and why the Romans built the largest temples of their entire empire in a relatively minor provincial city that appears nowhere in Roman records as a site of particular military or administrative importance. Baalbek's disproportionate grandeur makes more sense if the Romans were responding to something already there than if they were building from scratch on a hilltop whose only prior claim to significance was a Phoenician shrine.

Richards was characteristically direct about the speculative nature of his hypothesis: «To be clear, I'm not saying this is a product of ancient high technology.» He pointed to the hundreds of thousands of years during which stone was the premier tool-making and building material for hominids, a period of generational knowledge accumulation that modern humans, who no longer build with stone or make tools from it, have entirely lost. «We have millions and millions and millions of man-hours into it, literally millions, and we've cut that off. It's gone.» The idea that pre-Roman builders might have possessed stone-working techniques that the Romans could observe but not replicate is, in Richards's view, not a claim about alien technology or lost Atlantis. It is a claim about the depth of knowledge that can accumulate over the very long term and the speed with which it can be lost.

The Cosmic Summit Channel presenters pushed the argument further, noting that Roman concrete, **opus caementicium**, itself demonstrates inherited knowledge. This concrete, a blend of volcanic ash, lime, and seawater, undergoes a process called **pozzolanic reaction** that causes it to grow stronger over time, sealing cracks and regenerating its own structural integrity through the formation of **aluminum tobermorite** and **phillipsite** crystals. Scientists at MIT only succeeded in recreating a version of this concrete in 2023, and even then, they required a synthetic variant; the exact Roman recipe remains unreplicated. Whether the Romans discovered this material through trial and error or inherited the knowledge from an earlier tradition is unknown. What is known is that the material science of the ancient world was more sophisticated than modern reconstruction has managed to reproduce, a fact that sits uncomfortably alongside the assumption that the construction methods were always within reach.


### What the Stones Remember

The honest accounting of the Trilithon is shorter than either side would prefer.

The Temple of Jupiter and the Temple of Bacchus are Roman. This is documented by construction-era inscriptions, dateable pottery sequences, architectural style, and the physical presence of distinctively Roman column drums and decorative elements. The date range is the first and second centuries CE. Graffiti from visitors stretches across the subsequent millennia: Greek inscriptions from 1806, Arabic from the medieval period, and, as Sokolov noted with amusement, a Russian name, Nesterov, dated 1860.

The Trilithon stones were in place before the Temple of Jupiter's pediment was built. This is demonstrated by the pediment drawing found on the surface of the southernmost block, which dates to approximately 60 CE. The drawing was used as an architectural plan and subsequently covered by Roman construction.

How much before the temple the Trilithon was put in place remains unresolved. A column drum from the Temple of Jupiter found in the Trilithon's foundation suggests the gap was narrow, possibly only a few decades. Lohmann argues the entire complex was a single Roman project executed in phases. This is the mainstream position, and it has architectural and epigraphic support. It does not explain why the Romans would go to the extraordinary expense and effort of installing 800-ton blocks for a wall that serves no structural, load-bearing purpose, merely surrounding an earlier platform for cosmetic reasons. As Hancock pointed out, the word "podium" implies structural support; the U-shaped wall does not support the Temple of Jupiter. It surrounds it. Calling a non-supporting wall a "podium" is a use of terminology that quietly smuggles in the assumption that the wall and the temple are parts of the same project.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup>

The three quarry stones were never installed. Whether they were Roman projects that failed or earlier projects that the Romans found and considered reusing is unknown. The quarry has been known and visited since antiquity, which means that whatever contextual evidence it once held has been disturbed beyond recovery.<sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup>

The site's continuous occupation from the Neolithic through the Phoenician, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, and Ottoman periods means that every layer of construction was built on, through, or around earlier layers. The stratigraphy is too compromised for the question to be resolved by excavation alone, and there are no inscriptions on the Trilithon itself that identify its builders.<sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup>

What can be said is this: the Romans built some of the most impressive architecture of the ancient world at Baalbek, and they did it in a place where someone, at some point, had already installed three stones that weigh more than anything the Romans are documented as having moved anywhere else in their empire. The graffiti on those stones proves Roman engineers worked on them. It does not prove Roman engineers placed them.<sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup>

That distinction, small enough to fit in the gap between two adjacent Trilithon blocks, has generated more argument per square meter than any other ruin in the Near East.<sup>[16](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[17](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[18](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[19](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[20](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[21](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[22](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[23](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup><sup>[24](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-10)</sup>
