## Chapter 19: The Destruction Layer – Tall el-Hammam and the Bronze Age Cosmic Airburst
The mound rises fifteen meters above the floor of the **Jordan Valley**, a few kilometers northeast of the **Dead Sea**, in a landscape so old that the word "ancient" does not adequately describe it. **Tall el-Hammam** is a tell, one of those stratified accumulations of human settlement that dot the ancient Near East like geological layer cakes, each stratum recording a century or two of habitation, destruction, rebuilding, and destruction again. The lowest levels reach back to the **Chalcolithic** period, five thousand years before Christ. The upper levels belonged, for a time, to the largest fortified city in the southern **Levant**: a walled settlement covering approximately thirty-six hectares, with a palace complex, public monumental architecture, and a population estimated in the tens of thousands. The fortifications included massive mudbrick walls set on stone foundations, and the city sat on a well-watered plain with agricultural land extending to the Jordan River. By any measure of the Bronze Age, this was a place of consequence.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

For sixteen seasons, beginning in 2005, the American archaeologist **Steven Collins** served as dig director at the site. Collins was affiliated with **Trinity Southwest University**, a small institution in Albuquerque, New Mexico, that offers graduate degrees in biblical studies and archaeology. He had come to the Jordan Valley not on a survey grant or as part of a regional mapping project, but with a hypothesis that predated his first trench by years. Collins was looking for **Sodom**.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

He was not the first. The search for Sodom has occupied biblical archaeologists since the discipline's founding in the nineteenth century, and it has consumed careers without producing consensus. The Genesis narrative places the "cities of the plain" in the Jordan Valley, in a region described as "well watered everywhere like the garden of the Lord," but the text is sufficiently vague about exact coordinates that scholars have proposed candidates from the southern Dead Sea basin to the northern Jordan Valley to the eastern shore of what is now the kingdom of **Jordan**. Collins believed that the northern location was correct, that existing scholars had been looking in the wrong place, and that Tall el-Hammam was the site the Bible described.<sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

What he found, or believed he found, in the excavation strata corresponding to the **Middle Bronze Age**, roughly 1650 BCE, was a destruction layer unlike anything the regional archaeology had prepared him for. The city ended. Not gradually, not through the slow abandonment that characterizes economic decline, and not through the organized rubble left by a conquering army that burns what it cannot carry and builds on what remains. The mudbrick walls of the palace complex were vitrified, their outer surfaces fused into glass. Pottery was melted. Human skeletal remains appeared in positions suggesting instantaneous death rather than the scattered disarticulation of battlefield casualties or the curled postures of disease. And in the soil matrix itself, Collins and his colleagues reported finding geochemical signatures that no Bronze Age technology could have produced: shocked quartz, **diamondlike carbon**, **platinum-group element anomalies**, and tiny glass beads called **spherules**, the calling card of extreme heat delivered at extreme speed.<sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

Collins brought these findings to a team of twenty-one scientists spanning geology, physics, mineralogy, and archaeology. The lead author was **Phillip Silvia**, a colleague from Trinity Southwest; the co-authors included **James Kennett**, a geochemist from the **University of California, Santa Barbara**, and **Allen West**, a retired geophysicist who had previously been involved in the **Younger Dryas impact hypothesis** and who would bring to the collaboration both a reputation for controversial claims and expertise in identifying cosmic impact signatures in sediment. The resulting paper took four years to assemble, pass peer review, and reach publication. In September 2021, the journal *Scientific Reports*, a publication of the Nature group, released it under a title that left little room for ambiguity: "A Tunguska-sized airburst destroyed Tall el-Hammam, a Middle Bronze Age city in the Jordan Valley near the Dead Sea."<sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

The central claim was that a cosmic object, comparable in size to the bolide that flattened two thousand square kilometers of Siberian forest on June 30, 1908, had detonated in the atmosphere above the Jordan Valley roughly 3,650 years ago, generating temperatures in excess of 2,000 degrees Celsius, producing a shock wave that stripped the city's mudbrick superstructure from its stone foundations, and depositing a thin layer of exotic debris that the paper's authors called, with a directness unusual in academic prose, **the destruction layer**. The paper ran to fifty-four pages and contained over a hundred figures. It modeled the event computationally, proposed a specific detonation altitude and blast radius, and provided scanning electron microscope images of the materials recovered from the destruction layer. It was, in terms of sheer ambition, one of the most comprehensive claims ever made for a localized cosmic impact in human history.<sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

The paper became, briefly, the most-read scientific article in the world. Within months of publication, it had been accessed over 1.5 million times, a figure that no paper in *Scientific Reports* had previously approached. Coverage appeared in ***The New York Times***, ***The Washington Post***, the **BBC**, and hundreds of smaller outlets. The combination of cosmic violence, biblical resonance, and a destruction mechanism comprehensible to any reader who had ever seen a fireworks display explode overhead made it irresistible to media organizations accustomed to translating academic papers into human interest stories.<sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

Its appeal was not merely scientific. A cosmic airburst arriving without warning from above bore a structural resemblance to the biblical account of Sodom's destruction that Collins was happy to acknowledge. "Fire and brimstone from heaven" is precisely what a low-altitude detonation of a cosmic body would look like to witnesses standing at the edge of the blast zone: a flash of blinding light, a wall of superheated air, and then the destruction of everything within the radius. The parallel was not subtle. It was not accidental. And within months, it was in serious trouble.<sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup><sup>[10](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup><sup>[11](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>


### The Analyst and the Pixels

The first sustained challenge came not from geochemists or Near Eastern archaeologists but from a forensic image analyst with a microscope and a very particular set of skills.<sup>[12](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

**Elisabeth Bik** is a Dutch microbiologist who, after fifteen years of research in microbiology at **Stanford University**, built a second career doing something that no scientific institution seems willing to do at scale: examining the figures in published papers and asking whether the images actually show what the authors claim they show. Her method is straightforward, tedious, and devastating. She downloads micrographs from published papers, increases the magnification, and scans for duplicated regions, rotated panels, spliced images, or digitally manipulated features. Since 2014, she has examined over twenty thousand papers. She has identified problems in roughly four thousand of them. She works largely without institutional support, funded by her own savings and by small grants from organizations that care about scientific integrity. She is, in the peculiar ecosystem of modern science, a one-woman quality-control department that the journals themselves have failed to build.<sup>[13](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

Bik examined the figures in the Tall el-Hammam paper and found irregularities. Several micrographs appeared to contain duplicated or manipulated regions: identical patterns of pixels appearing in what were presented as images of different samples from different locations within the destruction layer. She posted her findings publicly on the platform then called Twitter. Other researchers, drawn by her analysis, began scrutinizing the data with the kind of attention that the peer-review process, in this particular case, had evidently not applied.

The scrutiny expanded. A 2022 counter-study, also published in *Scientific Reports*, went directly at the geochemistry. Researchers who visited the same site and collected their own samples found no mineralogical or geochemical evidence consistent with a cosmic airburst. The shocked quartz reported in the original paper could not be replicated. The platinum anomalies fell within ranges explainable by natural soil chemistry in a region with complex geological history. The spherules were present in quantities and compositions indistinguishable from those produced by ordinary kiln firing, a technology that a Middle Bronze Age city with an active pottery and metallurgy industry would have employed daily. The mudbrick vitrification, presented in the 2021 paper as evidence of temperatures exceeding 1,500 degrees Celsius, was re-examined by researchers who pointed out that mudbrick vitrification occurs in ordinary structural fires at temperatures well within the range of burning roof timbers and stored grain. A city with granaries, oil stores, and wooden architecture does not need a cosmic event to burn spectacularly.

On April 24, 2025, *Scientific Reports* formally retracted the paper. The retraction notice cited "errors in the methodology, analysis and interpretation of the data" and stated that "the comparisons to the Tunguska event were not adequately substantiated." The journal's investigation confirmed that multiple figures contained manipulated data.

Collins and his co-authors disputed the retraction. The paper was gone. The destruction layer was not.


### The Sodom Question

To understand why the retraction matters beyond the narrow technical question of airburst evidence, it is necessary to understand what the airburst hypothesis was built to support.

Collins did not arrive at Tall el-Hammam by accident. He selected the site based on a systematic reading of the Hebrew text of Genesis, which he believed previous scholars had misinterpreted by focusing their search on the southern Dead Sea basin. In Collins's reading, the text places the cities of the plain north of the Dead Sea, in the lower Jordan Valley, visible from the hill country near **Bethel** where **Abraham** is said to have stood. Collins surveyed the valley and identified Tall el-Hammam as the only site of sufficient size and appropriate date in the correct geographic window. His 2013 book, ***Discovering the City of Sodom***, co-authored with **Latayne Scott**, laid out the geographic argument with considerable detail: the sight lines from Bethel, the fertility of the surrounding plain, the presence of a destruction horizon in the correct chronological range, and the absence of competing candidates in the same location.

The identification was rejected by the majority of specialists in biblical geography and Near Eastern archaeology before the airburst paper was even conceived.

**Bill Schlegel**, a biblical geographer who has spent decades mapping the southern Levant, argued that the text places Sodom not in the northern Jordan Valley where Tall el-Hammam sits but farther south, near the Dead Sea's current southern basin, in a region called the **kikkar** in the Hebrew original. The word kikkar, Schlegel contended, refers specifically to the circular plain at the southern end of the Dead Sea, not to the broader valley. Other scholars noted that the Genesis narrative names five "cities of the plain" (Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, Zeboiim, and Zoar) and that no cluster of five contemporary sites has been found in the Tall el-Hammam vicinity.

The chronological objection was equally sharp and more difficult to dismiss. The Genesis narrative, as traditionally dated by biblical scholars using internal chronological references and cross-referencing with Egyptian and Mesopotamian timelines, places the destruction of Sodom in the **Early Bronze Age**, roughly 2100 to 1900 BCE. The destruction layer at Tall el-Hammam dates to approximately 1650 BCE. Three hundred to four hundred years is not a rounding error. It is the difference between the age of the pyramids and the age of the **Hyksos** invasion. Neither mainstream biblical archaeology nor the regional specialists who have spent careers mapping settlement patterns in the southern Levant have found this gap acceptable.

Collins, speaking at the Cosmic Summit conference in 2023, was characteristically undaunted by any of these objections. He arrived with 150 slides and the frank admission that he was, by temperament, an "inveterate rabbit chaser" who had to physically restrain himself from driving down every side tunnel of his subject "to the core of the earth." His presentation had the fluency of a man who has made his case several hundred times to audiences ranging from academic conferences to church groups to podcast interviews. He described dinner conversations about **Akhenaten** and **Moses** and Tutankhamun that left his hosts scrambling for notepads. The Cosmic Summit organizer introduced him as the writer of "the most read scientific paper in the world" and described the dinner the night before as "a movie in the making." Collins radiated the confidence of someone who does not distinguish sharply between passionate advocacy and dispassionate inquiry.

His presentation opened with his own abstract, which he read aloud in full, a choice more common in pulpit oratory than in scientific lectures. The abstract declared that "so-called biblical archaeology can be long on speculation, short on facts, and colored grotesquely by theological predispositions," a framing that presented Collins as the sober empiricist resisting the very biases that his critics accused him of harboring. He proposed what he called the "Historical Synchronisms and Material Specificity Approach," or HSMSA, a methodology that, "as much as is humanly possible," avoids speculation and focuses only on factual data. Whether the methodology lived up to that description was the question the retraction would eventually answer.

The airburst paper, in Collins's telling, was not the reason to believe that Tall el-Hammam was Sodom; it was merely the physical confirmation of what the archaeology and the biblical geography had already established.

The retraction removed the physical confirmation while leaving the underlying geographic argument exactly where it had been before the paper was published: persuasive to those already persuaded, unconvincing to everyone else.


### The Archaeology of Confirmation

The institutional history of biblical archaeology is crowded with researchers who found exactly what they set out to find.

The pattern is not limited to the ancient Near East, and it does not require a religious motivation. It requires only an investigator who is committed to a particular outcome and who interprets ambiguous data through the lens of that commitment. But in biblical archaeology, the stakes are higher than in any other subdiscipline, because the audience extends far beyond the academy. A paper identifying a new pottery type receives attention from a few dozen specialists. A paper confirming a biblical narrative receives attention from millions of people who have a deep personal investment in the outcome. The incentive structure is asymmetric: the professional costs of failure are modest compared to the public rewards of success, and the public does not read retraction notices with the same enthusiasm that it reads discovery announcements.

**Ron Wyatt**, a nurse anesthetist from Nashville, Tennessee, spent decades from the 1970s onward claiming to have discovered Noah's Ark in a boat-shaped geological formation at **Durupinar**, in eastern Turkey, roughly twenty-four kilometers from **Mount Ararat**. He reported finding iron fragments that he identified as rivets and brackets from the Ark's construction, and he publicized his findings through lectures, self-published books, and a network of evangelical supporters who treated his claims as vindication of the literal truth of Genesis. The formation was undeniably boat-shaped. It was also undeniably natural.

**Lawrence Collins** (no relation to Steven), a geologist at **California State University, Northridge**, examined samples from the Durupinar formation and found that among twelve rock specimens, there were no signs of wood. The "chaotic patterns" visible in thin-section analysis bore no resemblance to the structure of petrified timber. The material was basalt and andesite, common volcanic rock. Core drilling showed nothing resembling a hull or keel. The iron fragments that Wyatt had identified as fasteners were iron-bearing minerals, primarily **magnetite**, a standard component of magmatic rock abundantly distributed throughout the volcanic formations of eastern Turkey. Iron traces were found outside the "ark" as well as inside it.

The "anchor stones" found twenty-four kilometers from the site, large rocks with holes drilled through them that Wyatt identified as sea anchors dropped by Noah during the flood, turned out to be local andesite, a rock absent from Mesopotamia, the supposed point of departure for the Ark. Identical stones, complete with identical holes, were found in Christian cemeteries throughout Turkey and Armenia, and at **Carahunge**, an Armenian stone monument sometimes called the "Armenian Stonehenge." They were memorial pillars with rope-holes for hauling, not nautical hardware. As the Antropogenez team observed, the boat shape at Durupinar was a textbook example of **pareidolia**, the same cognitive pattern that makes humans see faces on the Moon or divine images on burnt toast.

Alexander Sokolov of Antropogenez, representing the mainstream Russian scientific position, placed the Tall el-Hammam episode in this longer pattern. The cycle is remarkably consistent: a sensational identification announced to great media fanfare, followed by either quiet retraction or slow erosion of supporting evidence, while the original claim continues to circulate in popular literature and church bulletins unchanged. A particularly instructive example occurred on April 1, 1883, when a New Zealand clergyman and journalist named **George Macready Price** (a man who doubled as a Presbyterian minister) wrote a humorous newspaper item claiming that an avalanche on Mount Ararat had exposed remains of Noah's Ark, complete with an enterprising American who wished to purchase the vessel for exhibition in the United States. The joke was picked up by newspapers across the British Empire and the Americas. Price published a retraction and apology on November 24 of the same year, expressing genuine amazement at how widely his fiction had traveled. The retraction did not matter. The story kept circulating for decades. Sokolov summarized the moral with characteristic dryness: may the force of real science help us all.

The desire to confirm scripture is among the most powerful motivational forces in the history of Near Eastern fieldwork. It has produced discoveries of genuine importance: the **Dead Sea Scrolls** were found by scholars who took the biblical text seriously as a historical source, and **Heinrich Schliemann** found Troy by reading Homer as a literal geographic guide. But the same motivation has produced some of the worst science the discipline has ever seen. The difference between Schliemann and Wyatt is not the motivation but the willingness to let the evidence speak for itself even when it contradicts the hypothesis that brought you to the dig in the first place.


### The Tunguska Comparison

The choice of the **Tunguska event** as the paper's comparative framework was both its greatest rhetorical strength and its most vulnerable scientific point.

The Tunguska airburst of June 30, 1908, is the largest confirmed cosmic impact event in recorded human history. An object estimated at sixty to one hundred meters in diameter entered the atmosphere above central Siberia, traveling at approximately fifteen kilometers per second, and detonated at an altitude of five to ten kilometers above the Stony Tunguska River. The explosion released energy equivalent to ten to fifteen megatons of TNT, roughly a thousand times the yield of the Hiroshima bomb. It flattened approximately two thousand square kilometers of taiga forest, producing a radial pattern of downed trees centered on the detonation point, visible in aerial photographs taken twenty years later when the first scientific expedition reached the site. No crater was formed. The object vaporized entirely in the atmosphere, leaving behind a field of destruction and anomalous concentrations of iridium, nickel, and spherules in the surrounding soil.

The event went largely unstudied for two decades because it occurred in one of the most remote regions on Earth. **Fred Whipple**, writing in 1930, marveled: "It is most remarkable that such an event should occur in our generation and yet be so nearly ignored. No civilized man sought out the falling place of the meteorite for 20 years." When Soviet expeditions finally reached the site in the late 1920s and 1930s, they found the forest still flattened, the trees still lying in radial patterns, and no trace of the object that had done the damage. The absence of a crater and of any recoverable fragments led to decades of speculation about the nature of the impactor (asteroid, comet, or something more exotic). The current scientific consensus favors a stony asteroid that broke apart and vaporized during atmospheric entry, never reaching the ground intact.

The comparison allowed the Tall el-Hammam paper to accomplish two things simultaneously. It provided a modern, well-documented analogue for an event that would otherwise seem implausible: a city-destroying explosion with no visible impactor and no crater. And it connected the Jordan Valley destruction to a phenomenon that is physically uncontroversial. Cosmic airbursts are not theoretical constructs. They happen. The 2013 **Chelyabinsk** event in Russia, caused by a bolide roughly twenty meters across, injured over 1,500 people and damaged approximately 7,200 buildings with its shock wave alone, and it was a fraction of Tunguska's energy. Airbursts of Tunguska scale are estimated to occur on average once every few hundred to few thousand years.

The question was never whether such events are physically possible. The question was whether the specific evidence at Tall el-Hammam was actually consistent with one, and the retraction answered that question in the negative. When the figures that are supposed to show you the evidence have been manipulated, the question of what the evidence actually shows becomes, for practical purposes, impossible to adjudicate. The credibility of the data is destroyed, and credibility, once destroyed, does not reassemble itself merely because someone insists the underlying conclusions were correct.

Carlson ([Chapter 4](04-the-cubit-coincidence.md)) discussed the broader context at the Cosmic Summit, noting that the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis and the Tall el-Hammam claim operate at very different scales of evidence but are often rhetorically conflated by critics of both. The difference is important. The Younger Dryas hypothesis has been accumulating supporting data from independent research groups across three continents for over fifteen years, with findings of nanodiamonds, platinum anomalies, high-temperature spherules, and meltglass replicated at more than fifty sites from North America to Europe to the Middle East. The Tall el-Hammam paper was a single study from a single team, and it was retracted. Conflating the two is the intellectual equivalent of dismissing all oncology because one pharmaceutical company fabricated trial data.<sup>[14](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>


### What the Layer Actually Contains

Strip away the Sodom identification and the cosmic airburst hypothesis, and what remains at Tall el-Hammam is still a legitimate archaeological puzzle.<sup>[15](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-19)</sup>

The city was destroyed, abruptly and violently, around 1650 BCE. The destruction layer is real. It contains evidence of intense heat, though the temperatures involved are now disputed. The mudbrick superstructure of the city was obliterated while the stone foundations survived, a pattern consistent with intense conflagration but also, it must be said, with earthquake damage followed by fire in a city whose mudbrick architecture was sealed with bitumen and whose granaries contained thousands of kilograms of combustible grain. The site was abandoned after the destruction and not reoccupied for approximately six hundred years, a gap that is unusual but not unprecedented in the archaeology of the Levant.

Bronze Age cities were typically rebuilt within a generation of their destruction, because the geographic advantages that made a site worth settling (water, arable land, trade routes) did not disappear when the buildings burned. The six-century gap at Tall el-Hammam therefore demands an explanation that goes beyond simple destruction. The 2021 paper attributed it to soil contamination: salt and other materials deposited by the airburst shock wave rendering the land infertile. The alternative explanation is more mundane but more consistent with the broader regional record. The broader Jordan Valley experienced a demographic collapse during the same period. At least fourteen settlements in the lower valley show destruction layers roughly contemporaneous with the main site. Whether this pattern reflects a single catastrophic event or the same political, economic, and possibly climatic disruptions that accompanied the Hyksos expulsion from Egypt (which occurred in the same century), or some combination of the two, remains an open question.

The salt is real. The destruction layer at Tall el-Hammam contains anomalously high concentrations of **sodium chloride**. The 2021 paper attributed this to a Dead Sea surface layer ejected by the airburst shock wave and deposited over the surrounding agricultural land. The counter-study noted that high salt concentrations in Jordan Valley soils require no extraordinary explanation. The Dead Sea, the most saline body of water on Earth's surface, sits a few kilometers from the site. Salt migrates through soil via capillary action. It accumulates in ruins over centuries as water evaporates and leaves its dissolved minerals behind. A six-hundred-year occupational gap is plenty of time for natural salt accumulation to reach levels that the 2021 paper attributed to an airburst.

The observation stands. The cosmic explanation does not.

What has been lost in the noise of the retraction is that the puzzle of the Jordan Valley's Middle Bronze Age collapse has not been solved. The region experienced something that ended urban life across a wide area for centuries. At **Jericho**, roughly twenty-five kilometers southwest of Tall el-Hammam, **Kathleen Kenyon** documented a comparable destruction layer from the same general period. At **Tell es-Sultan**, the fortification walls were collapsed outward, a pattern that has been debated for decades without resolution. Across the lower Jordan Valley, a dozen smaller tells show evidence of destruction and abandonment in the same temporal window. These are not the signatures of a single city's bad luck. They are the signatures of a regional catastrophe, and the honest answer is that the cause of that catastrophe has not been identified. It may have been seismic. It may have been political. It may have been a combination of factors that archaeology, working at the granularity of centuries rather than years, will never be able to disentangle. The airburst hypothesis offered a tidy explanation for a messy pattern. The pattern remains. The explanation is gone.


### The Galileo Inversion

Collins and his supporters have occasionally invoked the comparison to **Galileo**: a lone researcher persecuted by an establishment that refuses to accept revolutionary evidence. The comparison is instructive, but not in the way Collins intends.

Galileo's evidence was replicable. Anyone with a telescope could observe the phases of Venus and the moons of Jupiter. The Inquisition's objection was not that Galileo's observations were fabricated but that they contradicted scripture. Collins's situation is the inverse: his evidence, as presented in the retracted paper, was not replicable. The 2022 counter-study examined the same site and could not reproduce the geochemical signatures. The manipulated figures raised questions not about the interpretation of the data but about the data itself. In Galileo's case, the establishment denied real evidence because it was theologically inconvenient. In Collins's case, the evidence itself is in question, and the theological convenience of the hypothesis is what kept it in circulation long after its scientific foundations had been undermined.

The Antropogenez team, analyzing the broader pattern of biblical archaeology, noted that the Galileo comparison is among the most frequently misused analogies in fringe science. The fact that Galileo was right and was persecuted does not establish that everyone who claims to be persecuted is right. The Inquisition's suppression of heliocentrism was wrong because the heliocentric model was correct, not because institutional opposition is always wrong. The relevant question is never "does the establishment resist this claim?" (the establishment resists many claims, most of them wrong) but "does the evidence actually support it?"

At Tall el-Hammam, the answer, as of 2025, is: the evidence supports a violent Bronze Age destruction event. It does not support a cosmic airburst. The paper that claimed otherwise was retracted for data manipulation. That is not persecution. That is quality control, arriving late.


### The Residue

The story of Tall el-Hammam is, at its core, a story about what happens to evidence when the desired conclusion precedes the inquiry by decades.

Collins began his career searching for Sodom. He selected his dig site based on a reading of Genesis. He interpreted his findings through the lens of that reading. When the destruction layer presented itself, with its genuine evidence of intense heat and sudden termination, the airburst hypothesis offered a mechanism that aligned with the biblical narrative in a way that no mundane explanation (earthquake, siege warfare, accidental conflagration) could match. The cosmic airburst was not merely a physical hypothesis. It was a theological confirmation. Fire from the sky was what Genesis described, and fire from the sky was what the paper delivered.

This does not mean Collins fabricated evidence. The retraction was based on manipulated figures in a twenty-one-author paper, and many hands touched that data before it reached print. Scientists who have worked on multi-author papers know that no single author reviews every figure in every co-author's contribution with the intensity that Elisabeth Bik brings to her work. Collins has disputed the retraction and continues to defend the underlying findings. The site is still being excavated. Data is still being collected. The destruction layer is still there.

But the paper is gone, and the gap it leaves behind is filled by the same question that existed before it was published: what destroyed the largest city in the Middle Bronze Age Jordan Valley, and why was the land not resettled for six hundred years?

The evidence supports a violent destruction event of some kind. It does not support a cosmic airburst. It does not support the identification with Sodom. What it supports is the prosaic but important conclusion that a significant Bronze Age city met a sudden end, for reasons that have not been definitively determined, in a region where sudden ends were not uncommon.

No one has proposed a mechanism for the six-century abandonment that is as dramatic as a cosmic airburst or as satisfying as divine wrath. The answers that remain are the quiet ones: disrupted trade networks, exhausted agricultural land, contaminated water sources, the accumulated psychological weight of a catastrophe that local populations remembered and did not wish to revisit, or simply the drift of human settlement patterns in a region where political power shifted constantly between Egyptian, Hyksos, and local Canaanite spheres. These explanations lack the grandeur of fire from the sky. They have the compensating virtue of not having been retracted.

Collins will return to the site. The trenches will deepen. New strata will be exposed. The destruction layer will continue to sit in the stratigraphy, stubbornly real, waiting for an explanation that is as honest as the question it poses.

That paper was supposed to provide one. As of April 24, 2025, it is no longer part of the scientific record.
