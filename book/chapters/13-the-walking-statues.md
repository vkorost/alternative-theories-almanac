## Chapter 13: The Walking Statues – Easter Island, the Moai, and Ecological Collapse
Eighteen people, three ropes, and a statue that weighed slightly more than a pickup truck. That was the experiment. In 2011, archaeologists **Carl Lipo** and **Terry Hunt** stood on a dirt road on the Hawaiian island of **Oahu**, watching a 4.35-metric-ton concrete replica of a **moai** rock forward on its D-shaped base, tilting left, then right, then left again, in a motion that looked less like heavy hauling and more like a very large man trying to walk home after a very long evening. One team pulled forward from a rope attached to the statue's head. Two teams on the flanks kept it from toppling sideways. The statue lurched, tilted, and advanced one hundred meters in forty minutes.

The islanders of **Rapa Nui** had been saying this for centuries. The statues walked.

It was the kind of explanation that arrives with a shrug and leaves the listener wondering why anyone spent decades arguing about it. European visitors, beginning with **Jacob Roggeveen** in 1722, had looked at the moai and immediately assumed some lost technology, some forgotten labor force, some engineering secret that the impoverished people living among the toppled statues could not possibly possess. Thor Heyerdahl, the Norwegian adventurer who sailed a balsa raft from Peru to Polynesia in 1947 to prove ancient maritime contact, organized an experiment in 1956 in which islanders dragged a moai flat on a wooden sledge. It worked, technically, but the damage to the road surface and the number of people required made the method implausible at the scale the island demanded. A Czech engineer named **Pavel Pavel** tried rocking a moai forward on its base in 1986 and cracked the statue. Another team tried log rollers; the logs splintered. The moai seemed to resist every method of transport that involved treating them as dead weight.

And then Lipo and Hunt tried treating them as walking machines, and it worked on the first attempt.

Their 2025 peer-reviewed study, published in the *Journal of Archaeological Science*, extended this insight from a single experiment to a comprehensive morphological analysis of 962 moai across the entire island. The statues that had been abandoned along the ancient roads, the ones that never reached their final platforms, had a distinctive set of features: wider bases, a more pronounced forward lean in their center of mass, and a D-shaped cross-section at the bottom that would allow controlled rocking. The statues that sat atop the **ahu**, the stone platforms along the coast where they were meant to stand in perpetuity, had different proportions. The road statues were designed to walk. The platform statues were designed to stand. These were not engineering failures scattered along a path of broken ambition. They were purpose-built vehicles for their own transport, and the roads themselves, 4.5 meters wide with slightly concave surfaces, were purpose-built to stabilize the rocking motion.

**The Walking Statues** is a concept that sounds like a metaphor until you see the video footage. Then it sounds like the most obvious explanation in the world, and the only remaining question is why it took anyone so long to try it.


### The Island at the End of the World

Rapa Nui sits 3,700 kilometers west of Chile and 4,000 kilometers east of Tahiti, a volcanic triangle of 163.6 square kilometers in the middle of the largest ocean on Earth. On a clear day, you can see the sea from virtually anywhere on the island. The nearest inhabited land is **Pitcairn Island**, 2,075 kilometers to the west, population roughly fifty, famous primarily for harboring the descendants of the *Bounty* mutineers. By any reasonable measure, Rapa Nui is one of the most isolated inhabited places on the planet. The airport at **Mataveri**, the island's sole settlement of **Hanga Roa**, is sometimes cited as the most remote commercial airport in the world. It was reconstructed in 1986 not for tourism but by **NASA**, which needed a backup emergency landing strip for the **Space Shuttle**. The island that ancient Polynesians reached by canoe owes its modern accessibility to the American space program.

Finding the island in the first place was not, Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)) argued, the impossible feat that landlocked observers imagine. In ***Мифы об острове Пасхи*** (Myths About Easter Island), he cited the account of the Russian navigator **Yuri Lisyansky**, who wrote in 1803: «Мы увидели остров, не доходя до него 40 миль, хотя горизонт был и не совершенно чист» (We saw the island from forty miles away, even though the horizon was not perfectly clear). In fair weather, Lisyansky estimated, the island would be visible from sixty miles. And in any weather, the clouds that form above an island as moist oceanic air rises and cools are visible from far beyond the island itself. Experienced Polynesian navigators, born on islands and raised on the ocean, would have known to read the sky for exactly these signs. The Spanish expedition of 1770 spotted sea birds several days before reaching Easter Island. Finding a speck of land in the Pacific is a feat of navigation, but it is not a miracle. It is a skill set, and the Polynesians had it.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup><sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup>

Sklyarov visited the island in October 2013 as part of a research expedition organized by the **Foundation for the Development of Science "Third Millennium."** He was characteristically blunt about the state of prior scholarship. Most published works on Easter Island, he noted, were not independent research but retellings of other people's findings. The number of authors who had actually visited the island and based their conclusions on firsthand observation could be counted on one hand. And even those original works diverged so wildly in their conclusions that it was clear each author had been selecting evidence to support a preferred theory rather than describing what was actually there. «Ни одна (!) из них этой проверки не выдержала» (Not a single one of them withstood this verification), he wrote of the existing hypotheses after comparing them with conditions on the ground.

The island's first European contact came on Easter Sunday 1722, when Roggeveen's Dutch expedition anchored offshore and sent a landing party. What Roggeveen recorded was a society that venerated its stone figures with visible devotion. «Туземцы, по всей видимости, не имели никакого оружия и, как я думаю, они во всем полагались на своих богов и идолов, которые стояли в большом количестве на берегу и перед которыми они падали ниц и молились» (The natives apparently had no weapons and, as I think, placed all their trust in their gods and idols, which stood in great numbers on the shore and before which they fell prostrate and prayed), Roggeveen wrote in his journal. But Sklyarov questioned whether the Europeans had actually understood what they were seeing. The Rapa Nui might have been reverencing the ahu platforms, not the statues standing on them. In the islanders' own cosmology, it was the platforms that held **mana**, spiritual power. The moai were, in their own telling, «мертвые» (dead), without mana. Roggeveen, carrying European assumptions about religious statuary, might simply have seen what his cultural framework prepared him to see.

The total count of moai on the island approaches nine hundred, though estimates vary. They range from small figures barely a meter tall to the largest specimen ever attempted, a behemoth left unfinished in the quarry at **Rano Raraku** that would have stood roughly twenty-one meters tall and weighed an estimated 270 metric tons. The overwhelming majority were carved from compressed volcanic ash, or **tuff**, quarried from Rano Raraku's inner slopes. The quarry itself is an extinct volcanic caldera whose inner walls are studded with statues in every stage of completion: some supine, some vertical, some jutting at improbable angles from the hillside, as though a factory had been abandoned mid-shift with no one bothering to switch off the lights.

**Jahannah James**, who visited the island in the early 2020s, described the experience of standing among the moai with an intimacy that research papers cannot convey. The famous fifteen statues of **Ahu Tongariki** and the seven of **Ahu Akivi** were nearly empty of tourists when she arrived. Most of the moai that had spent centuries exposed to wind and rain had lost their carvings entirely. Only a handful of statues on a single stretch of sandy beach, at **Anakena**, retained the faintest outlines of what had once been elaborate designs: spirals and geometric patterns carved into their backs, representing the tattoos of the individuals they depicted. The sand had buried and preserved them while the rest of the island's statues were scoured blank. The red stone cylinders balanced atop the moai's heads, often described as hats, were not hats. They were **pukao**, topknots representing hair, carved from a different quarry of red scoria.

James's tour guide mentioned something that most visitors do not learn. Beneath the soil, there are moai that are fully covered, completely invisible from the surface, known to the islanders through ground-penetrating surveys or through oral tradition passed down before the knowledge holders were taken. These buried statues have never been excavated and, according to the guide, never will be. Whatever carvings exist on their backs, preserved by centuries of burial in the same way the Anakena sand preserved the beach moai, will remain unseen. The decision to leave them is the islanders' own, and it carries a certain grim logic: the last time outsiders took an active interest in the island's heritage, half the population was shipped to Peru in chains.

Below the visible statues lay more. Excavations by Heyerdahl in 1956 and 1987 revealed that many Rano Raraku moai were buried up to nine meters deep in sediment, with full bodies and arms extending far below the surface. Hancock ([see Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)), in *Magicians of the Gods*, pressed the implication: how could such massive sedimentation accumulate in just a few hundred years on an island of only sixty-three square miles? The mainstream dating places the end of moai production around 1650 CE. Hancock and geologist Robert Schoch ([Chapter 7](07-the-rain-that-wasnt.md)) argued that the depth of burial implied a far greater antiquity, possibly extending back to the end of the last Ice Age, when lower sea levels would have made Easter Island part of a much larger archipelago. A small number of moai carved from basalt presented an additional puzzle: no basalt deposits exist on the island's current surface, leading Schoch to speculate that the basalt quarries now lie beneath the ocean.<sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup>


### The Navel of the World

The Rapa Nui name for their island, or one of them, is **Te Pito o Te Henua**, the navel of the world. This is a concept with deep resonance across Pacific and global cultures, known variously as the **axis mundi** or **omphalos**: the point where creation began, the spot connecting earth to the divine, the center of everything.

Richards pointed out the obvious paradox. Every other culture that claims a world navel placed it at the center of their civilization: **Jerusalem**, Cusco, **Delphi**, the temples of India. These were capitals, seats of power, places that everyone could reach. The Polynesians put their navel of the world on the most remote scrap of inhabited land in the Pacific Ocean, a place so far from anywhere that even reaching it by modern jet requires five hours from Santiago. If you were deliberately choosing the least convenient location for a sacred center, you could not do much better than Rapa Nui.

Richards proposed that the paradox dissolves if you change the timeframe. Before the end of the last Ice Age, when sea levels were roughly 120 meters lower, the submarine ridges extending east and west from Easter Island would have been above water or near the surface, forming a chain of islands linking South America to western Polynesia. Under those conditions, Rapa Nui would not have been isolated at all. It would have been a waypoint, perhaps even a major one, on a trans-Pacific route. The world navel designation might be a fossil memory of that earlier geography, preserved in oral tradition long after the sea swallowed the connecting islands and left Rapa Nui stranded in open ocean.

This is speculative, and Richards was candid about it. But the Rapa Nui foundation myth, the story of **Hotu Matu'a**, fits the pattern in unsettling ways. The legend holds that Hotu Matu'a, a king on a distant island called **Hiva**, learned that his homeland was about to be swallowed by the sea. He sent seven scouts eastward to find a new land. They discovered Rapa Nui, reported back, and then the entire community made the voyage to settle the island. The seven moai at Ahu Akivi, the only platform situated inland rather than on the coast, are said to represent these seven scouts, and they face the sea rather than inward, as though still watching for what they left behind.

Sklyarov noted a buried detail in the legend that most researchers had overlooked. When the seven scouts arrived on Rapa Nui, the text mentions that one of them encountered someone already living there, someone who told them a still older story: that the island had once been much larger but had partly sunk beneath the waves. And the scouts themselves, in the telling recorded by French ethnographer **Francis Maziere** in the 1960s, referred to a moai already standing on the island. «Направо, Рухи, налево, Пу, вокруг шеи моаи Хина Риру лежит жемчужное ожерелье» (To the right, Rukhi; to the left, Pu; around the neck of the moai Hina Riru lies a pearl necklace), one scout tells another. If this detail is not a later interpolation, the moai predated the Polynesian settlement. Someone, or something, was there first. Sklyarov pressed the point: «остров Пасхи был обитаем еще до прибытия разведчиков Хоту Матуа» (Easter Island was inhabited even before the arrival of the scouts of Hotu Matu'a).<sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup><sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup><sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup><sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup><sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup><sup>[9](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-13)</sup>


### The Two Peoples

In July 2024, a genetic study did something that mainstream archaeology had resisted for decades: it confirmed that the Rapa Nui carried approximately ten percent **Native American** DNA, specifically from Andean South American populations. The genetic signature was not a product of post-European-contact admixture. It predated the arrival of Europeans and appeared to have traveled westward through other Polynesian island populations, suggesting sustained contact rather than a single accidental voyage.

This finding vindicated, at least in part, the most mocked hypothesis in Pacific archaeology. Sklyarov devoted the opening chapter of his Easter Island book to the story, and it is worth telling in full because it illustrates how the boundaries of acceptable scientific inquiry are policed.

On April 28, 1947, six men, five Norwegians and one Swede, assembled in the port of **Callao** near Lima, Peru, aboard a rectangular balsa-wood raft held together without a single nail or metal fastener. The raft was named **Kon-Tiki**, after the Inca sun god. Its builder and commander, Thor Heyerdahl, intended to sail it across the Pacific Ocean to demonstrate that pre-Columbian South Americans could have reached Polynesia. The crowd that gathered to watch them depart, Sklyarov noted, consisted largely of people who regarded the crew as suicidal lunatics. One hundred and one days later, after covering roughly 6,900 kilometers, the Kon-Tiki made landfall on the **Raroia** atoll in the **Tuamotu** Islands. The raft had survived the crossing, and its crew was alive.

The academic response was to acknowledge the physical possibility and deny the historical probability. Heyerdahl had proved that a balsa raft could cross the Pacific. He had not proved that anyone ever did. The archaeological establishment maintained its position: Polynesian settlement proceeded west to east, from Southeast Asia through Melanesia and Micronesia, not east to west from South America. Heyerdahl was a popularizer, an adventurer, a man who sailed rafts and made documentaries. He was not, in the eyes of professional archaeologists, a serious scholar. For decades, the official position held that South America contributed nothing to Pacific settlement, and that any superficial resemblance between Rapa Nui stonework and Andean masonry was coincidental. Richards, who had studied the Heyerdahl controversy in detail, was blunt about the dynamic: the wall that had been erected against the Peru-Easter Island connection was not built on evidence. It was built on the professional consequences of questioning an established narrative.

The 2024 genetic paper dismantled this position with data. The researchers initially found no statistically significant Native American ancestry in previously published ancient DNA from Rapa Nui, but recognized that the low sequencing depth of earlier studies could explain the absence. Through a downsampling experiment and alternative analytical approaches, they detected the Native American component in three out of five previously published ancient samples. The admixture was real, and it was old.

The same year, a study of starch grains embedded in obsidian tools from Anakena, the oldest confirmed habitation site on the island, revealed traces of breadfruit, ginger, and sweet potato. Breadfruit and ginger are of Southeast Asian origin. Sweet potato is South American. The paper was careful in its language: the finding of breadfruit represented "the third and earliest record of the species in the archaeological context of East Polynesia." The ginger finding was described as "the first evidence that the species was introduced to Easter Island during early settlement." These were not casual discoveries. They were the kind of data that rewrites textbooks.

The earliest settlers of Rapa Nui, whatever their primary identity, brought foodstuffs from both sides of the Pacific. The implications rippled outward: if the founding population had contact with both Asia and South America, then the neat narrative of unidirectional Polynesian expansion required revision. The question of when the island was first settled also grew more complicated. Radiocarbon dating of ant species found in sediment cores from Rapa Nui produced dates approximately two thousand years older than the accepted settlement date of roughly 1200 CE. The researchers acknowledged three possible explanations: that humans visited the island millennia before the currently accepted settlement, inadvertently introducing ants; that the ants were indigenous to Rapa Nui, having arrived via floating vegetation or bird transport; or that the ants had ingested old carbon within the local food web, skewing their apparent age. If the first explanation is correct, the implications extend beyond Easter Island: the ant-based dating method used across Polynesia to establish settlement timelines would need to be revisited at every site where it was employed.

Richards connected this evidence to a pattern he had observed across the broader Pacific. Easter Island's moai, while unique in their scale and concentration, share an iconographic vocabulary with South American traditions: the crossed arms, the forward-facing posture, the elongated heads. The **Candelabra of the Andes**, a 180-meter-tall geoglyph carved into a desert hillside near the Paracas caves in Peru, faces the open ocean and is visible from twelve miles offshore. Whether it was a navigational marker, a territorial claim, or something else, it was made to be seen by people arriving by sea. And Paracas, of course, is the site of the elongated skulls, a practice found across the Pacific but conspicuously absent from Rapa Nui itself, though the moai's own elongated heads suggest at least an iconographic awareness of the tradition.


### The Collapse That Wasn't

For a generation, the dominant narrative about Easter Island was a parable of ecological self-destruction. **Jared Diamond**, in his 2005 bestseller ***Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed***, presented Rapa Nui as the definitive case study in civilizational suicide. The islanders cut down every tree, exhausted their soil, triggered a famine, descended into warfare, toppled their own statues in factional rage, and were found by Europeans in a state of destitution and near-starvation. It was a morality tale with global implications: this is what happens when humans consume faster than their environment can regenerate. The story was clean, powerful, and wrong.

Or, at minimum, severely incomplete. Hunt and Lipo, the same researchers who demonstrated the walking-moai transport method, published ***The Statues that Walked*** in 2011, arguing that Diamond's narrative rested on a fundamental misunderstanding of the archaeological record. The deforestation was real, but the primary agent was not human profligacy. It was the **Polynesian rat**, *Rattus exulans*, an invasive species that arrived with the first settlers and consumed palm seeds at a rate that prevented forest regrowth. The islanders did not chainsaw their way through the palm forests. The rats ate the future.

Ancient DNA studies published in 2024 delivered a further blow to the ecocide model. The genetic data showed no evidence of a pre-European population crash, the signature event that Diamond's narrative required. The island, it appeared, never supported the massive population of fifteen thousand or more that the collapse model assumed. Radiocarbon dating from multiple sites suggested a smaller, more stable population that practiced what Hunt and Lipo described as resilient resource management: rock gardens called **manavai** that sheltered crops from wind, lithic mulching to retain moisture in volcanic soil, and selective harvesting patterns that maintained marine resources over centuries.

The real collapse came with European contact. In 1862, Peruvian slavers raided the island and abducted approximately 1,500 people, roughly half the remaining population, including the last holders of traditional knowledge, the **ariki** priests who could read the island's indigenous script, **rongorongo**. The captives were shipped to Peru to mine guano on the **Chincha Islands**. When international pressure forced their repatriation, only fifteen survivors returned, and they brought smallpox. Within a decade, the Rapa Nui population had fallen below two hundred. The traditions died with the elders. The rongorongo script, incised on wooden boards in a fully developed writing system unprecedented for a small island culture, became unreadable. No examples remain on Easter Island itself. The surviving tablets are scattered across museums in Europe and the Americas, copies of copies of copies of originals that no living person can decipher.

Mainstream archaeology now largely sides with Hunt and Lipo over Diamond on the question of pre-contact collapse, though the debate is not fully settled. What is not in dispute is that the story Diamond told, the story of a primitive people who destroyed themselves through greed and stupidity, was precisely the kind of narrative that European colonial frameworks had been applying to indigenous cultures for centuries.

Richards made the point with characteristic directness. In 2024, a study commissioned to test the DNA of fifteen Easter Island natives used samples collected between 1877 and the 1930s. Every single sample came from French museums. Not one came from the Easter Island Museum's own collections. **Kansas University**, the institution of one of the most vocal critics of Graham Hancock's Netflix series, had yet to fully comply with the **Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act**, which was passed in 1990. The scientist who collected the 1877 samples did so twelve years after the slave raid that nearly extinguished the Rapa Nui population. The bones of enslaved people, collected by colonial-era scientists, stored in foreign museums for a century and a half, were now yielding the genetic data that overturned the theories built on the assumption of the islanders' isolation. The irony was not lost on Richards, and he did not soften it.

The Rapa Nui did not fail. They were conquered, enslaved, and depopulated by outside forces, and then the story of what happened to them was rewritten by the conquerors as a cautionary tale about the conquered.


### The Script That No One Can Read

There is one more anomaly on Easter Island that belongs in its own category, because it is not a question about stone or bones or DNA. It is a question about the mind.

**Rongorongo** is a writing system. Not a set of symbols, not a collection of decorative marks, but a fully developed script with roughly 120 distinct glyphs arranged in lines that read alternately left-to-right and right-to-left, a pattern called **reverse boustrophedon** in which the reader rotates the tablet 180 degrees at the end of each line. The script was incised on wooden boards, and the surviving examples, roughly two dozen tablets and fragments scattered across museums in Europe, Chile, and the United States, are copies of copies of older originals. None of the originals survive. None of the copies remain on Easter Island.

Hancock, in *Magicians of the Gods*, stated the puzzle plainly: it is unprecedented in human history for a sophisticated, fully developed writing system to be invented by a small, isolated island community. Writing systems in every other recorded case emerge from large, complex, urbanized societies with extensive trade networks, administrative needs, and long histories of proto-writing and symbolic notation. The Sumerians had cuneiform because they needed to track grain shipments. The Egyptians had hieroglyphs because they needed to record dynastic histories. The Chinese had oracle bone script because they needed to communicate with their ancestors. Rapa Nui, with a population that may never have exceeded three thousand, and with no known trade partners, no administrative bureaucracy, and no record of proto-writing stages, produced rongorongo.

No one alive can read it. The last literate speakers were the ariki priests abducted in 1862. A few fragmentary accounts from the nineteenth century suggest that the tablets were chanted aloud in ritual contexts, but the missionaries who collected the tablets before the slave raid made no sustained effort to record their contents. By the time anyone thought to try systematic decipherment, there was no one left to ask.

The script remains undeciphered as of 2026. Multiple attempts at decipherment have been published, none successfully. The tablets sit in their museum cases, the incised glyphs as crisp as the day they were carved, the language they encode as silent as the statues that once watched over the island where they were written.


### The Unfinished Quarry

The quarry at Rano Raraku remains the most striking archaeological feature on the island, and possibly the most puzzling. Approximately 270 moai sit in various stages of completion on the caldera's inner slopes, and roughly 165 more lie abandoned along the ancient roads leading away from the quarry. Something halted production. The conventional explanation is that the social system that organized moai construction collapsed, either through the factional warfare that Diamond described or through the general disruption of European contact. But the scene at Rano Raraku does not look like a gradual decline. It looks like a sudden stop, a worksite abandoned between shifts.

**Russ Allen**, co-host of the **Brothers of the Serpent** podcast and a construction professional who has spent decades operating heavy machinery, visited Rano Raraku with an eye trained by a career in moving heavy objects. What struck him was not the scale of individual statues but the pattern of incompletion. Some moai were nearly finished, needing only to be detached from the bedrock at their backs. Some had been separated and were ready for transport. Some had been tilted into vertical position and apparently left standing, as though awaiting a signal that never came. The distribution did not suggest a labor force that was slowly running out of energy. It suggested a labor force that put down its tools.

The walking-statue hypothesis resolves the transport puzzle but introduces its own questions. If eighteen people could move a 4.35-ton statue one hundred meters in forty minutes, what about the largest moai, which weigh eighty tons or more? Lipo and Hunt's model scales, up to a point: larger statues require more people and more ropes, but the physics of the rocking gait remain the same. The abandoned road statues, with their distinctive transport-optimized morphology, suggest that the builders understood the engineering constraints intimately. A wider base meant more stability during the walk. A more pronounced forward lean meant less force needed to initiate each rocking step. The statues were, in effect, self-transporting machines, their own bodies engineered to cooperate with the rope teams pulling them forward.

But the moai at Rano Raraku also tell a story about time that is harder to reconcile with conventional dating. Heyerdahl's 1956 excavations revealed statues buried up to nine meters in sediment. Hancock argued that this depth of burial required thousands of years, not hundreds, and that the quarry's history might extend deep into the last Ice Age. Mainstream geologists counter that volcanic ash weathers rapidly into clay in tropical conditions and that the Rano Raraku caldera, as a natural sediment trap, could accumulate deposits faster than an open landscape. The question remains unresolved because no definitive stratigraphic dating of the buried moai has been published.

Sklyarov, characteristically, asked a different question entirely. He noted that the Rapa Nui oral tradition contains a reference to moai already standing on the island when the Polynesian settlers arrived. If that tradition preserves a genuine memory, then the earliest moai were not carved by the ancestors of the current population at all, but by someone who came before. «Кто-то же ему должен был это рассказать» (Somebody must have told him), Sklyarov wrote of the scout who allegedly learned the island's deeper history from a pre-existing inhabitant. The possibility of a pre-Polynesian occupation of Rapa Nui is not mainstream, but it is not entirely unsupported: the anomalous ant radiocarbon dates, the multi-layered pollen and charcoal deposits suggesting more than one episode of human presence, and the breadfruit and ginger traces from the earliest cultural layer all point to a more complex settlement history than the standard single-migration model allows.


### What Stands and What Has Fallen

The honest inventory of what is known about Rapa Nui is more interesting than any theory about it.

The moai were transported upright using ropes and the rocking gait enabled by their own D-shaped bases. This has been demonstrated experimentally and confirmed by the morphological analysis of 962 statues. The Rapa Nui oral tradition that the statues walked is, in this precise mechanical sense, accurate.

The deforestation of the island was driven primarily by the Polynesian rat, not by human recklessness. Ancient DNA does not support a pre-European population crash. The "ecocide" narrative is substantially weakened, though not all scholars have abandoned it.

The Rapa Nui carried approximately ten percent Andean South American DNA that predates European contact. The earliest settlers brought foodstuffs from both Southeast Asia and South America. The isolation of Easter Island from South American influence, a claim defended for decades, is no longer tenable.

The moai at Rano Raraku are buried to depths that raise questions about the timeline of their creation, questions that have not been resolved by published stratigraphic studies. The oral tradition of pre-Polynesian habitation exists in the ethnographic record but has not been confirmed by archaeology.

The rongorongo script remains undeciphered. It is the only fully developed indigenous writing system in Polynesia, and its existence on a tiny, remote island is itself an anomaly that no theory has adequately explained. The last people who could read it were taken to Peru in chains in 1862 and did not survive the return voyage.

The seven statues of Ahu Akivi still face the sea. Whatever they were looking for, the ocean has kept it.
