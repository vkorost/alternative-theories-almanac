## Chapter 15: The Wrong Face – Mesoamerica: The Olmec, the Maya, and Pre-Columbian Mysteries
The head weighs fifty-two metric tons. It sits in the city of **Jalapa**, Veracruz, in a museum courtyard where the light hits it from the southeast in the morning and school groups file past it without stopping. Until very recently, its listed weight was forty-two tons; the figure was revised upward within the last year, which says something about how casually the Olmec colossal heads have been treated even by the institutions that house them. It is the largest of the seventeen known heads, carved from a single boulder of basalt, a stone so dense that it registers six to seven on the Mohs hardness scale, comparable to granite. The face has a broad nose, heavy lips, slightly forward-set eyes beneath a close-fitting helmet, and an expression that manages to convey both authority and patience, as though the person depicted had been waiting for something and was not particularly surprised that it had not yet arrived.

**Luke Caverns**, a cultural anthropologist who graduated from **South Texas University** and has become one of the more prominent young science communicators in the alternative-history space, stood in front of this head on a drone filming morning in the early months of 2024. He had been to the Olmec heartland before, working under **Dr. Jamie Awe** on the **Belize Valley Archaeological Reconnaissance Project**, a field operation that involved walking through jungle on foot because lidar flights were expensive and bureaucratically tangled. Caverns was twenty-six years old, newly married, newly graduated, and had just finished speaking at the Cosmic Summit conference alongside researchers decades his senior. His specialty was Mesoamerica, and his thesis was simple: the Olmec were the Mesoamerican equivalent of ancient Egypt, a civilization whose economic and cultural dominance was built on the same foundation that built the pharaohs, which is to say, a river.

The **Coatzacoalcos River**, which runs through what is now the Mexican state of Veracruz, has the richest agricultural soil in all of Mesoamerica. Twenty thousand years ago, long before anyone was carving colossal heads, the people in this region were cultivating **maize** on farms that extended from northern Mesoamerica deep into what is now Central America. Of all the maize-growing cultures, the ones along the Coatzacoalcos had the highest yields and the greatest surplus. The surplus generated wealth. The wealth generated power. And the power generated **San Lorenzo**, the oldest known Olmec city, where the first colossal heads were carved and where the culture that would shape every subsequent Mesoamerican civilization first took form.

Caverns described the Olmec as one of the seven fertile crescents of the ancient world, an independently arising agricultural powerhouse that generated the surplus wealth necessary for monumental architecture, centralized authority, and the specialization of labor that produced, among other things, sculptors capable of carving fifty-two-ton portraits from volcanic stone.

The drone footage he captured that morning showed the head from above, the helmet's curve catching the early light, the face staring upward at a sky that looked nothing like the one it was carved under. San Lorenzo is a quiet, unremarkable place now. The colossal head sits like a geological feature, something the earth produced rather than something humans made. It has been sitting there, or in a museum, or under sixty feet of soil, depending on which account you believe, for somewhere between 2,500 and 3,500 years.

This is not a controversial reading of the archaeology. What is controversial, and what has been controversial since the day the first colossal head was excavated, is what the faces on those heads are supposed to look like.


### The Claim That Would Not Die

The argument is older than anyone currently making it. In 1862, a farmer in the **Tres Zapotes** region of Veracruz stumbled across the top of an enormous stone head protruding from the earth. It was excavated, measured, photographed, and immediately interpreted through the lens of the observer's cultural framework. The features were "African." The nose was broad. The lips were full. The person depicted, in the opinion of various nineteenth-century commentators, could not be a Native American. Therefore, Africans had crossed the Atlantic before Columbus, settled in Mesoamerica, and founded the Olmec civilization.

The theory has been advanced in various forms for over 160 years. **Ivan Van Sertima**, a Rutgers University professor of African studies, gave it its most influential academic treatment in his 1976 book ***They Came Before Columbus***, which argued that West African mariners reached the Gulf of Mexico around 1200 BCE and established the Olmec as a colony or cultural offshoot. The book was popular, politically charged, and almost entirely speculative. The physical evidence was the faces on the heads, and the faces on the heads looked, to Van Sertima and his supporters, African.

The problem with this argument is not that it is offensive, though many Mesoamerican scholars consider it precisely that, a form of racist denial of indigenous American achievement. The problem is that the evidence does not support it, and the one peer-reviewed paper that claimed otherwise was retracted.

In 2020, a study was published claiming to have found African genetic signatures in Olmec skeletal remains. The paper appeared in a journal, made the rounds of alternative-history communities, and was cited as the long-awaited confirmation of pre-Columbian African contact with Mesoamerica. It was retracted for failing to meet evidentiary standards. The data did not show what the authors claimed it showed. The analysis was flawed. The conclusion was unsupported.

The actual genetic evidence points in a different direction entirely. **Mitochondrial DNA** extracted from Olmec-era individuals belongs to **haplogroup A**, characteristic of the founding Native American populations that entered the Americas via the **Bering Land Bridge** and coastal routes. Haplogroup A is not African. It is not debated. No African-specific haplogroup L has been found at any Olmec site. No African artifacts, trade goods, writing systems, pottery styles, or skeletal remains have been recovered from any Olmec context. The broad noses and full lips depicted on the colossal heads are consistent with the natural facial variation present in indigenous Mexican populations and with the artistic conventions of Olmec sculpture, which emphasized bulk and mass in facial features as markers of authority.

**Dmitry Belyaev**, a historian and deputy director of the **Knorozov Mesoamerican Center** at the **Russian State University for the Humanities**, addressed this myth directly at the Antropogenez forum. The Olmec were an indigenous American civilization with no African ancestry or contact. Belyaev noted that the pre-Columbian African-contact theory attracted different constituencies in different countries: in the United States, it drew Afrocentrists seeking to expand the narrative of African achievement; in Russia, after Sklyarov's team publicized photographs from their 2007 expedition, it drew proponents of ancient advanced civilizations who folded the Olmec faces into a broader argument about global pre-Flood contact. In both cases, the evidence was identical and identically insufficient. The claim persists not because evidence supports it but because the faces continue to look, to people unfamiliar with the range of indigenous American physiognomy, like something other than what they are.

**The Wrong Face** is not the Olmec face. The wrong face is the one the observer projects onto it.


### The Basalt and the River

Caverns presented two key observations at the 2024 Cosmic Summit that had not received wide attention outside the Mesoamerican archaeological community.

The first concerned depth. The San Lorenzo head, the famous one that appears on every textbook cover, was found buried far deeper than Wikipedia reports. Caverns described a conversation with an elderly Mexican archaeologist at the site, a man in his eighties who had been present during the original excavations. Through Dr. **Mark Barnhart**, who translated, the old man was asked how far down the head had been found. "About six feet," Caverns suggested, quoting the standard published figure. No, the archaeologist corrected him. Sixty feet. Barnhart asked again: sixteen feet? No. Sixty feet. Other archaeologists at the site corroborated the figure. If accurate, this depth would push the burial date of the San Lorenzo head far earlier than conventional estimates allow, though depth alone does not equate to age without stratigraphic dating of the surrounding sediment layers.

The second concerned the altars. The Olmec carved megalithic altars weighing forty to fifty tons from solid basalt blocks, transported them to their final locations, and then carved them in place. The remnants of the excess stone, chipped away after the altars reached their destinations, are buried next to the finished pieces. This means the original blocks were substantially heavier than the finished altars, possibly sixty to seventy tons each. The altars almost uniformly depict a figure emerging from a portal or cave, with a jaguar being above, a motif that Caverns argued represents the origin of society itself. The figures' lips have a uniform depth that matches the lip of the altar's upper edge, suggesting a standardized design template that was applied across multiple sites over centuries.

A third category of Olmec artifact has received almost no public attention: **megalithic column tombs** found at San Lorenzo and nearby sites, consisting of stone columns seven to eight feet tall and weighing two to seven tons each, arranged to form burial chambers. These tombs predate Mesoamerican pyramids by centuries and suggest a megalithic tradition in the Olmec heartland that influenced everything that followed. The columns, like the altar blocks, were transported to their final locations as raw material and finished on site. The remnants of the uncarved stone, excavated from the soil beside the finished tombs, confirm the sequence: move the block, then carve it.

The logistics of this operation deserve closer attention than they typically receive. The basalt used for the colossal heads and many of the altars was quarried in the **Tuxtla Mountains**, roughly eighty kilometers from San Lorenzo. Transporting a fifty-two-ton block of basalt eighty kilometers through tropical lowland, across rivers and wetlands, without wheeled vehicles or draft animals, using only human labor and possibly log rollers or sledges, is a feat that requires not just muscle but organization: food supply for the workers, coordination of the transport route, engineering of river crossings, and a social structure capable of diverting enormous quantities of labor from agriculture to monument-building. The Olmec accomplished this repeatedly, for seventeen known heads and an unknown number of altars, tombs, and other megalithic objects, over a period of roughly a thousand years.

The Olmec timeline runs from roughly 1500 BCE to 400 BCE, though the dating is complicated by the depth issue Caverns described. San Lorenzo was the first major center, followed by **La Venta** and then Tres Zapotes. The civilization's influence radiated outward: the **Long Count calendar**, later adopted and refined by the **Maya**, originated in the Olmec region. The zero, one of the most consequential mathematical inventions in human history, was an Olmec innovation, predating the Indian zero by at least several centuries. The **ballgame**, played across Mesoamerica for two thousand years with profound ritual and political significance, was an Olmec institution. When Caverns described the Olmec as Mesoamerica's Egypt, the parallel was not casual. The Olmec did for Central America what the Nile Valley civilizations did for North Africa: they created the template.


### The Megalithic Substrate

Sklyarov's March 2007 Mexico expedition, which had already traversed 4,238 kilometers by bus through the Olmec zone, Palenque, and the Yucatan Peninsula before the team launched three members in a hot-air balloon over Teotihuacan, produced one of his more striking individual observations in the capital's National Museum.

What Sklyarov found in Mexico confirmed a pattern he had documented in Egypt, Peru, and Bolivia: evidence of two distinct construction traditions at the same sites, one older and more technologically sophisticated, one younger and cruder. In *Древняя Мексика без кривых зеркал* (Ancient Mexico Without Distorted Mirrors), he presented his observations from the Olmec and Maya zones. The situation in ancient history, he wrote in the book's preface, had reached a point where «древнюю историю по сути надо заново переписывать целиком и полностью» (ancient history essentially needs to be rewritten entirely from scratch), because too many facts contradicted the official narrative, and the contradiction was not in minor details but in fundamentals.

One of his most provocative observations concerned a polished obsidian disk found in the **National Museum of Anthropology** in Mexico City, resembling a modern CD in size and shape. Obsidian, volcanic glass rated at 5 to 6 on the Mohs hardness scale, can be chipped and flaked easily to produce sharp blades. But it cannot be sawed into flat planes or polished to mirror finish by simple percussion. A flat, polished obsidian disk requires cutting and polishing technology that the known Mesoamerican toolkit, consisting of stone hammers and obsidian blades, cannot produce. Sklyarov's team visited a modern obsidian workshop near Teotihuacan, where artisans demonstrated that producing such surfaces required high-speed abrasive wheels driven by electric motors. The ancient disk was in the museum. The ancient tool that made it was not.

At sites like **Teshcotingo**, where Hancock ([see Chapter 23](23-the-clovis-wall.md)) had filmed portions of *Ancient Apocalypse*, Caverns documented megalithic bedrock cuts that he argued were pre-Olmec: straight lines, parallel cuts, smooth surfaces carved into granite at a level of precision that bronze-age tools could not achieve. These cuts existed alongside later Aztec and Maya construction, smaller stones, plaster, mortar, the standard toolkit of Mesoamerican masonry. The juxtaposition was visible to the naked eye: one layer precise, geometric, and anonymous; the other ornate, varied, and attributable to known cultures. Caverns defined a megalith, for his purposes, as "anything that you basically couldn't do with bronze," anything too precise, too exact, with right angles and smooth surfaces that do not match the capabilities of the tool kit available to the cultures officially credited with the construction.<sup>[1](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>

This is the same two-layer pattern documented at Ollantaytambo, Puma Punku, and the Valley Temple at Giza ([Chapter 6](06-the-wadi-al-jarf-papyri.md)): precision megalithic construction at the base, cruder work above, and a gulf between them that the conventional story fills with the phrase "the same builders, different phases." In Mesoamerica, as elsewhere, that answer leaves the physical evidence one step ahead of the explanation.


### The Teotihuacan Problem

Before reaching the Maya, the chain of Mesoamerican civilizations passes through Teotihuacan, the largest city in the pre-Columbian Americas and one of the most enigmatic urban centers in the archaeological record. At its peak around 450 CE, Teotihuacan housed an estimated 125,000 people in a meticulously planned grid of apartments, temples, and avenues centered on the **Pyramid of the Sun** and the **Pyramid of the Moon**. The city was built incrementally over several centuries, beginning around 100 BCE, using locally sourced volcanic materials including **tezontle**, a lightweight scoriaceous basalt that is naturally porous and easy to shape.

Mainstream archaeology presents Teotihuacan as a product of sophisticated but entirely human urban planning, with no requirement for outside technological intervention. Ongoing excavations by institutions including **Arizona State University's Teotihuacan Research Laboratory** continue to uncover evidence of craft production, administration, and trade networks that explain the city's power without recourse to mystery. The city's builders left no writing system, or none that has survived, which means that the identities of the rulers, the name of the civilization, and the reasons for the city's eventual abandonment around 550 CE remain unknown. Even the name "Teotihuacan" is Aztec, applied centuries after the city was abandoned. It translates, roughly, as "the place where the gods were born," which is either a factual claim about divine origins or a recognition by a later civilization that the place was too impressive to have been built by mere humans.

Sklyarov ([Chapter 1](01-the-tolerance-problem.md)), who visited Teotihuacan during his 2007 expedition and captured it from a hot-air balloon, noted the extent to which modern restoration had transformed the site. The Pyramid of the Sun had been "restored" in the early twentieth century, and by the 1960s much of the northern complex had been rebuilt to attract tourists. Comparing pre-restoration photographs with the current site revealed differences so stark that Sklyarov warned readers of his Mexico book to remember that «видимое вами может быть совсем не тем, что было в реальности» (what you see may be nothing like what was actually there). The reconstruction, he observed, followed the plan approved by mainstream archaeology, which meant that any evidence of earlier, more sophisticated construction layers could have been inadvertently concealed beneath the plaster and rubble of the "restored" surface.<sup>[2](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>

The alternative community points to Teotihuacan's precise astronomical alignments, the mathematical ratios encoded in its architecture, and the sheer scale of the construction as evidence of knowledge that exceeds what the standard model attributes to Mesoamerican builders. Caverns, who had studied the site both from the air and on the ground, took a more measured position: Teotihuacan is extraordinary, but it is extraordinary in ways that are consistent with what highly organized, mathematically literate human societies can achieve. The question is not whether the builders needed alien help. The question is how a society that produced this level of urbanism could vanish so completely that even its name was forgotten.<sup>[3](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>


### The Calendar and the Cosmos

The Maya, who inherited the Olmec cultural template and built upon it for two thousand years, produced one of the most sophisticated astronomical and mathematical systems in the pre-modern world. Their achievements in this area are not in dispute by any side of the debate.<sup>[4](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>

The Long Count calendar, which the Maya refined from its Olmec origins, tracked time in cycles of 5,125 years. The calendar's base date corresponds to August 11, 3114 BCE in the Gregorian calendar, and its most recent cycle completion occurred on December 21, 2012, an event that generated enormous popular excitement and precisely zero apocalyptic consequences. The 2012 phenomenon, in which millions of people worldwide anticipated the end of the world based on a misunderstanding of Maya calendrical mathematics, was the subject of extensive debunking by Mesoamerican scholars, including Belyaev, who noted with evident weariness that the end of a calendrical cycle no more implies the end of the world than December 31 implies the end of time.<sup>[5](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>

The Maya zero, however, is a genuinely extraordinary achievement. The concept of zero as a placeholder and as a number in its own right was independently invented only three times in human history: by the Babylonians, the Indians, and the Mesoamericans. The Maya version predates the Indian version by at least several centuries, though precise dating is complicated by the fragmentary nature of early Mesoamerican inscriptions. The mathematical system built on this foundation, a vigesimal (base-20) counting system with place value, enabled the Maya to perform calculations of staggering accuracy. Their estimate of the solar year, 365.2420 days, is closer to the modern measured value of 365.2422 days than the Gregorian calendar's 365.2425.<sup>[6](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>

Carlson ([Chapter 4](04-the-cubit-coincidence.md)) discussed Maya astronomical knowledge with Caverns on the Cosmic Summit podcast. Carlson's interest was in the mathematical constants encoded in Maya architecture and calendrical systems, parallels he saw with Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Vedic traditions. Whether these parallels indicate cultural transmission, independent discovery, or a shared inheritance from an earlier common source is exactly the question that the mainstream regards as outside the boundaries of acceptable inquiry, and that the alternative community regards as the most important question of all.<sup>[7](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>

Caverns, who had studied Mesoamerica in both the classroom and the jungle, offered a perspective that straddled the divide. The Maya, he argued, should serve as a model for how ancient civilizations are studied elsewhere. Maya archaeology is, in his assessment, the most open-minded and data-driven branch of the discipline. Archaeologists working in Central America, under researchers like Dr. Awe, let the data point them toward conclusions rather than defending predetermined narratives. This stood in contrast, Caverns suggested, to the more entrenched positions found in Egyptology and European prehistory, where challenging the status quo remained more professionally dangerous.<sup>[8](../ENDNOTES.md#sources-chapter-15)</sup>

His own position was carefully triangulated. He described himself as "kind of a rogue anthropologist," a characterization that undersold his credentials while preserving his freedom to ask questions that a more conventional academic might avoid. He had spoken at the Cosmic Summit alongside Randall Carlson, Ben van Kerkwyk ([Chapter 3](03-the-second-builder.md)), and other figures that the mainstream would classify as pseudo-archaeologists, yet his degree was real, his fieldwork was legitimate, and his approach to evidence was empirical rather than speculative. The tension between these worlds, the academic and the alternative, was visible in his presentations: rigorous data delivered with the energy of a science communicator who knew that his audience included both trained researchers and enthusiasts whose primary education in Mesoamerican history came from Netflix documentaries and Joe Rogan episodes.

The Maya, in Caverns' framing, were not a lost civilization. They were a thoroughly documented one whose documentation kept revealing surprises. The **lidar** surveys of the **Guatemalan** jungle, conducted over the past decade, had uncovered tens of thousands of previously unknown structures beneath the canopy, revealing a civilization far larger and more urbanized than anyone had suspected. Canals, roads, defensive fortifications, and agricultural terraces extended across areas that had been assumed to be uninhabited jungle. The Maya population at its peak may have reached ten million or more, a figure that would make the Maya lowlands one of the most densely populated regions in the pre-modern world.

Caverns had direct experience with the limitations that preceded lidar. Working under Dr. Awe on the Belize Valley Archaeological Reconnaissance Project, his team had surveyed jungle on foot because aerial scanning was expensive, bureaucratically complicated, and not yet standard in the field. The difference between ground-level survey and lidar revelation was, for someone who had done both, not abstract but visceral: features that would have taken months of machete-and-compass work to identify were visible from the air in minutes. The jungle had been hiding a civilization in plain sight. The lidar data did not discover the Maya. It discovered the scale of the Maya, and the scale was staggering.

The Mesoamerican past, as Caverns liked to point out, was not shrinking with new discoveries. It was growing.


### The Crystal Skull and the Acambaro Dinosaurs

Any honest account of Mesoamerican alternative history must reckon with the forgeries, because the forgeries have done more damage to legitimate questions than any amount of mainstream dismissal.

The **crystal skulls**, allegedly ancient Maya artifacts possessed of mystical powers, were promoted for decades as evidence of lost technology. They are modern fabrications. Detailed analysis of the most famous examples, including the one in the British Museum, revealed tool marks consistent with nineteenth-century European lapidary techniques. No crystal skull has ever been found in a documented archaeological context. They are not Maya. They are not ancient. They are frauds.

The **Acambaro figurines**, a collection of thousands of small clay figures depicting humans alongside dinosaurs, were purchased by a German-Mexican collector named **Waldemar Julsrud** from local farmers beginning in the 1940s. Julsrud paid one peso per figurine, a payment structure that virtually guarantees the production of fakes: when you pay per piece, you get as many pieces as the supplier can manufacture. By some counts, the collection grew to over 33,000 pieces, of which approximately 2,600 depicted dinosaurs. The remainder included anthropomorphic figures, zoomorphic figures, and objects of ambiguous form. The figurines are promoted by creationists in the West as evidence that humans and dinosaurs coexisted, and by alternative researchers in Russia and elsewhere as evidence of a lost civilization. Belyaev addressed the Acambaro figurines at the Antropogenez forum, noting that their distribution within alternative-history communities varied by country: in the United States, they attracted creationists who saw proof against evolution; in Russia, after Sklyarov's team photographed them during their 2007 expedition and published the images through the Laboratory of Alternative History, they attracted proponents of ancient advanced civilizations who were less interested in dinosaurs than in the figurines' supposed antiquity. In both cases, the figurines failed standard archaeological authentication. Julsrud himself was not an archaeologist. He was a hardware store owner of German extraction who had settled in Mexico because the cost of living was low.

These forgeries matter because they create guilt by association. A researcher who points to the genuine puzzle of how the Olmec carved and transported fifty-two-ton basalt heads using bronze-age technology is immediately placed in the same category as someone promoting crystal skulls. The mainstream response to the crystal skulls (debunk and dismiss) is applied, often reflexively, to the basalt heads (explain and move on). The result is that questions which deserve careful investigation are treated as already answered, not because the answers are satisfying but because the questions have been contaminated by proximity to fraud.

Sklyarov, who visited Acambaro during his 2007 Mexico expedition and photographed the figurine collection, was cited by **Belyaev** at the Antropogenez forum as one of the popularizers who had given the figurines wider circulation. This is factually accurate and illustrates the double-edged nature of the alternative-research enterprise: the same expedition that produced Sklyarov's observations about megalithic bedrock cuts at Mesoamerican sites also lent visibility to a collection that mainstream scholarship considers fraudulent. The legitimate observations and the dubious ones travel in the same suitcase.


### The Deeper Layers

The Olmec heartland, like Easter Island, like Gobekli Tepe, like the Giza plateau, resists simple narratives. The colossal heads are real, indigenous, and without parallel in any other American civilization. The genetic evidence conclusively places the Olmec within the founding Native American population, with no African or other Old World ancestry. The "wrong face" theory is dead, and its retracted 2020 paper is its tombstone.

The depth at which the San Lorenzo head was found, if the sixty-foot figure reported by the site archaeologist is accurate, raises questions about the conventional timeline that have not been addressed in the published literature. The megalithic bedrock cuts at multiple Mexican sites, documented by both Caverns and Sklyarov, present the same pattern observed in Egypt and Peru: precision stonework underlying later, cruder construction. Whether this pattern has a single explanation or many is unknown.

The Olmec invented the zero, the Long Count calendar, the ballgame, the colossal head tradition, and the template for every subsequent Mesoamerican civilization. They did this along a river, in a region of extraordinary agricultural fertility, with tools that, according to the archaeological record, consisted of stone, bone, and obsidian. What they achieved with those tools is a fact. Whether those tools were the only ones they possessed is a question.

The Olmec zero, the Long Count calendar, the colossal head tradition, the megalithic altars, the column tombs, the ballgame: these are the cultural DNA of Mesoamerica. Every civilization that followed, the Maya, the Zapotec, the Mixtec, the Toltec, the Aztec, built on the Olmec template. The relationship is not metaphorical. It is traceable, documented, and accepted by all sides of the archaeological debate. What the Olmec achieved, along a river, in a tropical lowland, with tools made of stone, bone, and obsidian, is the foundation of two thousand years of cultural development across an entire continent. The colossal heads alone would be sufficient to ensure their place in the archaeological record. The zero would ensure their place in the history of mathematics. Together they describe a civilization whose intellectual and material achievements are still not fully accounted for by any model, mainstream or alternative.

The sixty-foot depth at which the San Lorenzo head was reportedly found, if confirmed by published stratigraphic analysis, would push the Olmec timeline deeper into the past than current models allow. That confirmation has not appeared. The megalithic bedrock cuts documented at sites across Mexico exhibit a precision that bronze-age tools would struggle to reproduce, but "struggle" is not "impossible," and the absence of a satisfactory explanation is not the same as the presence of a better one. The retracted 2020 DNA paper claiming African ancestry in Olmec individuals is dead, and the haplogroup A evidence confirming indigenous American origin is definitive. The alternative-origin theory survives in popular culture but not in the data.

The fifty-two-ton head in Jalapa does not answer the remaining questions. It sits in its courtyard, wearing its helmet, its broad face turned toward the morning light, and it will go on sitting there after the arguments about its makers have moved on to the next theory and the one after that. The wrong face turned out to be the right one all along. The people who carved it did not need to be anyone other than who they were. They just needed to be better understood.
